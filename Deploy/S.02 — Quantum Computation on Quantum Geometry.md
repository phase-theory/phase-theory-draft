Abstract

Quantum computation is conventionally formulated on a fixed classical geometric background. Qubits evolve according to unitary dynamics within Hilbert spaces whose geometric structure is assumed to be continuous, differentiable, and fundamentally classical. Meanwhile, numerous approaches to quantum gravity—including Generalized Uncertainty Principle (GUP) theories, non-commutative geometry, deformation quantization, loop quantum gravity, causal structures, and emergent spacetime models—suggest that geometry itself may possess quantum degrees of freedom.

This paper develops a comprehensive theory of Quantum Computation on Quantum Geometry (QCQG), in which information processing occurs within geometries exhibiting minimum length scales, non-commuting coordinates, quantized areas, geometric fluctuations, and topology-changing processes.

We demonstrate that quantum geometry modifies:

* Qubit localization
* Quantum gate implementation
* Circuit complexity
* Entanglement structure
* Error correction thresholds
* Information density limits
* Quantum communication capacity
* Computational universality

A new computational paradigm emerges in which geometry becomes an active information-processing resource rather than a passive background.

Conclusions

Quantum computation has historically assumed a passive classical geometric background. However, emerging theories of quantum geometry suggest that spacetime itself may possess discrete, non-commutative, and information-bearing degrees of freedom. Once geometry is promoted from background to dynamical participant, entirely new computational phenomena become possible.

The framework of Quantum Computation on Quantum Geometry introduces geometric qubits, geometric error correction, holographic information processing, non-commutative algorithms, geometric channel capacities, and curvature-assisted computation. It predicts new limits on information density, new sources of decoherence, and potentially new forms of computational advantage unavailable to conventional quantum processors.

The long-term implication is profound: computation may ultimately be understood as the evolution of quantum geometry itself. In that view, matter-based quantum computers represent only a special case of a broader class of geometric information-processing systems whose full capabilities remain largely unexplored.
