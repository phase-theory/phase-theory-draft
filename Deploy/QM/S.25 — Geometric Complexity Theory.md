Abstract

Computational complexity theory studies the resources required to solve problems, including time, memory, communication, and circuit depth. Traditional complexity theory describes computation through discrete models such as Turing machines, Boolean circuits, and quantum circuits. While extraordinarily powerful, these approaches treat computation primarily as symbolic transformation.

Geometric Complexity Theory (GCT) introduces a different viewpoint: computational difficulty is a geometric property of transformation spaces. Problems become points or regions within high-dimensional computational manifolds. Algorithms correspond to trajectories through these spaces. Complexity becomes related to geometric distance, curvature, topology, symmetry, and the structure of admissible transformations.

This paper develops the framework of Geometric Complexity Theory (GCT-G), extending the Structural Sector into computational complexity. We define computational manifolds, algorithmic geodesics, complexity metrics, circuit geometry, complexity curvature, transformation barriers, geometric universality classes, quantum complexity landscapes, and geometric approaches to hardness and optimization.

The central thesis is:

Computational complexity is the geometry of possible transformations.
