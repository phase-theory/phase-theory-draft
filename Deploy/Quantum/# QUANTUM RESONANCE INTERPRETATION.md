Abstract

Quantum mechanics provides an extraordinarily successful predictive framework while leaving open a fundamental question: what does the quantum state describe?

The Everett interpretation answers this question by treating the universal wavefunction as physically complete and interpreting measurement as branching into multiple effectively autonomous worlds. The present work develops a different possibility.

We introduce the Quantum Resonance Interpretation (QRI), a single-world interpretation in which the quantum state does not represent a collection of simultaneously realized classical alternatives. Instead, it represents a structured field of relational possibilities whose components possess amplitudes, phases, and compatibility relations. Physical events occur when interactions establish sufficiently stable resonant relations between a system and its environment. A measurement is therefore not the creation of parallel branches, nor necessarily a mysterious intervention by an external observer. It is a resonant actualization event in which one relationally compatible outcome becomes physically instantiated while alternative possibilities cease to possess actuality in that interaction history.

The central ontological distinction is

[
\boxed{
\text{possibility}\neq\text{actuality}.
}
]

The quantum state describes structured possibility. The realized history describes actuality.

QRI retains unitary quantum evolution as the fundamental dynamical law between actualization events while introducing a relational criterion for the transition from coherent possibility to classical actuality. The interpretation is explicitly non-Everettian: there is one realized history, no ontologically real branching universe, and no requirement that every term in a superposition correspond to an equally real macroscopic world.

The framework develops a formal ontology of quantum possibility, a resonance functional, a compatibility operator, a relational actualization principle, a generalized Born rule, decoherence as resonance stabilization rather than merely environmental tracing, and a theory of measurement in which apparatuses, observers, and environments are physical participants in the same process.

QRI is presented as a foundational research programme rather than an experimentally established replacement for standard quantum mechanics. Its central empirical requirement is that it reproduce the successful predictions of quantum theory in regimes where no additional dynamics are introduced. Where QRI adds dynamical structure beyond standard quantum mechanics, those additions become experimentally testable.

Part I — The Foundational Problem

1. The Quantum Ontology Problem

Standard quantum mechanics provides a mathematical state

[
|\psi\rangle
]

and a dynamical law

[
i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}|\psi\rangle

\hat H|\psi\rangle.
]

The formalism predicts experimental probabilities with extraordinary accuracy.

Yet the interpretation of the state remains unsettled.

If

[
|\psi\rangle

\alpha |0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle,
]

what is the ontological status of ( |0\rangle ) and ( |1\rangle )?

Three broad possibilities arise.

1. Both are physically real.
2. One is physically real and the other merely represents unrealized possibility.
3. The distinction between possibility and actuality must itself be reconstructed from the quantum dynamics.

Everettian interpretations adopt a version of the first position. The wavefunction is taken as physically complete, and measurement produces effectively autonomous branches.

QRI adopts the third.

The fundamental object is neither a classical particle possessing an unknown value nor a universe continuously splitting into mutually realized branches.

Instead:

[
\boxed{
\text{Quantum reality consists of structured possibilities evolving toward relational actualities.}
}
]

Part II — The Central Postulates

Actualization is therefore a physical relation rather than an act of consciousness.

At any completed macroscopic event, exactly one outcome becomes actual in the realized history:

[
\boxed{
