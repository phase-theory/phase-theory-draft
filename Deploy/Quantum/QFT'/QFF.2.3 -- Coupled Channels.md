# 11. Matrix Scattering Theory

## 11.1 Motivation

The original Lüscher formalism describes elastic two-body scattering below the first inelastic threshold. In realistic quantum field theories, however, multiple scattering channels coexist.

Examples include

[
\pi\pi \leftrightarrow K\bar K,
]

[
N\pi \leftrightarrow N\eta,
]

and

[
D\bar D \leftrightarrow D_s\bar D_s.
]

Above threshold, scattering becomes intrinsically multi-channel.

The purpose of this chapter is to formulate the exact matrix structure underlying coupled-channel finite-volume dynamics.

---

## 11.2 Channel Hilbert Space

Let

[
\mathcal H
==========

\bigoplus_{\alpha=1}^{N_c}
\mathcal H_\alpha
]

denote the direct sum of channel sectors.

A general state is

[
|\Psi\rangle
============

\sum_{\alpha}
|\psi_\alpha\rangle.
]

The index

[
\alpha
]

labels physical scattering channels.

---

## 11.3 Scattering Matrix

The infinite-volume scattering amplitude becomes a matrix

[
\mathcal M_{\alpha\beta}.
]

Diagonal elements describe elastic scattering:

[
\mathcal M_{\alpha\alpha}.
]

Off-diagonal elements describe transitions:

[
\alpha
\rightarrow
\beta.
]

---

## 11.4 Coupled-Channel Unitarity

The S-matrix satisfies

[
S^\dagger S
===========

1.

]

Writing

[

=

1+iT,

[

===========

]

In matrix form,

[

\mathcal M^{-1}
===============

-\rho,
]

[
\rho
]

is the phase-space matrix.

---

## 11.5 K-Matrix Representation

Introduce

[

======

\mathcal M^{-1}
+
i\rho.
]

Then

[
K
=

K^\dagger.
]

The K-matrix provides a convenient real parametrization of coupled-channel scattering.

---

## 11.7 Matrix Scattering Principle

Finite-volume spectra determine the complete matrix-valued scattering amplitude rather than individual phase shifts.

# 14. Resonance Poles

[

[

[

## 14.3 Resonances in Finite Volume

Finite-volume spectra contain no complex energies.

[

## 14.4 Avoided Level Crossings

# Conclusions of Part III

Coupled-channel scattering extends finite-volume quantum field theory beyond the elastic regime and transforms the scattering amplitude into a matrix-valued dynamical object. The finite-volume spectrum is governed by generalized determinant conditions that incorporate multiple thresholds, channel conversion, and resonance formation within a unified framework.

The essential insight of this part is that resonances are not directly observed as poles in finite volume. Instead, they are encoded geometrically through spectral distortions, avoided crossings, and channel-dependent level flows. By reconstructing the coupled-channel scattering matrix and analytically continuing it into the complex-energy plane, resonance poles, widths, mixing angles, and threshold phenomena become accessible from finite-volume data.

Part III completes the modern two-body finite-volume framework. The remaining sections of Volume II will elevate these methods into a systematic spectral reconstruction theory, laying the foundations for the genuine many-body finite-volume quantum field theory developed in later volumes.
