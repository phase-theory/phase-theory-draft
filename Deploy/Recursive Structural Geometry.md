# Recursive Structural Geometry  
## A Point-Free Foundation via Self-Generating Morphisms

**Preprint**  
July 31, 2026  

---

## Abstract

This paper develops a foundational framework for **Recursive Structural Geometry** (RSG), a point-free geometric theory in which the primitive ontological layer consists not of points, but of **self-generating morphisms**. Classical geometry, whether Euclidean, differential, or algebraic, ordinarily begins with a space populated by points and subsequently introduces maps, curves, functions, or fields on that space. RSG reverses this order. The primary data are typed transformations, refinement rules, and recursive substitution laws; points, coordinates, metrics, and tensors arise only as derived, secondary structures.

The formal apparatus is built from a small category whose objects are interpreted as **structural ports** rather than points, together with a refinement functor into a path category. A geometry is then a coalgebraic or guarded-recursive fixed point of a morphism-substitution system. The resulting point-free locale of regions may be spatial or non-spatial; when spatial, its points appear as infinite coherent refinement paths. Metric structure is obtained from recursive length and diameter functionals, and differential-tensorial structure is recovered through functorial transport laws. Curvature is interpreted as the failure of recursive transport to commute, and satisfies recursive scaling laws analogous to renormalization-group flow.

The paper provides axioms, categorical semantics, existence theorems, formal derivations of emergent metric and curvature tensors, and several worked examples: the dyadic interval, the Sierpiński gasket, non-spatial recursive locales, and recursive gauge geometries. The central thesis is that geometry should be understood not as a set of points equipped with structure, but as the invariant content of a self-generating system of morphisms.

## 1. Introduction

The classical conception of geometry is point-centric. One begins with a set \(X\), then equips it with a topology, a smooth atlas, a metric, a measure, or a sheaf of functions. Maps are then secondary: they are relations between pre-existing points. Even in category-theoretic treatments, where morphisms are conceptually primary, the ambient categories are often still understood as categories of spaces, i.e. as collections of point-bearing objects.

This point-centric bias becomes problematic in several contexts:

1. **Fractal and self-similar geometry**, where the natural description is by recursive substitution rather than by a prior point set.
2. **Constructive and computational geometry**, where objects are generated by algorithms and not given extensionally.
3. **Quantum and causal geometry**, where events, processes, or transitions are more fundamental than static points.
4. **Point-free topology and locale theory**, where spaces may fail to have enough points, yet possess robust geometric structure.
5. **Renormalization and multiscale geometry**, where the essential structure is the relation between scales, not a fixed underlying manifold.

The purpose of this paper is to formulate a geometry in which **morphisms generate themselves recursively**, and where points are recovered only as stable observational residues of infinite morphic processes.

The central principle is:

> **A geometry is the invariant structure of a recursive system of morphisms.**

In RSG, a “space” is not a set of points. It is a recursive morphism system, equipped with refinement laws, coherence conditions, and optionally metric or tensorial transport data. Points are not assumed. They are derived, when they exist at all, as maximal coherent chains of nested morphisms.

This leads to a reversal of the usual order of explanation:

\[
\text{points} \longrightarrow \text{maps}
\]

is replaced by

\[
\text{recursive morphisms} \longrightarrow \text{derived points}.
\]

The resulting theory is point-free, coalgebraic, and intrinsically multiscale.

## 2. Conceptual Axioms

The framework is governed by six axioms.

### Axiom 1: Morphism Primacy

The primitive entities are morphisms. Objects are not points; they are **ports**, **interfaces**, or **boundary types** for morphisms.

A morphism

\[
f : a \to b
\]

is not a function between point sets. It is a structural transformation from port \(a\) to port \(b\).

### Axiom 2: Recursive Generation

Morphisms are generated by recursive substitution. A morphism \(f\) may be replaced by a finite or infinite composite of subordinate morphisms:

\[
f \cong f_n \circ \cdots \circ f_1.
\]

This replacement is not merely an accidental decomposition. It is part of the geometric structure.

### Axiom 3: Boundary Coherence

Recursive refinement preserves boundaries. If

\[
f : a \to b,
\]

then every refinement of \(f\) is a path from \(a\) to \(b\). The recursive generation of morphisms does not alter external typing.

### Axiom 4: Scale Contraction

Geometric recursion is multiscale. Subordinate morphisms are smaller, finer, or more refined than their parents. This is formalized by a scale or diameter functional \(\Delta\) satisfying inequalities of the form

\[
\Delta(f_i) \leq r_i \Delta(f),
\qquad 0 < r_i < 1.
\]

Contraction ensures that infinite refinement produces well-defined limiting behavior.

### Axiom 5: Point-Free Spatiality

The geometry is represented by a frame or locale of regions generated by morphisms. Points, if they exist, are completely prime filters of this frame. Spatiality is a theorem, not an axiom.

### Axiom 6: Functorial Transport

Differential and tensorial structure arise from functorial transport along morphisms. A recursive geometry may carry a representation

\[
T : \mathcal{C} \to \mathbf{Vect}
\]

or

\[
T : \mathcal{C} \to \mathbf{GL}(V),
\]

assigning linear transports to morphisms. Curvature is the obstruction to recursive transport commutativity.

## 3. Categorical Foundations

### 3.1 Ports, arrows, and the rejection of primitive points

Let \(\mathcal{C}\) be a small category. Its objects are not interpreted as points. They are interpreted as **ports**. A port is a type of boundary condition for morphisms.

A morphism

\[
f : a \to b
\]

is a directed structural transformation. The category \(\mathcal{C}\) need not possess a terminal object, nor enough global elements

\[
1 \to x.
\]

Indeed, in many recursive geometries, global elements are absent or non-separating.

A point, when it exists, will be a derived construct: an infinite coherent path of refinements, or equivalently a frame homomorphism

\[
\Omega(\mathcal{C}) \to \mathbf{2}.
\]

Thus the category \(\mathcal{C}\) is not a category of spaces. It is a category of structural interfaces and transformations.

---

### 3.2 The path category

Given a small category \(\mathcal{C}\), define its **path category** \(\operatorname{Path}(\mathcal{C})\) as follows.

- Objects of \(\operatorname{Path}(\mathcal{C})\) are the objects of \(\mathcal{C}\).
- A morphism \(a \to b\) in \(\operatorname{Path}(\mathcal{C})\) is a finite composable sequence

\[
(f_n,\dots,f_1),
\]

where

\[
f_i : x_{i-1} \to x_i,
\qquad
x_0 = a,
\qquad
x_n = b.
\]

- Composition is concatenation of paths.
- The identity path at \(a\) is the empty path.

There is a canonical evaluation functor

\[
E : \operatorname{Path}(\mathcal{C}) \to \mathcal{C}
\]

defined by

\[
E(f_n,\dots,f_1) = f_n \circ \cdots \circ f_1.
\]

On objects,

\[
E(a)=a.
\]

The functor \(E\) forgets the internal path structure and retains only the composite morphism.

---

### 3.3 Refinement systems

A **refinement system** on \(\mathcal{C}\) is a functor

\[
R : \mathcal{C} \to \operatorname{Path}(\mathcal{C})
\]

such that \(R\) is the identity on objects, together with a natural isomorphism

\[
\varepsilon : E R \Rightarrow \operatorname{Id}_{\mathcal{C}}.
\]

In strict form, one may require

\[
E R = \operatorname{Id}_{\mathcal{C}}.
\]

For a morphism \(f : a \to b\), the refinement \(R(f)\) is a finite path

\[
R(f) = (f_n,\dots,f_1),
\]

with

\[
f_i : x_{i-1} \to x_i,
\qquad
x_0=a,
\qquad
x_n=b,
\]

and the coherence isomorphism is

\[
\varepsilon_f : f_n \circ \cdots \circ f_1 \xrightarrow{\sim} f.
\]

In the strict case,

\[
f = f_n \circ \cdots \circ f_1.
\]

Thus \(f\) is generated by its refinement.

Because \(R\) is a functor, refinement respects composition:

\[
R(g \circ f) = R(g) \ast R(f),
\]

where \(\ast\) denotes concatenation of paths. Similarly,

\[
R(\operatorname{id}_a) = \operatorname{id}_a.
\]

This expresses the principle that recursive generation is compatible with categorical structure.

---

### 3.4 Scale and contraction

A refinement system becomes geometric once equipped with scale data.

A **scale function** is a map

\[
\Delta : \operatorname{Arr}(\mathcal{C}) \to [0,\infty)
\]

such that

\[
\Delta(\operatorname{id}_a)=0.
\]

For each non-identity morphism \(f\), suppose

\[
R(f) = (f_n,\dots,f_1).
\]

We require that there exist constants \(r_i(f) \in (0,1)\) such that

\[
\Delta(f_i) \leq r_i(f) \Delta(f).
\]

If there is a uniform constant \(r<1\) such that

\[
\Delta(f_i) \leq r \Delta(f)
\]

for all nontrivial refinements, the system is called **uniformly contractive**.

The functional \(\Delta\) is interpreted as a diameter or scale, not necessarily as length. Length will be introduced separately.

---

### 3.5 Recursive structural pregeometry

A **recursive structural pregeometry** is a tuple

\[
\mathscr{G} = (\mathcal{C}, R, \varepsilon, \Delta)
\]

where:

1. \(\mathcal{C}\) is a small category whose objects are ports;
2. \(R : \mathcal{C} \to \operatorname{Path}(\mathcal{C})\) is a refinement functor;
3. \(\varepsilon : E R \Rightarrow \operatorname{Id}_{\mathcal{C}}\) is a boundary-coherence natural isomorphism;
4. \(\Delta\) is a contractive scale functional.

If the refinement functor is finitely branching and uniformly contractive, the pregeometry is called **regular**.

## 4. Guarded Recursive Morphism Specifications

The preceding definition is intrinsic. For construction, it is useful to give an algebraic specification.

### 4.1 Typed recursive equations

Fix a set \(T\) of types, interpreted as ports. Let \(G\) be a set of generating morphisms

\[
g : a \to b,
\qquad a,b \in T.
\]

Let \(X\) be a set of recursive morphism variables

\[
x : a_x \to b_x.
\]

A recursive specification consists of equations

\[
x = p_x,
\]

where \(p_x\) is a finite path from \(a_x\) to \(b_x\) whose edges are labeled by generators or variables.

For example,

\[
x = h_3 \circ y \circ h_2 \circ z \circ h_1.
\]

Each occurrence of a variable may carry a scale weight \(\lambda > 0\). The weighted occurrence indicates that the recursive call is made at a smaller scale.

---

### 4.2 Dependency matrix and guardedness

Define the **dependency matrix** \(A = (A_{xy})\) by

\[
A_{xy}
=
\sum_{\text{occurrences of } y \text{ in } p_x}
\lambda_{xy}.
\]

The specification is **guarded** if the spectral radius satisfies

\[
\rho(A) < 1.
\]

This condition ensures that recursive unfolding is contractive.

---

### 4.3 Existence theorem for recursive morphism geometries

**Theorem 4.1.**  
Let \(\Sigma\) be a finite typed recursive morphism specification with weighted dependency matrix \(A\). If

\[
\rho(A) < 1,
\]

then there exists, up to equivalence, a unique regular recursive structural geometry \(\mathcal{C}_\Sigma\) generated by \(\Sigma\).

#### Proof sketch.

For each type pair \((a,b)\), let \(\mathcal{T}_{ab}\) be the set of rooted, ordered, edge-labeled refinement trees with boundary \(a \to b\). Define truncation functors

\[
\pi_n : \mathcal{T}_{ab} \to \mathcal{T}_{ab}^{(n)}
\]

to depth \(n\). Define an ultrametric

\[
d(t,s) = 2^{-N},
\]

where \(N\) is the largest depth to which \(t\) and \(s\) agree.

Because the specification is weighted, one uses a weighted metric. Since \(\rho(A)<1\), there exists a positive vector \(w=(w_x)\) such that

\[
A w < w.
\]

Define

\[
D((t_x)_x,(s_x)_x)
=
\max_x w_x d(t_x,s_x).
\]

The unfolding operator \(\Phi\) replaces each variable by its right-hand side. Every recursive occurrence lies beneath at least one scale weight, so \(\Phi\) is a contraction:

\[
D(\Phi(t),\Phi(s)) \leq \rho(A) D(t,s).
\]

By the Banach fixed-point theorem, \(\Phi\) has a unique fixed point in the completion. This fixed point is the family of infinite refinement trees solving the recursive equations.

Composition is defined by grafting trees along compatible ports. Since grafting is continuous in the tree metric, the resulting structure is a category. The refinement functor \(R\) sends a tree to its immediate root decomposition. The contraction condition gives the scale functional \(\Delta\). Hence a regular RSG is obtained. \(\square\)

## 5. Point-Free Regions and Derived Points

### 5.1 The locale of recursive regions

Given a recursive structural geometry \(\mathscr{G}\), define a frame \(\Omega(\mathscr{G})\) generated by symbols

\[
U_f
\]

for each morphism \(f\), subject to refinement relations.

If

\[
R(f) = (f_n,\dots,f_1),
\]

then the region represented by \(f\) is covered by the regions represented by its refinements:

\[
U_f = \bigvee_{i=1}^n U_{f_i}.
\]

If \(f\) and \(g\) overlap, their intersection is expressed through common refinements. Formally, one may define \(\Omega(\mathscr{G})\) as the frame of closed sieves for the Grothendieck topology generated by the refinement families.

Thus the geometry is first a locale, not a point set.

---

### 5.2 Points as filters

A **point** of \(\mathscr{G}\) is a frame homomorphism

\[
p : \Omega(\mathscr{G}) \to \mathbf{2}.
\]

Equivalently, a point is a completely prime filter of regions.

In concrete contractive systems, points can be described as infinite refinement chains.

A point is a sequence

\[
f_0, f_1, f_2, \dots
\]

such that:

1. \(f_{n+1}\) occurs in the refinement of \(f_n\);
2. the boundaries are compatible;
3. the diameters tend to zero:

\[
\lim_{n\to\infty} \Delta(f_n)=0.
\]

Thus a point is not primitive. It is an infinite coherent trajectory through the recursive morphism system.

---

### 5.3 Spatiality

The locale \(\Omega(\mathscr{G})\) may or may not be spatial.

Define the spatial reflection

\[
\operatorname{Pt}(\mathscr{G})
=
\operatorname{Frm}(\Omega(\mathscr{G}),\mathbf{2}).
\]

There is a canonical map

\[
\eta : \Omega(\mathscr{G}) \to \mathcal{O}(\operatorname{Pt}(\mathscr{G}))
\]

given by

\[
U \mapsto \{p : p(U)=1\}.
\]

The geometry is spatial if \(\eta\) is an isomorphism.

In classical point-set geometry, spatiality is assumed. In RSG, spatiality is a derived property. Some recursive geometries are spatial; others are genuinely non-spatial.

---

### 5.4 Non-spatial recursive geometries

Because the frame \(\Omega(\mathscr{G})\) is presented by generators and refinement relations, one can encode arbitrary finitely presented locales. In particular, one may encode known pointless locales.

Thus RSG is not merely a reformulation of point-set geometry. It properly extends it.

A recursive geometry may have no points at all while still possessing a nontrivial lattice of regions, recursive refinements, and transport laws.

This is one of the principal advantages of the morphism-first approach.

## 6. Emergent Metric Structure

### 6.1 Recursive diameter

Let \(\Delta\) be a diameter functional. For a refinement

\[
R(f) = (f_n,\dots,f_1),
\]

suppose

\[
\Delta(f_i) \leq r_i \Delta(f),
\qquad 0<r_i<1.
\]

For an infinite chain

\[
f_0 \succ f_1 \succ f_2 \succ \cdots,
\]

we obtain

\[
\Delta(f_n)
\leq
\left(\prod_{k=1}^n r_k\right)
\Delta(f_0).
\]

If \(r_k \leq r <1\), then

\[
\Delta(f_n) \leq r^n \Delta(f_0) \to 0.
\]

Hence nested refinement chains define pointlike limits without assuming points.

---

### 6.2 Address metric

Suppose the recursive geometry is finitely branching and contractive. Let \(p,q\) be two points represented by refinement chains

\[
p = (f_0,f_1,f_2,\dots),
\]

\[
q = (g_0,g_1,g_2,\dots).
\]

Define their separation depth \(N(p,q)\) as the largest integer \(n\) for which the chains remain compatible.

A natural ultrametric is

\[
d(p,q) = \Delta(f_{N(p,q)}).
\]

In self-similar cases with uniform ratio \(r\), this becomes

\[
d(p,q) = C r^{N(p,q)}.
\]

This is the standard address metric on symbolic spaces, but here it is derived from recursive morphisms rather than imposed on a pre-existing point set.

---

### 6.3 Recursive length

Diameter is not the same as length. A length functional

\[
L : \operatorname{Arr}(\mathcal{C}) \to [0,\infty]
\]

should satisfy:

\[
L(\operatorname{id}_a)=0,
\]

and for composable morphisms,

\[
L(g \circ f) = L(f) + L(g).
\]

If

\[
R(f) = (f_n,\dots,f_1),
\]

then recursive length additivity gives

\[
L(f) = \sum_{i=1}^n L(f_i).
\]

If each \(f_i\) is a scaled copy of some generator or variable \(x_i\), with scale factor \(\lambda_i\), then

\[
L(f_i) = \lambda_i L(x_i).
\]

Thus the recursive length equations become a linear system.

Let \(\ell\) be the vector of unknown lengths. Then

\[
\ell = A \ell + b,
\]

where \(A\) is the weighted dependency matrix and \(b\) encodes contributions from non-recursive generators.

If

\[
\rho(A)<1,
\]

then \(I-A\) is invertible and

\[
\ell = (I-A)^{-1} b.
\]

This gives a unique recursive length assignment.

If \(\rho(A)=1\), length may still be determined after normalization. For example, the unit interval satisfies

\[
L(I) = \frac{1}{2}L(I)+\frac{1}{2}L(I),
\]

which is tautological until one imposes

\[
L(I)=1.
\]

---

### 6.4 Distance from length

Given a length functional, one may define a path metric between derived points.

Let \(p,q \in \operatorname{Pt}(\mathscr{G})\). Define

\[
d_L(p,q)
=
\inf_{\gamma : p \leadsto q}
L(\gamma),
\]

where \(\gamma\) ranges over finite or limiting composable morphism paths connecting \(p\) to \(q\).

Under suitable coherence assumptions, \(d_L\) satisfies:

1. \(d_L(p,p)=0\);
2. symmetry, if the geometry contains inverses or reversible paths;
3. triangle inequality, by concatenation of paths.

In non-reversible directed geometries, \(d_L\) is a quasimetric.

Thus metric geometry emerges from recursive morphism length.

## 7. Recursive Tensorial Structure

The preceding constructions are topological and metric. We now introduce differential-tensorial structure.

### 7.1 Recursive vector transport

A **recursive vector transport** on \(\mathscr{G}\) consists of:

1. a vector space \(V_a\) for each port \(a\);
2. for each morphism \(f:a\to b\), a linear map

\[
T_f : V_a \to V_b;
\]

3. compatibility with composition:

\[
T_{g\circ f} = T_g T_f;
\]

4. compatibility with refinement:

if

\[
R(f) = (f_n,\dots,f_1),
\]

then

\[
T_f = T_{f_n}\cdots T_{f_1}.
\]

Thus transport is functorial and recursive.

In local coordinates, write

\[
(T_f)^a{}_b
\]

for the matrix of \(T_f\).

---

### 7.2 Infinitesimal linearization

Suppose a morphism \(f\) is infinitesimally close to an identity transport. Write

\[
T_f = I + A_f + O(A_f^2).
\]

If \(f\) corresponds to an infinitesimal displacement \(dx^c\), write

\[
(T_f)^a{}_b
=
\delta^a_b
+
\Gamma^a{}_{cb} dx^c
+
O(dx^2).
\]

The coefficients

\[
\Gamma^a{}_{cb}
\]

are the recursive connection coefficients.

They are not introduced by choosing a manifold atlas. They are extracted from the first-order deviation of recursive transport from identity.

---

### 7.3 Holonomy and curvature

Let \(u\) and \(v\) be two infinitesimal morphisms generating a recursive 2-cell. The holonomy around the infinitesimal loop is

\[
H_{u,v}
=
T_v^{-1} T_u^{-1} T_v T_u.
\]

Write

\[
T_u = I + \Gamma_c dx^c + O(dx^2),
\]

\[
T_v = I + \Gamma_d dx^d + O(dx^2).
\]

Expanding to second order gives

\[
H_{u,v}
=
I
+
\left(
\partial_c \Gamma_d
-
\partial_d \Gamma_c
+
[\Gamma_c,\Gamma_d]
\right)
dx^c dx^d
+
O(dx^3).
\]

In abstract index notation,

\[
(H_{u,v})^a{}_b
=
\delta^a_b
+
R^a{}_{bcd}
dx^c dx^d
+
O(dx^3),
\]

where

\[
R^a{}_{bcd}
=
\partial_c \Gamma^a{}_{db}
-
\partial_d \Gamma^a{}_{cb}
+
\Gamma^a{}_{ce}\Gamma^e{}_{db}
-
\Gamma^a{}_{de}\Gamma^e{}_{cb}.
\]

Thus curvature is the second-order recursive holonomy.

The usual Riemann curvature tensor appears not as a primitive field on a manifold, but as the local linearization of recursive transport failure.

---

### 7.4 Recursive curvature scaling

Let \(S\) be a refinement operation that scales lengths by \(\lambda\). Then areas scale by \(\lambda^2\).

Suppose the holonomy deviation scales by a factor \(\tau\):

\[
H_{S\sigma} - I
=
\tau \, P (H_\sigma - I) P^{-1},
\]

where \(P\) is a change of local frame.

Let \(A^{cd}\) be the oriented area bivector of a 2-cell \(\sigma\). Since

\[
H_\sigma
=
I
+
R^a{}_{bcd} A^{cd}
+
O(A^2),
\]

and

\[
A^{cd}_{S\sigma}
=
\lambda^2 A^{cd}_\sigma,
\]

we obtain

\[
R'^a{}_{bcd} \lambda^2 A^{cd}
=
\tau R^a{}_{bcd} A^{cd}.
\]

Therefore

\[
R'^a{}_{bcd}
=
\tau \lambda^{-2} R^a{}_{bcd}.
\]

If the recursive transport is self-similar in the natural gauge, then \(\tau=\lambda^2\), and curvature is invariant:

\[
R' = R.
\]

If \(\tau<\lambda^2\), curvature is irrelevant under refinement. If \(\tau>\lambda^2\), curvature is relevant.

This gives a geometric renormalization interpretation of curvature.

---

### 7.5 Recursive Bianchi identity

Consider a recursive 3-cell whose boundary consists of six faces. Functoriality of transport implies that the ordered product of face holonomies around the boundary is identity.

Expanding to third order yields the differential Bianchi identity. In component form,

\[
\nabla_{[e} R^a{}_{|b|cd]} = 0.
\]

Equivalently,

\[
d_\nabla R = 0,
\]

where \(R\) is the curvature 2-form and \(\nabla\) is the recursive connection.

Thus the classical Bianchi identity is a consequence of coherence among recursive transports around 3-dimensional refinement cells.

## 8. Examples

### 8.1 The dyadic interval

Let there be one port \(\ast\). Introduce two generating endomorphisms

\[
s_0, s_1 : \ast \to \ast.
\]

Interpret them as dyadic contractions with scale \(1/2\).

For each finite binary word

\[
w = i_1 i_2 \cdots i_n,
\qquad i_k \in \{0,1\},
\]

define

\[
s_w = s_{i_1} \circ s_{i_2} \circ \cdots \circ s_{i_n}.
\]

The refinement rule is

\[
U_w = U_{w0} \vee U_{w1}.
\]

A point is an infinite binary sequence

\[
\alpha = i_1 i_2 i_3 \cdots.
\]

The address metric is

\[
d(\alpha,\beta)
=
2^{-N(\alpha,\beta)},
\]

where \(N(\alpha,\beta)\) is the length of the longest common prefix.

The usual interval \([0,1]\) is obtained after identifying the two dyadic addresses

\[
i_1 \cdots i_n 0 1111\cdots
\sim
i_1 \cdots i_n 1 0000\cdots.
\]

Thus the points of the interval are not primitive. They are equivalence classes of infinite recursive morphism paths.

---

### 8.2 The Sierpiński gasket

Let \(T\) be a port representing a triangular structural type. Introduce three contracting morphisms

\[
\phi_0,\phi_1,\phi_2 : T \to T
\]

with scale ratio

\[
r = \frac{1}{2}.
\]

The recursive region relation is

\[
U_T = U_{\phi_0} \vee U_{\phi_1} \vee U_{\phi_2}.
\]

Iterating gives

\[
U_T
=
\bigvee_{w \in \{0,1,2\}^n}
U_{\phi_w}.
\]

Points are infinite ternary words

\[
\alpha \in \{0,1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}},
\]

modulo the usual boundary identifications.

The address metric is

\[
d(\alpha,\beta)
=
2^{-N(\alpha,\beta)}.
\]

The Hausdorff dimension \(s\) satisfies

\[
\sum_{i=0}^2 r_i^s = 1.
\]

Since \(r_i=1/2\),

\[
3 \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^s = 1,
\]

so

\[
s = \frac{\log 3}{\log 2}.
\]

Again, the gasket is not constructed as a subset of a pre-existing Euclidean plane. It is generated by recursive morphisms; the embedding is secondary.

---

### 8.3 A recursive gauge geometry

Let \(\mathcal{C}\) have one port \(\ast\) and two generating morphisms

\[
a,b : \ast \to \ast.
\]

Assign matrices

\[
U_a, U_b \in \mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{C})
\]

as transports.

Suppose the recursive rule is

\[
a = b \circ a \circ b^{-1}.
\]

Then transport functoriality imposes

\[
U_a = U_b U_a U_b^{-1}.
\]

Equivalently,

\[
[U_a,U_b]=0.
\]

Thus the recursive equation enforces flatness.

If the equation holds only approximately, or is deformed by a scale-dependent correction,

\[
U_a = U_b U_a U_b^{-1} + \epsilon F(U_a,U_b),
\]

then the commutator becomes a curvature term. The recursive geometry thereby encodes a gauge field whose curvature measures failure of recursive self-similarity.

---

### 8.4 Non-spatial recursive locales

Let \(F\) be any finitely presented frame, with generators \(u_i\) and relations expressing certain generators as joins of others. One can construct a recursive structural geometry whose region frame is \(F\) by taking morphism symbols \(f_i\) and imposing refinement relations

\[
U_{f_i}
=
\bigvee_{j \in J_i}
U_{f_j}.
\]

If \(F\) is a pointless frame, the resulting RSG has no points.

Thus recursive structural geometry naturally contains non-spatial examples. This is not a pathology. It is a feature.

## 9. Relation to Existing Frameworks

### 9.1 Locale theory

RSG generalizes locale theory by adding recursive morphism dynamics. A locale is a frame of opens. An RSG produces such a frame from morphism refinement. The locale is not assumed; it is generated by recursive structure.

### 9.2 Coalgebra

Recursive geometries are coalgebraic. Infinite refinement trees are final coalgebras of pattern functors. Points are observations of coalgebraic behavior.

### 9.3 Fractal geometry

Iterated function systems construct fractals as attractors of maps on point spaces. RSG instead treats the maps themselves as primitive and constructs the space as their recursive invariant. The attractor is a spatial reflection of a deeper morphism system.

### 9.4 Operads and rewriting

Recursive morphism substitution resembles operadic composition and graph rewriting. The difference is the geometric interpretation: rewriting rules are not merely syntactic; they generate locale, metric, and curvature structure.

### 9.5 Noncommutative geometry

In noncommutative geometry, spaces are encoded by algebras. In RSG, spaces are encoded by recursive categories of morphisms. The two approaches may be related through categorical representations and transport functors.

## 10. Open Directions

The present paper establishes foundations. Several developments are natural.

### 10.1 Higher recursive geometry

Replace categories by \(\infty\)-categories. Refinement coherence then holds only up to higher homotopies. Curvature becomes higher gauge curvature, and Bianchi identities arise from higher-dimensional coherence cells.

### 10.2 Recursive Riemannian geometry

One may ask whether Einstein equations can be expressed as fixed-point equations for recursive refinement of metric transport. In that setting, curvature would be a renormalization defect.

### 10.3 Quantum recursive geometry

Assign amplitudes to refinement branches. A geometry becomes a weighted coalgebra, and path integrals become measures on infinite refinement trees.

### 10.4 Computational RSG

Because recursive geometries are finitely specifiable, they are naturally implementable. They may provide a foundation for discrete differential geometry, procedural geometry, and constructive spatial type theory.

## 11. Conclusion

Recursive Structural Geometry replaces the classical primacy of points with the primacy of self-generating morphisms. A geometry is not a set of points with added structure. It is a recursive system of transformations, refined by substitution, governed by coherence, and observed through derived points.

The framework yields:

1. a point-free categorical foundation;
2. coalgebraic semantics for infinite recursive structures;
3. emergent locales and derived points;
4. recursive metric and length functionals;
5. tensorial transport and curvature from functoriality;
6. a renormalization interpretation of curvature scaling;
7. natural examples ranging from fractals to gauge geometries.

The central inversion is therefore complete:

\[
\text{geometry}
\neq
\text{points equipped with maps}.
\]

Rather,

\[
\text{geometry}
=
\text{recursive morphisms whose coherent infinities produce points}.
\]

Points are not the foundation. They are the residue of infinite structural recursion.
