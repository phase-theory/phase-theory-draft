# Operator Phase Topology  
## Topological Structure Induced by Operator Evolution Instead of Open Sets

**Preprint — July 2026**

---

## Abstract

We develop a mathematical framework in which topology is not axiomatized by open sets but induced by operator evolution. The basic object is an **operator phase space** \(P\), whose points are operatorial physical phases: density operators, observables, channels, or gauge-equivalence classes thereof. An **evolutionary structure** on \(P\) is specified by admissible Liouvillian or superoperator flows together with a dynamical cost. From these data one constructs, for every phase \(p\in P\), an evolutionary cone \(R_\varepsilon(p)\) of phases reachable from \(p\) at cost less than \(\varepsilon\). The family \(\{R_\varepsilon(p)\}_{\varepsilon>0}\) is taken as the primitive neighborhood system. We prove that this system satisfies the neighborhood axioms and therefore induces a topology \(\tau_{\mathrm{OP}}\), the **operator phase topology**. Open sets appear only as derived objects: they are precisely the subsets stable under sufficiently small admissible evolutions.

The construction is formulated in tensorial component notation and is compatible with Hamiltonian, Lindbladian, and completely positive evolution. In finite dimensions, the differential form of the theory yields vector fields on operator manifolds,
\[
X_\alpha(\rho)^i{}_j
=
\mathcal L_\alpha{}^i{}_j{}^k{}_l\,\rho^l{}_k,
\]
and the resulting topology is governed by the Lie algebra generated by the admissible superoperators. When this Lie algebra is bracket-generating, the operator phase topology recovers the usual manifold topology on each dynamically accessible leaf. When it is not, the topology stratifies the operator phase space into dynamical orbits and entanglement classes. We analyze composite tensor-product systems, locality-weighted costs, qubit examples, and the non-\(T_0\) specialization orders produced by dissipative evolution. The result is a topology in which nearness means **dynamical transformability at small cost**, rather than static membership in a preassigned open set.

**Keywords:** operator phase topology, quantum control, Liouvillian dynamics, superoperator tensors, dynamical topology, sub-Riemannian geometry, entanglement stratification.

## 1. Introduction

Classical topology begins with a set \(X\) and a family \(\tau\subseteq 2^X\) of open sets satisfying the Kuratowski or neighborhood axioms. This is a static foundation: nearness, continuity, closure, and convergence are all consequences of a prior decision about which subsets are open. In many operator-theoretic and quantum-dynamical settings, this ordering is conceptually backwards. The physically primitive relation is often not that two operator states are statically close, but that one can be transformed into the other by an admissible evolution.

We therefore reverse the usual order of construction.

Let \(P\) be a space of operatorial phases. A point \(p\in P\) may be a density operator \(\rho\), an observable \(A\), a quantum channel \(\Phi\), or a gauge-equivalence class of such objects. Let \(\{\Phi_u\}\) be a family of admissible evolutions indexed by controls \(u\), with an associated dynamical cost \(c(u)\). Instead of specifying open sets, we specify the relation
\[
q \in R_\varepsilon(p)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\text{there exists an admissible evolution }u
\text{ with }c(u)<\varepsilon
\text{ and } \Phi_u(p)=q.
\]
The sets \(R_\varepsilon(p)\) are **evolutionary cones**. They are the primitive neighborhoods of \(p\). The topology is then recovered by declaring a set \(U\subseteq P\) to be open precisely when for every \(p\in U\) there exists \(\varepsilon>0\) such that
\[
R_\varepsilon(p)\subseteq U.
\]
Thus open sets are not axiomatic; they are certificates of dynamical stability.

This paper develops that construction in a form suitable for operator algebras, quantum control, and tensor-product systems. The central claim is:

> **Thesis.** A topology on a space of operator phases can be induced directly by operator evolution and dynamical cost. The resulting topology is more primitive than, and in general different from, the standard manifold or norm topology. Its open sets are exactly the subsets invariant under sufficiently small admissible evolutions.

The framework has several immediate features.

1. **Directionality.** If evolution is dissipative or semigroup-like, the topology need not be symmetric. It carries a natural specialization preorder expressing dynamical accessibility.

2. **Stratification.** If the allowed evolutions preserve spectra, entanglement invariants, or locality constraints, the topology decomposes into dynamically accessible strata.

3. **Tensorial locality.** In composite systems, the cost function can penalize nonlocal generators, making the topology sensitive to tensor-product structure.

4. **Recovery of standard topology.** When the admissible Liouvillians generate a bracket-generating distribution, the operator phase topology coincides with the usual smooth topology on each connected accessible leaf.

5. **Operational meaning.** Continuity becomes preservation of small-cost evolvability, not merely preservation of static neighborhoods.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 fixes tensorial conventions for operator phase spaces. Section 3 defines controlled operator evolutions and their costs. Section 4 constructs the operator phase topology and proves the neighborhood axioms. Section 5 relates the construction to Lie-algebraic controllability and standard manifold topology. Section 6 treats tensor-product systems and locality. Section 7 gives explicit qubit and two-qubit examples. Section 8 discusses morphisms and functoriality. Section 9 indicates infinite-dimensional extensions. Section 10 concludes.

## 2. Operator Phase Spaces and Tensorial Conventions

### 2.1 Operator phases

By an **operator phase** we mean a point in a space of operators representing a physical phase of a system. The word “phase” is used here in the sense of phase space, not merely the complex argument of a vector.

Let \(\mathcal H\) be a finite-dimensional complex Hilbert space of dimension \(n\). Let \(\mathcal B(\mathcal H)\) denote the algebra of linear operators on \(\mathcal H\). Typical operator phase spaces include:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal D(\mathcal H)
&=
\{\rho\in \mathcal B(\mathcal H):
\rho^\dagger=\rho,\ \rho\ge 0,\ \operatorname{Tr}\rho=1\},
\\[2mm]
\mathcal O(\mathcal H)
&=
\{A\in \mathcal B(\mathcal H): A^\dagger=A\},
\\[2mm]
\operatorname{CPTP}(\mathcal H)
&=
\{\Phi:\mathcal B(\mathcal H)\to \mathcal B(\mathcal H):
\Phi \text{ completely positive and trace preserving}\}.
\end{aligned}
\]

A point of \(P\) may also be a quotient object, for example a ray \([\psi]\) in projective Hilbert space, a coadjoint orbit of density operators with fixed spectrum, or a gauge orbit under a group of physically irrelevant unitaries.

### 2.2 Abstract index notation

We use abstract tensor indices. Vectors carry upper indices \(v^i\), covectors carry lower indices \(\omega_i\), and operators carry one upper and one lower index:
\[
A^i{}_j.
\]
Composition is contraction:
\[
(AB)^i{}_j = A^i{}_k B^k{}_j.
\]
The trace is
\[
\operatorname{Tr}A = A^i{}_i.
\]
The adjoint is denoted \(A^\dagger\), with components understood relative to the Hilbert-space inner product.

A density operator is written
\[
\rho^i{}_j,
\qquad
\rho^i{}_j = \overline{\rho^j{}_i},
\qquad
\rho^i{}_i=1,
\qquad
\rho\ge 0.
\]

### 2.3 Superoperators as rank-four tensors

A linear evolution generator on \(\mathcal B(\mathcal H)\) is a superoperator
\[
\mathcal L:\mathcal B(\mathcal H)\to \mathcal B(\mathcal H).
\]
In components,
\[
(\mathcal L\rho)^i{}_j
=
\mathcal L^i{}_j{}^k{}_l\,\rho^l{}_k.
\]
Thus \(\mathcal L\) is a rank-four tensor with two output indices \((i,j)\) and two input indices \((k,l)\).

Trace preservation of the flow \(e^{t\mathcal L}\) is equivalent, at the generator level, to
\[
\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal L\rho)=0
\quad\forall \rho,
\]
which in components reads
\[
\mathcal L^i{}_i{}^k{}_l = 0.
\]
Unitality, \(\mathcal L(I)=0\), reads
\[
\mathcal L^i{}_j{}^k{}_k = 0.
\]

Complete positivity of a superoperator \(\Phi\) can be expressed through its Choi tensor. With the convention
\[
C_\Phi^{ik}{}_{jl}
=
\Phi^i{}_j{}^k{}_l,
\]
complete positivity is the positivity condition
\[
C_\Phi \ge 0
\]
as an operator on \(\mathcal H\otimes \mathcal H\).

### 2.4 Hamiltonian generators

Let \(H^i{}_j\) be Hermitian. The Hamiltonian Liouvillian is
\[
\mathcal L_H(\rho)
=
-i[H,\rho].
\]
In components,
\[
(\mathcal L_H\rho)^i{}_j
=
-i
\left(
H^i{}_k\rho^k{}_j
-
\rho^i{}_k H^k{}_j
\right).
\]
This generator is trace preserving because
\[
\operatorname{Tr}[H,\rho]=0.
\]

### 2.5 Lindblad generators

Let \(L^i{}_j\) be a jump operator. The Gorini–Kossakowski–Sudarshan–Lindblad generator is
\[
\mathcal L_L(\rho)
=
L\rho L^\dagger
-
\frac12\{L^\dagger L,\rho\}.
\]
In components,
\[
\begin{aligned}
(\mathcal L_L\rho)^i{}_j
&=
L^i{}_k\rho^k{}_l (L^\dagger)^l{}_j
\\
&\quad
-\frac12
(L^\dagger L)^i{}_k\rho^k{}_j
-\frac12
\rho^i{}_k(L^\dagger L)^k{}_j.
\end{aligned}
\]
Again,
\[
\operatorname{Tr}\mathcal L_L(\rho)=0.
\]

More generally, a Markovian quantum dynamical semigroup has generator
\[
\mathcal L
=
-i\,\operatorname{ad}_H
+
\sum_\mu
\left(
L_\mu \,\cdot\, L_\mu^\dagger
-
\frac12\{L_\mu^\dagger L_\mu,\cdot\}
\right).
\]

## 3. Evolutionary Structures

### 3.1 Controlled Liouvillian dynamics

Let \(P\) be an operator phase space. Fix an indexed family of admissible generators
\[
\{\mathcal L_\alpha\}_{\alpha\in I},
\]
where each \(\mathcal L_\alpha\) is a superoperator preserving \(P\). A control is a measurable function
\[
u:[0,T]\to \mathbb R^I
\]
with finite support in \(I\) at each time. The controlled evolution is
\[
\frac{d}{dt}\rho^i{}_j(t)
=
u^\alpha(t)\,
\mathcal L_\alpha{}^i{}_j{}^k{}_l\,
\rho^l{}_k(t),
\]
using the Einstein summation convention over repeated indices \(\alpha,k,l\).

For each control \(u\), let
\[
\Phi_u:P\to P
\]
denote the endpoint map:
\[
\Phi_u(p)=\rho(T),
\]
where \(\rho(t)\) solves the controlled equation with \(\rho(0)=p\). For piecewise-constant controls, \(\Phi_u\) is a finite composition of exponentials:
\[
\Phi_u
=
e^{t_m\mathcal L_{\alpha_m}}
\circ
\cdots
\circ
e^{t_1\mathcal L_{\alpha_1}}.
\]

We also allow discrete admissible evolutions, such as quantum channels
\[
\Gamma:\mathcal D(\mathcal H)\to \mathcal D(\mathcal H),
\]
provided they are assigned a finite cost.

### 3.2 Dynamical cost

A topology induced by evolution requires a notion of dynamical size. Let each generator \(\mathcal L_\alpha\) carry a positive weight \(w_\alpha>0\). For \(1\le p\le \infty\), define the weighted control norm
\[
\|u(t)\|_{w,p}
=
\left(
\sum_{\alpha\in I}
w_\alpha^p |u^\alpha(t)|^p
\right)^{1/p},
\]
with the usual modification for \(p=\infty\). The cost of a control is
\[
c(u)
=
\int_0^T \|u(t)\|_{w,p}\,dt.
\]
For piecewise-constant controls,
\[
c(u)
=
\sum_{r=1}^m
|t_r|\,
\|u_r\|_{w,p}.
\]
For a discrete channel \(\Gamma\), we assign a cost \(c(\Gamma)\in[0,\infty]\).

The weights \(w_\alpha\) encode physical constraints. For example:

- large \(w_\alpha\) penalizes expensive generators;
- local generators may be assigned small weights;
- nonlocal entangling generators may be assigned large weights;
- forbidden generators are assigned weight \(\infty\).

### 3.3 Evolutionary cones

For \(\varepsilon>0\) and \(p\in P\), define the \(\varepsilon\)-reachable cone
\[
R_\varepsilon(p)
=
\left\{
q\in P:
\exists u,\ c(u)<\varepsilon,\ \Phi_u(p)=q
\right\}.
\]
The family \(\{R_\varepsilon(p)\}_{\varepsilon>0}\) is the primitive object of the theory.

The construction assumes only the following two structural properties.

**E0. Identity.** For every \(p\) and every \(\varepsilon>0\),
\[
p\in R_\varepsilon(p).
\]
This follows from the zero-cost identity evolution.

**E1. Concatenation.** If
\[
q\in R_\delta(p),
\qquad
r\in R_\eta(q),
\]
then
\[
r\in R_{\delta+\eta}(p).
\]
This follows by concatenating the two controls and using subadditivity of cost.

No open sets are assumed at this stage.

## 4. Operator Phase Topology

### 4.1 Neighborhood filters from evolution

For each \(p\in P\), define a family of subsets
\[
\mathcal N_p
=
\left\{
N\subseteq P:
\exists \varepsilon>0,\ R_\varepsilon(p)\subseteq N
\right\}.
\]
The elements of \(\mathcal N_p\) are called **evolutionary neighborhoods** of \(p\).

This definition is deliberately not formulated in terms of open sets. The primitive datum is the cone \(R_\varepsilon(p)\), i.e. the set of phases reachable from \(p\) by small dynamical effort.

### 4.2 Topology theorem

**Theorem 1.**  
Let \(P\) be a set equipped with reachable cones \(R_\varepsilon(p)\) satisfying E0 and E1. Then the families \(\mathcal N_p\) satisfy the neighborhood axioms and therefore define a unique topology \(\tau_{\mathrm{OP}}\) on \(P\). A set \(U\subseteq P\) is open if and only if
\[
\forall p\in U,\ \exists \varepsilon>0,\ R_\varepsilon(p)\subseteq U.
\]

**Proof.**

We verify the neighborhood axioms.

1. **Containment of the base point.**  
   Let \(N\in\mathcal N_p\). Then \(R_\varepsilon(p)\subseteq N\) for some \(\varepsilon>0\). By E0, \(p\in R_\varepsilon(p)\). Hence \(p\in N\).

2. **Finite intersection.**  
   Let \(N_1,N_2\in\mathcal N_p\). Then there exist \(\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2>0\) such that
   \[
   R_{\varepsilon_1}(p)\subseteq N_1,
   \qquad
   R_{\varepsilon_2}(p)\subseteq N_2.
   \]
   Let \(\varepsilon=\min\{\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2\}\). Since smaller cost gives a smaller reachable cone,
   \[
   R_\varepsilon(p)
   \subseteq
   R_{\varepsilon_1}(p)\cap R_{\varepsilon_2}(p)
   \subseteq
   N_1\cap N_2.
   \]
   Hence \(N_1\cap N_2\in\mathcal N_p\).

3. **Stability under small evolution.**  
   Let \(N\in\mathcal N_p\). Choose \(\varepsilon>0\) such that
   \[
   R_\varepsilon(p)\subseteq N.
   \]
   Let \(\delta=\varepsilon/2\), and set
   \[
   M=R_\delta(p).
   \]
   Then \(M\in\mathcal N_p\). If \(q\in M\), then \(q\in R_\delta(p)\). By E1,
   \[
   R_\delta(q)\subseteq R_{\delta+\delta}(p)=R_\varepsilon(p)\subseteq N.
   \]
   Therefore \(N\in\mathcal N_q\) for every \(q\in M\).

4. **Upward closure.**  
   If \(N\in\mathcal N_p\) and \(N\subseteq O\subseteq P\), then clearly \(O\in\mathcal N_p\).

Thus \(\{\mathcal N_p\}_{p\in P}\) is a neighborhood system. By the standard neighborhood characterization of topological spaces, there is a unique topology \(\tau_{\mathrm{OP}}\) whose neighborhood filter at \(p\) is \(\mathcal N_p\). By construction, \(U\in\tau_{\mathrm{OP}}\) iff \(U\in\mathcal N_p\) for every \(p\in U\), i.e.
\[
\forall p\in U,\ \exists\varepsilon>0,\ R_\varepsilon(p)\subseteq U.
\]
∎

We call \(\tau_{\mathrm{OP}}\) the **operator phase topology** induced by the evolutionary structure.

### 4.3 Basis of evolutionary cones

**Proposition 2.**  
The collection
\[
\mathcal B
=
\{R_\varepsilon(p): p\in P,\ \varepsilon>0\}
\]
is a basis for \(\tau_{\mathrm{OP}}\).

**Proof.**  
Let \(x\in R_\varepsilon(p)\cap R_\eta(y)\). Then there exist controls \(u,v\) such that
\[
\Phi_u(p)=x,
\quad
c(u)<\varepsilon,
\]
and
\[
\Phi_v(y)=x,
\quad
c(v)<\eta.
\]
Choose
\[
\delta
=
\min\{\varepsilon-c(u),\ \eta-c(v)\}>0.
\]
If \(z\in R_\delta(x)\), then by concatenation,
\[
z\in R_{c(u)+\delta}(p)\subseteq R_\varepsilon(p),
\]
and similarly
\[
z\in R_{c(v)+\delta}(y)\subseteq R_\eta(y).
\]
Hence
\[
R_\delta(x)
\subseteq
R_\varepsilon(p)\cap R_\eta(y).
\]
Thus \(\mathcal B\) is a basis. ∎

### 4.4 Closure, interior, and dynamical adherence

Define the forward dynamical quasi-distance
\[
d^+(p,q)
=
\inf
\left\{
c(u): \Phi_u(p)=q
\right\},
\]
with \(d^+(p,q)=\infty\) if \(q\) is not reachable from \(p\). Then
\[
R_\varepsilon(p)
=
\{q\in P: d^+(p,q)<\varepsilon\}.
\]

For \(A\subseteq P\), define
\[
d^+(p,A)
=
\inf_{a\in A} d^+(p,a).
\]

**Proposition 3.**  
The closure operator of \(\tau_{\mathrm{OP}}\) is
\[
\operatorname{cl}_{\mathrm{OP}}(A)
=
\left\{
p\in P:
d^+(p,A)=0
\right\}.
\]
Equivalently,
\[
p\in \operatorname{cl}_{\mathrm{OP}}(A)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\forall\varepsilon>0,\ R_\varepsilon(p)\cap A\neq\varnothing.
\]

Thus \(p\) lies in the closure of \(A\) precisely when \(p\) can be evolved, at arbitrarily small cost, into \(A\). Closure is dynamical adherence, not merely static accumulation.

**Proof.**  
By definition of the topology,
\[
p\in \operatorname{cl}_{\mathrm{OP}}(A)
\]
iff every neighborhood of \(p\) intersects \(A\). Since the sets \(R_\varepsilon(p)\) form a neighborhood basis, this is equivalent to
\[
\forall\varepsilon>0,\ R_\varepsilon(p)\cap A\neq\varnothing.
\]
But \(R_\varepsilon(p)\cap A\neq\varnothing\) iff there exists \(a\in A\) with \(d^+(p,a)<\varepsilon\). Hence the condition is equivalent to
\[
\inf_{a\in A}d^+(p,a)=0.
\]
∎

The interior is
\[
\operatorname{int}_{\mathrm{OP}}(A)
=
\left\{
p\in P:
\exists\varepsilon>0,\ R_\varepsilon(p)\subseteq A
\right\}.
\]
Equivalently,
\[
\operatorname{int}_{\mathrm{OP}}(A)
=
\left\{
p\in P:
d^+(p,P\setminus A)>0
\right\}.
\]

### 4.5 Kuratowski closure axioms

The closure operator \(\operatorname{cl}_{\mathrm{OP}}\) satisfies the Kuratowski axioms.

**Proposition 4.**  
For all \(A,B\subseteq P\),

1. \(\operatorname{cl}_{\mathrm{OP}}(\varnothing)=\varnothing\);
2. \(A\subseteq \operatorname{cl}_{\mathrm{OP}}(A)\);
3. \(\operatorname{cl}_{\mathrm{OP}}(\operatorname{cl}_{\mathrm{OP}}(A))
   =
   \operatorname{cl}_{\mathrm{OP}}(A)\);
4. \(\operatorname{cl}_{\mathrm{OP}}(A\cup B)
   =
   \operatorname{cl}_{\mathrm{OP}}(A)\cup \operatorname{cl}_{\mathrm{OP}}(B)\).

**Proof.**  
The first three follow from Theorem 1. For the fourth, observe that
\[
d^+(p,A\cup B)
=
\min\{d^+(p,A),d^+(p,B)\}.
\]
Therefore
\[
d^+(p,A\cup B)=0
\]
iff
\[
d^+(p,A)=0
\quad\text{or}\quad
d^+(p,B)=0.
\]
∎

### 4.6 Specialization preorder and dissipative directionality

Every topology has a specialization preorder. In \(\tau_{\mathrm{OP}}\), it has a direct dynamical meaning.

Define
\[
p\preceq q
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
d^+(p,q)=0.
\]
Then
\[
p\preceq q
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
p\in \operatorname{cl}_{\mathrm{OP}}(\{q\})
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\text{every open set containing }p\text{ contains }q.
\]

Thus \(p\preceq q\) means that \(q\) is reachable from \(p\) at arbitrarily small dynamical cost. In reversible Hamiltonian systems this relation is symmetric. In dissipative systems it is generally not symmetric, and the topology is non-\(T_0\) or non-\(T_1\). The Kolmogorov quotient identifies phases \(p,q\) such that
\[
d^+(p,q)=d^+(q,p)=0.
\]
This quotient is the space of dynamically indistinguishable operator phases.

### 4.7 Symmetrized operator phase topology

When the admissible evolutions are reversible, for example under Hamiltonian unitary flows, one may define the symmetric distance
\[
d_{\mathrm{sym}}(p,q)
=
\max\{d^+(p,q),d^+(q,p)\}.
\]
If negative-time controls are allowed at the same cost, then \(d^+\) is already symmetric. The corresponding topology is generated by ordinary metric balls
\[
B_\varepsilon(p)
=
\{q:d_{\mathrm{sym}}(p,q)<\varepsilon\}.
\]
In dissipative settings, the forward topology \(\tau_{\mathrm{OP}}\) and the symmetrized topology contain different physical information. The forward topology retains dynamical direction; the symmetrized topology forgets it.

## 5. Differential Structure and Lie-Algebraic Controllability

### 5.1 Vector fields on operator phase spaces

Assume now that \(P\) is a smooth finite-dimensional manifold embedded in \(\mathcal B(\mathcal H)\), and that each generator \(\mathcal L_\alpha\) induces a smooth vector field
\[
X_\alpha
\]
on \(P\). In components,
\[
X_\alpha(\rho)^i{}_j
=
\mathcal L_\alpha{}^i{}_j{}^k{}_l\,\rho^l{}_k.
\]
The controlled equation is
\[
\dot \rho = u^\alpha X_\alpha(\rho).
\]

The distribution generated by the admissible evolutions is
\[
\Delta_\rho
=
\operatorname{span}\{X_\alpha(\rho)\}_{\alpha\in I}
\subseteq T_\rho P.
\]

### 5.2 Lie brackets of superoperator vector fields

For linear superoperator fields, the Lie bracket is again linear. Let
\[
X_{\mathcal L}(\rho)=\mathcal L\rho,
\qquad
X_{\mathcal M}(\rho)=\mathcal M\rho.
\]
Then
\[
[X_{\mathcal L},X_{\mathcal M}]
=
X_{\mathcal M\circ\mathcal L-\mathcal L\circ\mathcal M}.
\]
Equivalently, up to the conventional sign,
\[
[X_{\mathcal L},X_{\mathcal M}]
=
-X_{[\mathcal L,\mathcal M]},
\]
where
\[
[\mathcal L,\mathcal M]
=
\mathcal L\circ\mathcal M-\mathcal M\circ\mathcal L.
\]

For Hamiltonian generators,
\[
\mathcal L_H=-i\,\operatorname{ad}_H,
\qquad
\mathcal L_K=-i\,\operatorname{ad}_K,
\]
one obtains
\[
[\mathcal L_H,\mathcal L_K]
=
\mathcal L_{-i[H,K]}.
\]
Since \(H,K\) are Hermitian, \(-i[H,K]\) is Hermitian. Thus Hamiltonian Liouvillians close under Lie bracket modulo the center.

### 5.3 Bracket-generating condition

Let \(\operatorname{Lie}(\{\mathcal L_\alpha\})\) denote the Lie algebra generated by the admissible superoperators. At each \(\rho\), define
\[
\Delta^{(\infty)}_\rho
=
\operatorname{span}
\left\{
X_{\mathcal L}(\rho):
\mathcal L\in \operatorname{Lie}(\{\mathcal L_\alpha\})
\right\}.
\]
The system is **bracket-generating** on \(P\) if
\[
\Delta^{(\infty)}_\rho
=
T_\rho P
\]
for every \(\rho\in P\).

### 5.4 Recovery of the manifold topology

**Theorem 5.**  
Let \(P\) be a connected finite-dimensional smooth operator phase manifold. Let the admissible generators be smooth, complete, and bracket-generating on \(P\). Let the control cost be a continuous positive-definite Finsler cost on the distribution. Then the operator phase topology \(\tau_{\mathrm{OP}}\) coincides with the standard manifold topology on \(P\).

**Proof sketch.**  
The controlled system defines a sub-Finsler structure on \(P\). The distance
\[
d^+(p,q)
=
\inf\left\{c(u):\Phi_u(p)=q\right\}
\]
is the associated Carnot–Carathéodory distance. By the Chow–Rashevskii theorem, bracket-generation implies that any two points in the same connected component can be joined by an admissible horizontal curve, so the distance is finite. Standard results in sub-Riemannian geometry then imply that the metric topology induced by this distance coincides with the original manifold topology. Since in the reversible or symmetrized case the reachable cones are metric balls, \(\tau_{\mathrm{OP}}\) equals the manifold topology. ∎

If the bracket-generating condition fails, the accessible sets are lower-dimensional leaves. The operator phase topology then becomes the disjoint union of the leaf topologies, with distinct leaves separated by infinite dynamical distance.

### 5.5 Unitary orbits and isospectral strata

Let \(P=\mathcal D(\mathcal H)\), and let the admissible generators be all Hamiltonian Liouvillians
\[
\mathcal L_H=-i[H,\cdot],
\qquad
H=H^\dagger.
\]
The flow is
\[
\rho(t)=U(t)\rho(0)U(t)^\dagger.
\]
The spectrum of \(\rho\) is invariant. Hence the dynamically accessible set from \(\rho\) is the unitary orbit
\[
\mathcal O_\rho
=
\{U\rho U^\dagger: U\in U(n)\}.
\]
If \(\rho\) has eigenvalues with multiplicities \(m_1,\dots,m_r\), then
\[
\mathcal O_\rho
\cong
U(n)\big/
\left(
U(m_1)\times\cdots\times U(m_r)
\right).
\]
The Hamiltonian operator phase topology is therefore the standard topology on each isospectral orbit, but it does not connect different spectra. Radial motion in the state space requires dissipative or non-unitary generators.

## 6. Tensor-Product Systems and Locality

### 6.1 Composite index notation

Let
\[
\mathcal H
=
\bigotimes_{a=1}^N \mathcal H_a,
\qquad
\dim\mathcal H_a=n_a.
\]
A composite tensor index is written
\[
i=(i_1,\dots,i_N),
\qquad
j=(j_1,\dots,j_N).
\]
An operator has components
\[
A^{i_1\cdots i_N}{}_{j_1\cdots j_N}.
\]

A local operator acting only on subsystem \(a\) has components
\[
(A^{(a)})^{i_1\cdots i_N}{}_{j_1\cdots j_N}
=
A_{(a)}^{i_a}{}_{j_a}
\prod_{b\neq a}
\delta^{i_b}_{j_b}.
\]
A local superoperator acting on subsystem \(a\) similarly has components
\[
\mathcal L^{(a)}
{}^{i_1\cdots i_N}{}_{j_1\cdots j_N}
{}^{k_1\cdots k_N}{}_{l_1\cdots l_N}
=
\mathcal L_{(a)}
{}^{i_a}{}_{j_a}{}^{k_a}{}_{l_a}
\prod_{b\neq a}
\delta^{i_b}_{l_b}\delta^{k_b}_{j_b}.
\]

### 6.2 Locality-weighted cost

Let \(\operatorname{supp}(\alpha)\subseteq\{1,\dots,N\}\) denote the support of a generator \(\mathcal L_\alpha\). Define its locality degree
\[
|\alpha|
=
|\operatorname{supp}(\alpha)|.
\]
Choose a locality penalty \(\lambda>1\). A locality-sensitive cost is
\[
c_\lambda(u)
=
\int_0^T
\left(
\sum_{\alpha}
\lambda^{p|\alpha|}
|u^\alpha(t)|^p
\right)^{1/p}
dt.
\]
Small-cost evolutions are then predominantly local. The resulting operator phase topology encodes not merely reachability but reachability under locality constraints.

### 6.3 Entanglement stratification

Suppose the admissible generators are local Hamiltonians only:
\[
\mathcal L_{H_a}
=
-i[I\otimes\cdots\otimes H_a\otimes\cdots\otimes I,\cdot].
\]
The generated unitaries are of the form
\[
U=U_1\otimes\cdots\otimes U_N.
\]
For a pure state \(\psi\), the Schmidt coefficients across every bipartition are invariant. Therefore the operator phase topology stratifies the projective state space into local-unitary entanglement classes.

If one adds entangling interactions, the Lie algebra may become bracket-generating. For example, on two qubits, local controls
\[
\mathfrak{su}(2)\otimes I,
\qquad
I\otimes \mathfrak{su}(2),
\]
together with an entangling Hamiltonian such as
\[
H_{ZZ}=\sigma_z\otimes\sigma_z
\]
generate \(\mathfrak{su}(4)\). Consequently, the induced operator phase topology on the full unitary orbit becomes the standard topology.

More generally, for \(N\) qubits with a connected interaction graph, local \(\mathfrak{su}(2)\) controls plus pairwise entangling edges generate \(\mathfrak{su}(2^N)\) modulo the center. The operator phase topology then loses its entanglement stratification and recovers the full unitary topology.

## 7. Examples

### 7.1 Qubit under Hamiltonian control

Let \(\mathcal H=\mathbb C^2\). Write a density operator as
\[
\rho^i{}_j
=
\frac12
\left(
\delta^i{}_j
+
r^a \sigma_a{}^i{}_j
\right),
\]
where \(a=1,2,3\), \(\sigma_a\) are Pauli matrices, and \(r=(r^1,r^2,r^3)\) is the Bloch vector with \(|r|\le 1\).

Let
\[
H=\frac12 h^a\sigma_a.
\]
The Hamiltonian evolution is
\[
\dot\rho=-i[H,\rho].
\]
Using
\[
[\sigma_a,\sigma_b]=2i\epsilon_{abc}\sigma_c,
\]
one obtains
\[
\dot r^a
=
\epsilon^a{}_{bc}h^b r^c.
\]
Thus the Bloch vector rotates around the axis \(h\).

If all Hamiltonian controls are allowed and the cost is
\[
c(h)=\int_0^T \|h(t)\|\,dt,
\]
then the reachable set from \(r\) is the sphere
\[
S_{|r|}
=
\{s\in\mathbb R^3: |s|=|r|\}.
\]
The origin \(r=0\) is fixed. The operator phase topology is therefore the disjoint union
\[
\{0\}
\;\sqcup\;
\bigsqcup_{0<\rho\le 1} S_\rho.
\]
Different radii are dynamically disconnected. The topology is not the Euclidean topology on the Bloch ball; it is the topology induced by unitary evolution.

### 7.2 Qubit with dephasing

Add a dephasing Lindblad generator
\[
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{deph}}(\rho)
=
\gamma
\left(
\sigma_z\rho\sigma_z-\rho
\right).
\]
In Bloch coordinates,
\[
\dot r^x=-2\gamma r^x,
\qquad
\dot r^y=-2\gamma r^y,
\qquad
\dot r^z=0.
\]
The flow contracts the Bloch ball toward the \(z\)-axis.

The forward operator phase topology generated by Hamiltonian rotations and dephasing has open sets that are stable under small rotations and small dephasing contractions. If a set contains a point with transverse component \((r^x,r^y)\neq 0\), then, to be open, it must also contain a short forward segment of its dephasing trajectory. Thus openness is no longer purely spherical; it encodes the dissipative vector field.

The specialization preorder records the fact that points can be dynamically driven toward the dephasing axis. If additional reset channels are admitted, the preorder becomes stronger and may identify entire basins of attraction in the Kolmogorov quotient.

### 7.3 Two-qubit local versus entangling evolution

Let
\[
\mathcal H=\mathbb C^2\otimes\mathbb C^2.
\]
Consider the pure state
\[
\psi_\theta
=
\cos\theta\,|00\rangle
+
\sin\theta\,|11\rangle,
\qquad
0\le \theta\le \frac{\pi}{4}.
\]
Under local unitaries,
\[
U=U_A\otimes U_B,
\]
the parameter \(\theta\) is invariant. The reachable orbit is a local-unitary entanglement class. The corresponding operator phase topology has strata labeled by the Schmidt coefficient \(\cos\theta\sin\theta\).

If we add the entangling Hamiltonian
\[
H_{ZZ}=\sigma_z\otimes\sigma_z,
\]
then the Lie algebra generated by
\[
\mathfrak{su}(2)\otimes I,
\qquad
I\otimes\mathfrak{su}(2),
\qquad
-i[H_{ZZ},\cdot]
\]
is \(\mathfrak{su}(4)\) modulo the center. The reachable set from a generic pure state becomes the full unitary orbit, and the operator phase topology coincides with the usual topology on that orbit.

This example illustrates the central principle: the topology is not attached to the state space once and for all; it is induced by the allowed operator evolution.

## 8. Morphisms and Functoriality

Let
\[
(P,\{R^P_\varepsilon\}),
\qquad
(Q,\{R^Q_\varepsilon\})
\]
be two evolutionary operator phase spaces.

### 8.1 Evolutionary continuity

A map
\[
F:P\to Q
\]
is **evolutionarily continuous** if for every \(p\in P\) and every \(\varepsilon>0\), there exists \(\delta>0\) such that
\[
F(R^P_\delta(p))
\subseteq
R^Q_\varepsilon(F(p)).
\]
Equivalently, small-cost evolutions in \(P\) are mapped to small-cost evolutions in \(Q\).

**Proposition 6.**  
Evolutionary continuity is equivalent to continuity with respect to the operator phase topologies \(\tau_{\mathrm{OP}}^P\) and \(\tau_{\mathrm{OP}}^Q\).

**Proof.**  
Suppose \(F\) is evolutionarily continuous. Let \(U\subseteq Q\) be open and let \(p\in F^{-1}(U)\). Then \(F(p)\in U\), so there exists \(\varepsilon>0\) such that
\[
R^Q_\varepsilon(F(p))\subseteq U.
\]
By evolutionary continuity, there exists \(\delta>0\) such that
\[
F(R^P_\delta(p))
\subseteq
R^Q_\varepsilon(F(p))
\subseteq U.
\]
Hence
\[
R^P_\delta(p)\subseteq F^{-1}(U),
\]
so \(F^{-1}(U)\) is open.

Conversely, if \(F\) is topologically continuous, apply continuity to the open set
\[
U=Q
\]
or more usefully to neighborhoods of \(F(p)\). Since the sets \(R^Q_\varepsilon(F(p))\) form a neighborhood basis, their preimages contain some \(R^P_\delta(p)\). This gives evolutionary continuity. ∎

### 8.2 Intertwining maps

A strong sufficient condition for evolutionary continuity is intertwining of generators. Suppose
\[
F\circ \Phi^P_u
=
\Phi^Q_u\circ F
\]
for every admissible control \(u\), and suppose \(F\) does not increase cost. Then
\[
F(R^P_\varepsilon(p))
\subseteq
R^Q_\varepsilon(F(p)),
\]
so \(F\) is evolutionarily continuous.

In quantum settings, such maps arise from covariant channels, symmetry reductions, tensor-network coarse-grainings, and quotienting by gauge groups.

## 9. Infinite-Dimensional and Algebraic Extensions

The finite-dimensional construction extends naturally to operator algebras.

Let \(\mathfrak A\) be a \(C^*\)-algebra, and let \(S(\mathfrak A)\) be its state space. A point \(\omega\in S(\mathfrak A)\) is a positive normalized linear functional
\[
\omega:\mathfrak A\to\mathbb C.
\]
A quantum dynamical semigroup is a weak-\(*\) continuous semigroup of completely positive unital maps
\[
T_t:\mathfrak A\to\mathfrak A,
\]
with predual action on states
\[
\omega_t(a)=\omega(T_t a).
\]

An evolutionary structure may be specified by a family of bounded derivations or GKSL generators \(\mathcal L_\alpha\) on \(\mathfrak A\), together with a cost. The reachable cone \(R_\varepsilon(\omega)\) is defined exactly as before:
\[
R_\varepsilon(\omega)
=
\left\{
\eta\in S(\mathfrak A):
\eta=\Phi_u(\omega),\ c(u)<\varepsilon
\right\}.
\]
The neighborhood theorem remains valid because it uses only identity and concatenation.

For unbounded generators, one restricts to invariant domains or uses local semigroups. In weak-\(*\) settings, one may also introduce observational refinements. Let \(\mathcal F\subset \mathfrak A\) be a finite set of observables and \(\eta>0\). Define an observational reachable set
\[
R_{\varepsilon,\mathcal F,\eta}(\omega)
=
\left\{
\varphi\in S(\mathfrak A):
\exists u,\ c(u)<\varepsilon,
\ |\varphi(a)-\omega_u(a)|<\eta
\ \forall a\in\mathcal F
\right\}.
\]
This yields a topology induced by evolution plus finite-resolution observation. The pure evolutionary topology is the limit in which observational tolerance is removed.

## 10. Discussion

The operator phase topology replaces the static primacy of open sets with a dynamical primitive: small-cost evolvability. The construction is minimal. One needs only a set of operator phases, admissible evolutions, and a cost satisfying identity and concatenation. From these data, neighborhoods, open sets, closure, interior, and specialization arise automatically.

The framework clarifies several structural phenomena in operator dynamics.

First, it explains why unitary quantum dynamics naturally produces stratified topologies. Hamiltonian evolution preserves spectra, so the induced topology lives on coadjoint orbits rather than on the full state space.

Second, it gives a precise topological meaning to locality constraints. By weighting generators according to tensor-support size, one obtains topologies in which nonlocal transformations are topologically expensive. Entanglement classes appear as dynamically separated strata.

Third, it connects quantum control theory with topology. The bracket-generating condition is exactly the condition under which the dynamical topology forgets its anisotropic origins and becomes the ordinary manifold topology.

Fourth, it provides a natural language for dissipative systems. Semigroup evolution produces non-symmetric quasi-distances and specialization preorders. Attractors, basins, and irreversible reductions are encoded not as additional structures but as topological features.

The central shift is therefore not merely technical but conceptual. In ordinary topology, one asks:

> Which subsets are open?

In operator phase topology, one asks:

> Which phases are reachable from which others at small dynamical cost?

Open sets are then nothing more than subsets stable under such reachability.

This reversal is particularly appropriate for quantum theory, where physical equivalence is often generated by dynamics, gauge action, and control constraints rather than by a preassigned metric. Operator phase topology makes that dynamical equivalence into a topological structure in its own right.

## Appendix A. Summary of Core Definitions

Let \(P\) be an operator phase space. Let \(\Phi_u:P\to P\) be admissible evolutions with cost \(c(u)\). Define
\[
R_\varepsilon(p)
=
\{\Phi_u(p):c(u)<\varepsilon\}.
\]
Assume:

1. \(p\in R_\varepsilon(p)\);
2. \(q\in R_\delta(p)\) and \(r\in R_\eta(q)\) imply \(r\in R_{\delta+\eta}(p)\).

Then the operator phase topology is
\[
\tau_{\mathrm{OP}}
=
\left\{
U\subseteq P:
\forall p\in U,\ \exists\varepsilon>0,\ R_\varepsilon(p)\subseteq U
\right\}.
\]
The closure is
\[
\operatorname{cl}_{\mathrm{OP}}(A)
=
\{p\in P:\forall\varepsilon>0,\ R_\varepsilon(p)\cap A\neq\varnothing\}.
\]
The specialization preorder is
\[
p\preceq q
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\forall\varepsilon>0,\ q\in R_\varepsilon(p).
\]
For smooth systems,
\[
X_\alpha(\rho)^i{}_j
=
\mathcal L_\alpha{}^i{}_j{}^k{}_l\rho^l{}_k,
\]
and if the Lie algebra generated by the \(X_\alpha\) is bracket-generating, \(\tau_{\mathrm{OP}}\) coincides with the standard manifold topology on each connected accessible leaf.
