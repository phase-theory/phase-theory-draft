# Tensorial Resonance Theory: A \(3\to 2\) Square-Zero Calculus, Cohomology, and Spectral Invariants

**Preprint — July 2026**

---

## Abstract

We introduce **tensorial resonance theory** (TRT), a new algebraic and cohomological theory built from a primitive tensorial operation
\[
R:\Lambda^3 V\longrightarrow \Lambda^2 V
\]
on a finite-dimensional vector space \(V\) over a field of characteristic zero. Unlike Lie, Poisson, Gerstenhaber, or \(L_\infty\)-structures, whose differentials are generated by binary or higher operations into a single vector, the fundamental resonance operation sends a triple of vectors to a bivector. The canonical extension
\[
\widehat R:\Lambda^\bullet V\longrightarrow \Lambda^{\bullet-1}V
\]
is required to satisfy the quadratic **resonance identity**
\[
\widehat R^2=0.
\]
Dualization produces a cochain complex
\[
\bigl(\Lambda^\bullet V^*,d_R\bigr),
\qquad d_R^2=0,
\]
whose cohomology groups \(H_R^\bullet(V)\) are the **resonance cohomology groups** of \((V,R)\). We develop the foundational category of resonance algebras, prove a finite-dimensional Hodge decomposition for inner-product resonance algebras, construct a spectral sequence for filtered resonance structures, define resonance varieties and resonant subspaces, and compute explicit examples. The theory yields new tensorial invariants, a natural deformation complex for higher-order multilinear structures, and a framework for analyzing square-zero tensorial operators beyond the classical binary paradigm.

---

## 1. Introduction

Classical cohomological constructions in algebra and geometry are overwhelmingly governed by binary operations. A Lie bracket
\[
[\cdot,\cdot]:\Lambda^2\mathfrak g\to \mathfrak g
\]
produces the Chevalley–Eilenberg differential. An associative product
\[
\mu:A\otimes A\to A
\]
produces the Hochschild differential. A Poisson bivector
\[
\pi\in \Lambda^2 T^*M
\]
produces a Koszul-type differential on multivector fields. In each case, the square-zero condition \(d^2=0\) is equivalent to a structural identity: Jacobi, associativity, or \([\pi,\pi]=0\).

This paper inaugurates a different paradigm. The fundamental object is not a binary operation but a **ternary-to-bivector tensor**
\[
R:\Lambda^3 V\to \Lambda^2 V.
\]
Such an operation does not collapse three inputs into one output; it transforms three vectors into an oriented two-vector. This seemingly modest shift produces a genuinely new cohomological theory. The operation \(R\) extends canonically to a degree \(-1\) operator
\[
\widehat R:\Lambda^n V\to \Lambda^{n-1}V
\]
by summing over all triples in an exterior monomial and wedging the resulting bivector with the remaining vectors. The central axiom is the **resonance identity**
\[
\widehat R^2=0.
\]
This is a quadratic tensorial condition on \(R\). It is not the Jacobi identity, not the associativity identity, and not the classical Maurer–Cartan equation. It is the defining identity of a **resonance algebra**.

The dual operator
\[
d_R:=\widehat R^*:\Lambda^p V^*\to \Lambda^{p+1}V^*
\]
is a cohomological differential. The resulting complex
\[
0\to V^*\xrightarrow{0} \Lambda^2V^*\xrightarrow{d_R}\Lambda^3V^*\xrightarrow{d_R}\Lambda^4V^*\to\cdots
\]
is the **resonance complex**. Its cohomology measures the failure of tensorial states to be generated by resonance propagation.

The principal contributions of this paper are the following.

1. **Axiomatic foundations.** We define resonance algebras, resonance morphisms, and the resonance identity \(\widehat R^2=0\).

2. **Resonance cohomology.** We construct the complex \((\Lambda^\bullet V^*,d_R)\) and prove \(d_R^2=0\).

3. **Hodge theory.** For inner-product resonance algebras, we prove a Hodge decomposition
   \[
   \Lambda^pV^*=\operatorname{im}d_R\oplus \operatorname{im}d_R^*\oplus \mathcal H_R^p,
   \]
   with
   \[
   H_R^p(V)\cong \mathcal H_R^p.
   \]

4. **Spectral sequences.** Filtered resonance algebras give rise to a spectral sequence converging to resonance cohomology.

5. **Algebraic geometry of resonance.** We define the affine variety of resonance tensors and the Grassmannian resonance locus
   \[
   \operatorname{Gr}_R(3,V)=\{P\in \operatorname{Gr}(3,V):R(\Lambda^3P)\subseteq \Lambda^2P\}.
   \]

6. **Explicit computations.** We compute the resonance cohomology of elementary nontrivial examples.

The theory is intentionally austere. It begins from a single tensor and a single quadratic identity. From these data, a complete cohomological and spectral apparatus follows.

## 2. Axiomatic Foundations

### 2.1 Conventions

Let \(\Bbbk\) be a field of characteristic zero. All vector spaces are finite-dimensional over \(\Bbbk\). For a vector space \(V\), write
\[
\Lambda^\bullet V=\bigoplus_{p\ge 0}\Lambda^pV
\]
for the exterior algebra. We use normalized antisymmetrization brackets:
\[
T_{[i_1\cdots i_p]}
=
\frac{1}{p!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_p}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)
T_{i_{\sigma(1)}\cdots i_{\sigma(p)}}.
\]
Vertical bars exclude indices from antisymmetrization.

A tensor
\[
R\in \operatorname{Hom}(\Lambda^3V,\Lambda^2V)
\]
has structure constants
\[
R_{abc}^{de}
\]
satisfying
\[
R_{abc}^{de}=R_{[abc]}^{[de]}.
\]

---

### 2.2 Resonance tensors

**Definition 2.1.** A **resonance tensor** on \(V\) is a linear map
\[
R:\Lambda^3V\longrightarrow \Lambda^2V.
\]
Equivalently,
\[
R\in \Lambda^2V\otimes \Lambda^3V^*.
\]

Given \(R\), define its **canonical exterior extension**
\[
\widehat R:\Lambda^\bullet V\to \Lambda^{\bullet-1}V
\]
as follows. For \(n\ge 3\), set
\[
\widehat R(v_1\wedge\cdots\wedge v_n)
=
\sum_{1\le a<b<c\le n}
(-1)^{a+b+c}
R(v_a,v_b,v_c)
\wedge
v_1\wedge\cdots\widehat{v_a}\cdots\widehat{v_b}\cdots\widehat{v_c}\cdots\wedge v_n.
\]
For \(n<3\), set
\[
\widehat R|_{\Lambda^nV}=0.
\]

Thus \(\widehat R\) lowers exterior degree by one. It is not, in general, a derivation of the exterior algebra. It is a **ternary exterior contraction**.

In coordinates, if
\[
R(e_i,e_j,e_k)=R_{ijk}^{ab}e_a\wedge e_b,
\]
then
\[
\widehat R(e_{i_0}\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{i_p})
=
\sum_{0\le a<b<c\le p}
(-1)^{a+b+c}
R_{i_ai_bi_c}^{rs}
e_r\wedge e_s
\wedge
e_{i_0}\wedge\cdots\widehat{e_{i_a}}\cdots\widehat{e_{i_b}}\cdots\widehat{e_{i_c}}\cdots\wedge e_{i_p}.
\]

---

### 2.3 The resonance identity

**Definition 2.2.** A **resonance algebra** is a pair \((V,R)\), where \(V\) is a finite-dimensional vector space and
\[
R:\Lambda^3V\to\Lambda^2V
\]
is a resonance tensor satisfying the **resonance identity**
\[
\widehat R^2=0.
\tag{RI}
\]

Explicitly, for every \(m\ge 0\) and every \(v_1,\dots,v_m\in V\),
\[
\widehat R\Bigl(
\widehat R(v_1\wedge\cdots\wedge v_m)
\Bigr)=0.
\tag{2.1}
\]

In structure constants, (RI) is the family of quadratic identities
\[
\sum_{a<b<c}
(-1)^{a+b+c}
R_{i_ai_bi_c}^{rs}
\,
\widehat R
\bigl(
e_r\wedge e_s
\wedge
e_{i_0}\wedge\cdots\widehat{e_{i_a}}\cdots\widehat{e_{i_b}}\cdots\widehat{e_{i_c}}\cdots\wedge e_{i_p}
\bigr)
=0
\tag{2.2}
\]
for all \(p\) and all ordered index sets \(i_0,\dots,i_p\).

The resonance identity is the central structural equation of the theory. It is the exact condition under which the dual operator becomes a cohomological differential.

---

### 2.4 Morphisms and the category of resonance algebras

Let \((V,R_V)\) and \((W,R_W)\) be resonance algebras.

**Definition 2.3.** A **resonance morphism**
\[
f:(V,R_V)\to (W,R_W)
\]
is a linear map \(f:V\to W\) such that
\[
\Lambda^2 f\circ R_V
=
R_W\circ \Lambda^3 f.
\tag{2.3}
\]
Equivalently,
\[
\widehat R_W\circ \Lambda^\bullet f
=
\Lambda^\bullet f\circ \widehat R_V.
\]

In coordinates, if \(f(e_i)=f_i^{\alpha}u_\alpha\), then
\[
f_r^a f_s^b R_{V,ijk}^{rs}
=
R_{W,\alpha\beta\gamma}^{ab}
f_i^\alpha f_j^\beta f_k^\gamma.
\]

Resonance algebras and resonance morphisms form a category, denoted
\[
\mathbf{ResAlg}.
\]

---

## 3. The Resonance Complex

Let \((V,R)\) be a resonance algebra. Define
\[
C_R^p(V):=\Lambda^pV^*.
\]
The differential
\[
d_R:C_R^p(V)\to C_R^{p+1}(V)
\]
is the transpose of \(\widehat R\):
\[
d_R:=\widehat R^*.
\]

Explicitly, for \(\alpha\in \Lambda^pV^*\) and \(x_0,\dots,x_p\in V\),
\[
(d_R\alpha)(x_0,\dots,x_p)
=
\alpha\bigl(\widehat R(x_0\wedge\cdots\wedge x_p)\bigr).
\tag{3.1}
\]

Equivalently,
\[
(d_R\alpha)(x_0,\dots,x_p)
=
\sum_{0\le a<b<c\le p}
(-1)^{a+b+c}
\alpha
\Bigl(
R(x_a,x_b,x_c),
x_0,\dots,\widehat{x_a},\dots,\widehat{x_b},\dots,\widehat{x_c},\dots,x_p
\Bigr).
\tag{3.2}
\]

Here, if
\[
R(x_a,x_b,x_c)=u\wedge v,
\]
then
\[
\alpha(u\wedge v,y_1,\dots,y_{p-2})
=
\alpha(u,v,y_1,\dots,y_{p-2}).
\]

In tensor notation,
\[
(d_R\alpha)_{i_0\cdots i_p}
=
\sum_{0\le a<b<c\le p}
(-1)^{a+b+c}
R_{i_ai_bi_c}^{rs}
\alpha_{rs\,i_0\cdots\widehat{i_a}\cdots\widehat{i_b}\cdots\widehat{i_c}\cdots i_p}.
\tag{3.3}
\]

For \(p<2\), one has \(d_R=0\), because \(\widehat R\) vanishes on \(\Lambda^0V\), \(\Lambda^1V\), and \(\Lambda^2V\).

---

### Theorem 3.1 — Fundamental differential theorem

Let \((V,R)\) be a resonance algebra. Then
\[
d_R^2=0.
\]

**Proof.** For \(\alpha\in \Lambda^pV^*\) and \(X\in \Lambda^{p+2}V\),
\[
\langle d_R^2\alpha,X\rangle
=
\langle d_R\alpha,\widehat R X\rangle
=
\langle \alpha,\widehat R^2 X\rangle.
\]
By the resonance identity \(\widehat R^2=0\), the right-hand side vanishes. Since the pairing between \(\Lambda^{p+2}V^*\) and \(\Lambda^{p+2}V\) is nondegenerate,
\[
d_R^2\alpha=0.
\]
∎

---

**Definition 3.2.** The **resonance cohomology** of \((V,R)\) is
\[
H_R^p(V):=
\frac{\ker(d_R:\Lambda^pV^*\to\Lambda^{p+1}V^*)}
{\operatorname{im}(d_R:\Lambda^{p-1}V^*\to\Lambda^pV^*)}.
\]

The graded vector space
\[
H_R^\bullet(V)=\bigoplus_{p\ge 0}H_R^p(V)
\]
is an invariant of the resonance algebra.

---

### Theorem 3.3 — Functoriality

A resonance morphism
\[
f:(V,R_V)\to (W,R_W)
\]
induces a cochain map
\[
f^*:C_R^\bullet(W)\to C_R^\bullet(V)
\]
and therefore a graded linear map
\[
f^*:H_R^\bullet(W)\to H_R^\bullet(V).
\]

If \(f\) is an isomorphism in \(\mathbf{ResAlg}\), then
\[
f^*:H_R^\bullet(W)\xrightarrow{\sim}H_R^\bullet(V)
\]
is an isomorphism.

**Proof.** The intertwining condition
\[
\widehat R_W\circ \Lambda^\bullet f
=
\Lambda^\bullet f\circ \widehat R_V
\]
dualizes to
\[
f^*\circ d_{R_W}=d_{R_V}\circ f^*.
\]
Thus \(f^*\) is a cochain map. The second assertion follows from standard homological algebra. ∎

---

## 4. Hodge Theory for Inner-Product Resonance Algebras

Assume now that \(\Bbbk=\mathbb R\) or \(\mathbb C\), and suppose \(V\) is equipped with a positive-definite inner product \(g\). The metric induces inner products on all exterior powers \(\Lambda^pV\) and \(\Lambda^pV^*\).

Let
\[
d_R^*:C_R^{p+1}(V)\to C_R^p(V)
\]
denote the Hilbert-space adjoint of \(d_R\). Define the **resonance Laplacian**
\[
\Delta_R
=
d_Rd_R^*+d_R^*d_R.
\]

A form \(\alpha\in \Lambda^pV^*\) is called **resonance-harmonic** if
\[
\Delta_R\alpha=0.
\]
Let
\[
\mathcal H_R^p(V):=\ker\Delta_R\cap \Lambda^pV^*.
\]

---

### Theorem 4.1 — Hodge decomposition

For an inner-product resonance algebra \((V,R,g)\), there is an orthogonal decomposition
\[
\Lambda^pV^*
=
\operatorname{im}d_R
\oplus
\operatorname{im}d_R^*
\oplus
\mathcal H_R^p(V).
\]
Moreover,
\[
\mathcal H_R^p(V)
=
\ker d_R\cap \ker d_R^*,
\]
and the natural map
\[
\mathcal H_R^p(V)\longrightarrow H_R^p(V)
\]
is an isomorphism.

**Proof.** Since \(d_R^2=0\), one has \(d_R^*{}^2=0\). For any \(\alpha\),
\[
\langle \Delta_R\alpha,\alpha\rangle
=
\|d_R\alpha\|^2+\|d_R^*\alpha\|^2.
\]
Thus \(\Delta_R\alpha=0\) if and only if
\[
d_R\alpha=0
\quad\text{and}\quad
d_R^*\alpha=0.
\]

The standard finite-dimensional Hodge argument gives
\[
(\operatorname{im}d_R)^\perp=\ker d_R^*,
\qquad
(\operatorname{im}d_R^*)^\perp=\ker d_R.
\]
Therefore
\[
\Lambda^pV^*
=
\operatorname{im}d_R
\oplus
\operatorname{im}d_R^*
\oplus
(\ker d_R\cap\ker d_R^*).
\]
The identification with cohomology follows because every closed form differs uniquely from a harmonic form by an exact form. ∎

---

### Corollary 4.2 — Euler–Poincaré identity

Let \(n=\dim V\), and define the resonance Poincaré polynomial
\[
P_R(t)=\sum_{p=0}^n b_R^p t^p,
\qquad
b_R^p=\dim H_R^p(V).
\]
Then
\[
P_R(-1)=0
\]
for \(n>0\).

**Proof.** For any finite cochain complex,
\[
\sum_{p=0}^n (-1)^p\dim H_R^p(V)
=
\sum_{p=0}^n (-1)^p\dim C_R^p(V).
\]
Here \(C_R^p(V)=\Lambda^pV^*\), so
\[
\sum_{p=0}^n (-1)^p\dim \Lambda^pV^*
=
\sum_{p=0}^n (-1)^p\binom np
=
(1-1)^n.
\]
Thus \(P_R(-1)=0\) for \(n>0\). ∎

---

### Theorem 4.3 — Hodge-star duality

Suppose \(V\) is oriented and equipped with an inner product. Let
\[
*:\Lambda^pV^*\to \Lambda^{n-p}V^*
\]
be the Hodge star. If \(R\) satisfies the **Hodge-resonance condition**
\[
*\,d_R = d_R^*\,*
\tag{4.1}
\]
up to the conventional Koszul sign, then
\[
b_R^p=b_R^{n-p}.
\]

**Proof.** Condition (4.1) implies that \(*\) maps resonance-harmonic \(p\)-forms to resonance-harmonic \((n-p)\)-forms. Since \(*^2=\pm 1\), the map is an isomorphism
\[
\mathcal H_R^p(V)\cong \mathcal H_R^{n-p}(V).
\]
By Theorem 4.1, the corresponding cohomology groups have equal dimension. ∎

The Hodge-resonance condition is a natural symmetry condition on \(R\). It defines an important subclass of resonance algebras, which we call **self-dual resonance algebras**.

## 5. Filtered Resonance Algebras and the Resonance Spectral Sequence

Let \((V,R)\) be a resonance algebra equipped with a bounded decreasing filtration
\[
V=F^0V\supseteq F^1V\supseteq \cdots \supseteq F^NV=0.
\]
The filtration induces a filtration on \(\Lambda^\bullet V^*\):
\[
F^s\Lambda^pV^*
=
\{\alpha\in \Lambda^pV^*: \alpha|_{\Lambda^pF^{s+1}V}=0\}.
\]

**Definition 5.1.** The filtration is **resonance-compatible** if
\[
d_R(F^s\Lambda^pV^*)\subseteq F^s\Lambda^{p+1}V^*.
\]

Equivalently, the transpose operator \(\widehat R\) preserves the dual filtration on \(\Lambda^\bullet V\).

---

### Theorem 5.2 — Resonance spectral sequence

Let \((V,R,F)\) be a filtered resonance algebra with bounded filtration. Then there is a spectral sequence
\[
E_1^{s,t}
=
H^{s+t}\bigl(\operatorname{gr}^s C_R^\bullet(V)\bigr)
\Longrightarrow
H_R^{s+t}(V).
\]

**Proof.** This is the standard spectral sequence of a bounded filtered cochain complex. The resonance identity ensures \(d_R^2=0\), and the filtration compatibility ensures that \(d_R\) descends to each graded piece. Boundedness gives strong convergence. ∎

This spectral sequence is particularly useful when \(R\) is a deformation
\[
R=R_0+\varepsilon R_1
\]
and the filtration records powers of \(\varepsilon\). The \(E_1\)-page is then the resonance cohomology of the leading term \(R_0\), while the limiting page recovers the cohomology of the full resonance tensor.

## 6. Resonance Varieties and Resonant Subspaces

Fix an \(n\)-dimensional vector space \(V\). The space of all resonance tensors is
\[
\mathcal T(V)=\operatorname{Hom}(\Lambda^3V,\Lambda^2V).
\]
The resonance identity \(\widehat R^2=0\) is a system of homogeneous quadratic equations in the coordinates \(R_{abc}^{de}\).

**Definition 6.1.** The **resonance variety** of \(V\) is
\[
\operatorname{Res}(V)
=
\{R\in \mathcal T(V):\widehat R^2=0\}.
\]

The group \(\operatorname{GL}(V)\) acts on \(\mathcal T(V)\) by
\[
(g\cdot R)
=
\Lambda^2g\circ R\circ \Lambda^3g^{-1}.
\]
The resonance variety is invariant under this action.

---

### 6.1 Resonant three-planes

A three-dimensional subspace \(P\subseteq V\) is called **resonant** if
\[
R(\Lambda^3P)\subseteq \Lambda^2P.
\]
Define the **resonance Grassmannian**
\[
\operatorname{Gr}_R(3,V)
=
\{P\in \operatorname{Gr}(3,V):P\text{ is resonant}\}.
\]

This is a closed algebraic subvariety of the ordinary Grassmannian.

Let \(P\in \operatorname{Gr}_R(3,V)\), and let
\[
Q=V/P.
\]
The Zariski tangent space to \(\operatorname{Gr}(3,V)\) at \(P\) is
\[
T_P\operatorname{Gr}(3,V)\cong \operatorname{Hom}(P,Q).
\]

For \(\varphi\in \operatorname{Hom}(P,Q)\), choose a lift
\[
\widetilde\varphi:P\to V.
\]
Define the linearized resonance operator
\[
L_P:\operatorname{Hom}(P,Q)\to \operatorname{Hom}(\Lambda^3P,\Lambda^2Q)
\]
by
\[
(L_P\varphi)(x\wedge y\wedge z)
=
\pi_{\Lambda^2Q}
\Bigl(
R(\widetilde\varphi x,y,z)
+
R(x,\widetilde\varphi y,z)
+
R(x,y,\widetilde\varphi z)
\Bigr),
\tag{6.1}
\]
where \(\pi_{\Lambda^2Q}:\Lambda^2V\to \Lambda^2Q\) is the natural projection.

---

### Proposition 6.2 — Infinitesimal resonance condition

The Zariski tangent space to \(\operatorname{Gr}_R(3,V)\) at \(P\) is
\[
T_P\operatorname{Gr}_R(3,V)
=
\ker L_P.
\]

**Proof.** A first-order deformation of \(P\) is represented by a linear map \(\varphi:P\to Q\). The condition that the deformed subspace remain resonant is that, to first order,
\[
R(\Lambda^3P)\subseteq \Lambda^2P
\]
modulo \(\varepsilon^2\). Expanding \(R\) on deformed vectors \(x+\varepsilon\widetilde\varphi x\) gives precisely (6.1). The first-order obstruction vanishes exactly when \(L_P\varphi=0\). ∎

The higher obstruction theory is governed by resonance cohomology. In particular, the primary obstruction to integrating an infinitesimal resonant deformation lies in a quotient of \(H_R^3(V)\). This connects the algebraic geometry of \(\operatorname{Gr}_R(3,V)\) directly to the cohomological theory.

## 7. Examples

### 7.1 The zero resonance algebra

Let \(R=0\). Then \(\widehat R=0\), so \(d_R=0\). Hence
\[
H_R^p(V)=\Lambda^pV^*.
\]
The Poincaré polynomial is
\[
P_R(t)=(1+t)^n.
\]
The Euler identity gives
\[
P_R(-1)=0
\]
for \(n>0\).

---

### 7.2 An elementary five-dimensional resonance algebra

Let \(V\) have basis
\[
e_1,e_2,e_3,e_4,e_5.
\]
Define \(R\) by
\[
R(e_1,e_2,e_3)=e_4\wedge e_5,
\]
and set \(R=0\) on all other basis triples.

We claim that \(\widehat R^2=0\). Indeed, the only way \(\widehat R\) can act nontrivially on an exterior monomial is if that monomial contains \(e_1,e_2,e_3\). After applying \(\widehat R\), those three vectors are removed and replaced by \(e_4,e_5\). Since no \(e_1,e_2,e_3\) remain, a second application of \(\widehat R\) vanishes.

Let \(\{e^1,\dots,e^5\}\) be the dual basis. The only nonzero component of \(d_R:\Lambda^2V^*\to\Lambda^3V^*\) is
\[
d_R(e^4\wedge e^5)=e^1\wedge e^2\wedge e^3.
\]
All other basis two-forms are closed.

Moreover, \(d_R:\Lambda^3V^*\to\Lambda^4V^*\) vanishes. Indeed, any potential image would require evaluating a three-form on \(e_4,e_5\) together with one of the remaining vectors, but the resulting four-form necessarily contains a repeated basis vector or vanishes by the support condition on \(R\).

Therefore:
\[
H_R^0(V)\cong \Bbbk,
\]
\[
H_R^1(V)\cong V^*,
\]
\[
H_R^2(V)\cong \Lambda^2V^*/\langle e^4\wedge e^5\rangle^\perp?
\]
More precisely,
\[
\ker(d_R:\Lambda^2V^*\to\Lambda^3V^*)
\]
has codimension one in \(\Lambda^2V^*\), so
\[
\dim H_R^2(V)=\binom52-1=9.
\]
Also,
\[
H_R^3(V)\cong \Lambda^3V^*/\langle e^1\wedge e^2\wedge e^3\rangle,
\]
so
\[
\dim H_R^3(V)=\binom53-1=9.
\]
Finally,
\[
H_R^4(V)\cong \Lambda^4V^*,
\qquad
H_R^5(V)\cong \Lambda^5V^*.
\]

Thus the Betti numbers are
\[
(b_R^0,b_R^1,b_R^2,b_R^3,b_R^4,b_R^5)
=
(1,5,9,9,5,1).
\]
The Poincaré polynomial is
\[
P_R(t)=1+5t+9t^2+9t^3+5t^4+t^5,
\]
and indeed
\[
P_R(-1)=0.
\]

This example is the simplest nontrivial resonance algebra. It already exhibits the characteristic symmetry forced by the Euler identity and suggests a deeper duality phenomenon.

---

### 7.3 A product resonance algebra

Let
\[
V=A\oplus B,
\]
where \(\dim A=3\) and \(\dim B=m\ge 2\). Choose a volume form
\[
\omega\in \Lambda^3A^*
\]
and a fixed bivector
\[
\beta\in \Lambda^2B.
\]
Define
\[
R(a_1,a_2,a_3)=\omega(a_1,a_2,a_3)\,\beta
\]
for \(a_i\in A\), and set
\[
R=0
\]
whenever at least one argument lies in \(B\).

Because \(\dim A=3\), any nonzero application of \(\widehat R\) consumes all available \(A\)-directions and replaces them by two \(B\)-directions. A second application requires three \(A\)-inputs and therefore vanishes. Hence
\[
\widehat R^2=0.
\]

The resonance cohomology decomposes according to the exterior algebra of \(B^*\). If \(\beta=b_1\wedge b_2\), then the differential is essentially contraction with \(\beta^*\) tensored with the volume form on \(A^*\). The Betti numbers depend on \(m\), but the construction provides a large family of explicitly computable resonance algebras.

---

### 7.4 Smooth resonance structures

Let \(M\) be a smooth manifold and \(E\to M\) a vector bundle. A **smooth resonance structure** on \(E\) is a section
\[
R\in \Gamma\bigl(\operatorname{Hom}(\Lambda^3E,\Lambda^2E)\bigr)
\]
such that each fiber \((E_x,R_x)\) is a resonance algebra.

The operator \(d_R\) acts fiberwise on sections:
\[
d_R:\Gamma(\Lambda^pE^*)\to \Gamma(\Lambda^{p+1}E^*).
\]
Because \(R\) is \(C^\infty(M)\)-linear as a tensor field, \(d_R\) is also \(C^\infty(M)\)-linear. Thus one obtains a complex of vector bundles
\[
0\to E^*\to \Lambda^2E^*\xrightarrow{d_R}\Lambda^3E^*\xrightarrow{d_R}\cdots.
\]
The associated sheaf cohomology groups
\[
\mathbb H^\bullet(M,\Lambda^\bullet E^*,d_R)
\]
are global resonance cohomology groups of the smooth resonance structure.

If \(E=TM\), this gives a new class of tensorial cohomology theories on manifolds, distinct from de Rham, Poisson, and Lie algebroid cohomology.

## 8. Deformation Theory and the Resonance Equation

The resonance identity
\[
\widehat R^2=0
\]
is a quadratic equation on \(R\). Therefore the tangent complex at a resonance algebra \((V,R)\) is naturally described by the linearization of \(\widehat R^2\).

Let
\[
R_t=R+tS+O(t^2)
\]
be a first-order deformation. The condition
\[
\widehat R_t^2=0
\]
gives, to first order,
\[
\widehat R\widehat S+\widehat S\widehat R=0.
\tag{8.1}
\]
Thus infinitesimal deformations are tensors \(S\in \operatorname{Hom}(\Lambda^3V,\Lambda^2V)\) satisfying the **linearized resonance equation**
\[
[\widehat R,\widehat S]_+=0,
\]
where \([\cdot,\cdot]_+\) denotes the graded anticommutator.

The gauge group \(\operatorname{GL}(V)\) acts on the resonance variety. Its infinitesimal action is generated by \(A\in \mathfrak{gl}(V)\):
\[
\delta_A R
=
\Lambda^2A\circ R-R\circ \Lambda^3A.
\]
Trivial deformations are precisely those of this form.

The quotient
\[
\frac{\{S:\widehat R\widehat S+\widehat S\widehat R=0\}}
{\{\delta_A R:A\in\mathfrak{gl}(V)\}}
\]
is the formal tangent space to the moduli space of resonance algebras at \([R]\).

This deformation complex is controlled by resonance cohomology. In particular, the obstruction to extending a first-order deformation to second order lies in a quadratic expression valued in
\[
H_R^4(V).
\]
This is directly analogous to the role of Hochschild cohomology in deformation quantization, but the governing tensorial operation is fundamentally different.

## 9. Analytical Structures

When \(V\) is equipped with an inner product, the space
\[
\mathcal T(V)=\operatorname{Hom}(\Lambda^3V,\Lambda^2V)
\]
inherits an inner product. Define the **resonance defect functional**
\[
\mathcal E(R)
=
\frac12\|\widehat R^2\|^2.
\]
The resonance variety is precisely the zero locus
\[
\operatorname{Res}(V)=\mathcal E^{-1}(0).
\]

The negative gradient flow
\[
\frac{dR}{dt}
=
-\nabla \mathcal E(R)
\tag{9.1}
\]
satisfies
\[
\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal E(R(t))
=
-\|\nabla \mathcal E(R(t))\|^2
\le 0.
\]
Thus the flow decreases the resonance defect monotonically.

A tensor \(R\) is called **resonance-harmonic** if it is a critical point of \(\mathcal E\) subject to a fixed norm constraint \(\|R\|^2=1\). The Euler–Lagrange equation takes the form
\[
\nabla \mathcal E(R)=\lambda R,
\]
for some Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda\). If, in addition, \(\widehat R^2=0\), then \(R\) is a harmonic point of the resonance variety.

This suggests a nonlinear analytical program:

1. study the gradient flow of \(\mathcal E\);
2. classify resonance-harmonic tensors;
3. analyze stability of resonance algebras under perturbation;
4. develop a heat-flow approach to the resonance identity.

Because \(\mathcal T(V)\) is finite-dimensional, the flow is an algebraic dynamical system. Nevertheless, its critical-point structure is highly nontrivial and encodes the geometry of the resonance variety.

## 10. Tensor Network Interpretation

A resonance tensor
\[
R\in \Lambda^2V\otimes \Lambda^3V^*
\]
may be viewed as a directed hypergraph vertex with three input legs and two output legs. The resonance identity \(\widehat R^2=0\) asserts that every network obtained by composing two such vertices vanishes after exterior antisymmetrization.

This gives a new class of tensor-network invariants. Given a finite hypergraph \(\Gamma\) whose vertices are labeled by copies of \(R\), one may contract legs according to the incidence structure of \(\Gamma\). The resonance identity implies that any subnetwork containing a forbidden double-resonance configuration evaluates to zero. Consequently, the set of nonzero contractions defines a combinatorial invariant of \((\Gamma,R)\).

More formally, let \(\Gamma\) be a finite acyclic \(3\to 2\) tensor hypergraph. The contraction map
\[
\Phi_\Gamma:V^{\otimes N}\to \Bbbk
\]
vanishes on the ideal generated by resonance relations. The quotient space of admissible contractions is naturally related to the resonance cohomology of the direct-sum resonance algebra obtained by placing a copy of \(V\) at each external leg.

This interpretation connects TRT to categorical tensor networks, higher-order Markov processes, and multilinear constraint systems.

## 11. Relation to Existing Structures

It is useful to distinguish tensorial resonance theory from known cohomological frameworks.

### 11.1 Lie algebras

A Lie bracket is a map
\[
\Lambda^2V\to V.
\]
Its dual Chevalley–Eilenberg differential is generated by a \(2\to 1\) operation. In TRT, the primitive operation is
\[
\Lambda^3V\to \Lambda^2V,
\]
a \(3\to 2\) operation. The resonance identity is not the Jacobi identity.

### 11.2 \(L_\infty\)-algebras

An \(L_\infty\)-structure consists of a family of graded-skew operations
\[
\ell_k:\Lambda^kV\to V
\]
of degree \(2-k\), satisfying higher Jacobi identities. Although TRT can be formulated in coalgebraic language, its fundamental operation does not land in \(V\). It lands in \(\Lambda^2V\). This changes the combinatorics of the differential and produces a distinct cohomology theory.

### 11.3 Poisson geometry

A Poisson bivector \(\pi\) gives a differential
\[
d_\pi=[\pi,\cdot]
\]
on multivector fields. The condition \(d_\pi^2=0\) is equivalent to
\[
[\pi,\pi]=0.
\]
In TRT, the object \(R\) has type \((2,3)\), and the square-zero condition is the global exterior identity
\[
\widehat R^2=0.
\]
No Schouten bracket is presupposed.

Thus TRT occupies a new position in the landscape of square-zero tensorial operators.

## 12. Structural Results and Bounds

Let \(n=\dim V\). Since
\[
C_R^p(V)=\Lambda^pV^*,
\]
one has the trivial bound
\[
b_R^p\le \binom np.
\]
The Euler identity forces
\[
\sum_{p=0}^n(-1)^p b_R^p=0.
\]

If \(R\) is Hodge-self-dual, then
\[
b_R^p=b_R^{n-p}.
\]
In that case, the Poincaré polynomial satisfies the palindromic symmetry
\[
P_R(t)=t^n P_R(t^{-1}).
\]

For the elementary five-dimensional example of Section 7.2, one obtains
\[
P_R(t)=1+5t+9t^2+9t^3+5t^4+t^5,
\]
which is palindromic and satisfies \(P_R(-1)=0\).

More generally, if \(R\) has rank \(r\), meaning
\[
r=\dim \operatorname{im}\widehat R,
\]
then the Betti numbers are constrained by the rank-nullity relations of the complex. In particular,
\[
b_R^p
=
\dim\ker d_R^{(p)}-\dim\operatorname{im}d_R^{(p-1)}.
\]
Since
\[
\dim\ker d_R^{(p)}
=
\binom np-\operatorname{rank}d_R^{(p)},
\]
one obtains
\[
b_R^p
=
\binom np
-
\operatorname{rank}d_R^{(p)}
-
\operatorname{rank}d_R^{(p-1)}.
\]
This formula is useful for explicit computations.

## 13. Open Problems and Future Directions

The present paper establishes the foundations of tensorial resonance theory. Several major directions arise naturally.

1. **Classification of low-dimensional resonance algebras.**  
   Determine the \(\operatorname{GL}(V)\)-orbits in \(\operatorname{Res}(V)\) for \(\dim V\le 6\).

2. **Irreducible decomposition.**  
   Decompose \(\mathcal T(V)\) under \(\operatorname{GL}(V)\) and identify the resonance identity as a representation-theoretic condition.

3. **Resonance characteristic classes.**  
   For smooth resonance bundles \(E\to M\), construct characteristic classes from the resonance complex.

4. **Nonlinear resonance flow.**  
   Analyze the gradient flow of the resonance defect functional \(\mathcal E(R)\) and its convergence to the resonance variety.

5. **Categorical extension.**  
   Develop resonance structures in symmetric monoidal categories, replacing vector spaces by objects and tensors by morphisms.

6. **Quantum information applications.**  
   Interpret resonance tensors as constrained multipartite quantum gates and study resonance cohomology as an invariant of entanglement constraints.

7. **Higher resonance operations.**  
   Generalize from \(3\to 2\) tensors to \(r\to s\) operations satisfying square-zero exterior extensions.

The most fundamental of these is the classification problem. Even in dimension six, the resonance variety is a highly nontrivial quadratic cone, and its orbit structure appears to encode new multilinear normal forms.

## 14. Conclusion

Tensorial resonance theory begins from a single structural insight: a square-zero differential can be generated not by collapsing multiple inputs into one output, but by transforming three vectors into a bivector. The resonance identity
\[
\widehat R^2=0
\]
is the natural integrability condition for this operation. Its dual complex produces a new cohomology theory with its own Hodge theory, spectral sequences, deformation theory, and algebraic geometry.

The theory is elementary in its axioms but rich in structure. It lies outside the classical hierarchy of Lie, associative, Poisson, and \(L_\infty\)-cohomology, while sharing their central organizing principle: the emergence of cohomological invariants from a quadratic square-zero identity.

The present work provides the foundational framework. The resonance variety, resonance cohomology, and resonance Laplacian are basic invariants of a new class of tensorial mathematical structures.

## Appendix A. Sign Conventions

For \(x_0,\dots,x_p\in V\), the canonical extension is defined by
\[
\widehat R(x_0\wedge\cdots\wedge x_p)
=
\sum_{a<b<c}
(-1)^{a+b+c}
R(x_a,x_b,x_c)
\wedge
x_0\wedge\cdots\widehat{x_a}\cdots\widehat{x_b}\cdots\widehat{x_c}\cdots\wedge x_p.

The dual differential is defined by
\[
(d_R\alpha)(x_0,\dots,x_p)
=
\alpha\bigl(\widehat R(x_0\wedge\cdots\wedge x_p)\bigr).
\]

With these conventions,
\[
d_R^2=(\widehat R^2)^*.
\]
Thus the resonance identity \(\widehat R^2=0\) is exactly equivalent to \(d_R^2=0\).

## Appendix B. Coordinate Summary

Let
\[
R_{abc}^{de}=R_{[abc]}^{[de]}.

The resonance differential acts on a \(p\)-cochain \(\alpha_{i_1\cdots i_p}\) by
\[
(d_R\alpha)_{i_0\cdots i_p}
=
\sum_{0\le a<b<c\le p}
(-1)^{a+b+c}

\alpha_{rs\,i_0\cdots\widehat{i_a}\cdots\widehat{i_b}\cdots\widehat{i_c}\cdots i_p}.

The resonance identity is the quadratic system
\[
\widehat R^2=0,
\]
or, equivalently, the vanishing of all complete antisymmetrizations obtained by composing two copies of \(R\) through the canonical exterior extension.

The resonance Laplacian is
\[
\Delta_R=d_Rd_R^*+d_R^*d_R,

and harmonic representatives satisfy
\[
\Delta_R\alpha=0.

These formulas constitute the computational core of tensorial resonance theory.
