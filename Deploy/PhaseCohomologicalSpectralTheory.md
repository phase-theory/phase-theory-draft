## Abstract

We introduce **Phase-Cohomological Spectral Theory (PCST)**, a new foundational theory in which physical dimension, distance, gauge structure, and particle identity emerge from the cohomology and spectrum of an adaptive phase complex. PCST differs from ordinary phase-field constructions by treating the primitive datum as a phase-valued cochain on a relational complex whose incidence structure is itself selected by phase compatibility. No manifold, metric, causal order, connection, or particle species is assumed at the starting point.

The primitive object is

\[
\Phi\in C^1(\mathcal K,U(1)),
\]

where \(\mathcal K\) is an abstract locally finite relational complex and \(C^1(\mathcal K,U(1))\) is its space of phase one-cochains. Phase compatibility defines an adaptive incidence operator \(\mathbf B_\Phi\), from which a phase Laplacian \(\boldsymbol\Delta_\Phi\), a heat kernel, and a spectral dimension are derived. The theory is governed by the Universal Phase Equation

\[
\mathscr F[\Phi;\lambda]=0,
\]

with stability operator \(\mathscr L_\Phi=D_\Phi\mathscr F\), bifurcation operator \(\Delta_\Phi=\operatorname{Det}_\Phi(\mathscr L_\Phi)\), and phase susceptibility \(\boldsymbol\chi_\Phi=\mathscr L_\Phi^{-1}\) on the normal slice.

The central PCST claim is that **geometry is the infrared organization of phase cohomology**. The emergent distance is a spectral distance of \(\boldsymbol\Delta_\Phi\), dimension is the logarithmic scaling exponent of its heat trace, gauge structure is the cohomological freedom of phase representatives, and particles are stable localized defects carrying nontrivial relative cohomology and isolated Laplacian eigenvalues. The generative hierarchy is

\[
\boxed{
\Phi
\rightarrow
\text{adaptive complex}
\rightarrow
\text{cohomology}
\rightarrow
\text{phase Laplacian}
\rightarrow
\text{spectral dimension and distance}
\rightarrow
\text{connections}
\rightarrow
\text{defect sectors}
\rightarrow
\text{particles and observables}.
}
\]

We formulate the PCST action, derive the universal stability and bifurcation structure, perform Lyapunov–Schmidt reduction, and prove propositions establishing the persistence of cohomological sectors and the discreteness of defect excitations under spectral isolation. A minimal realization predicts a scale-dependent spectral dimension, a universal logarithmic correction to the long-distance inverse-square potential, and a relation between the mass gap of a phase defect and the first nontrivial cohomological eigenvalue. The theory is falsifiable: it fails if no stable adaptive complex supports a finite-dimensional infrared spectral geometry, if cohomological sectors do not produce isolated localized modes, or if the predicted correlations between dimensional flow, force-law corrections, and defect spectroscopy are absent.

**Keywords:** phase cohomology, adaptive complexes, spectral geometry, emergent dimension, phase Laplacian, topological defects, Lyapunov–Schmidt reduction, gauge redundancy, particle sectors, universal phase theory.

## 1. Introduction

A physical theory normally begins by specifying the arena on which events occur. General relativity begins with a differentiable manifold and metric; gauge theory begins with a principal bundle and connection; quantum field theory begins with fields over spacetime; condensed-matter theory begins with a lattice, crystal, or continuum. PCST reverses this order in a way distinct from the memory-based construction of causal phase theories. It asks whether the arena itself can be generated by the compatibility pattern of phase relations.

The central thesis is:

> **A physical dimension is the stable infrared rank of phase cohomology as measured by the spectrum of its derived Laplacian.**

The phrase “phase” here does not mean a scalar angle assigned to a point of a pre-existing manifold. It denotes a relational phase assignment to oriented links of an abstract complex. The links are not spatial intervals. They are admissible comparisons between phase-bearing relational units. A link becomes physically effective only when the phase mismatch across it is compatible with the universal phase equation.

PCST therefore introduces a new ontological sequence. The primitive object is the phase cochain \(\Phi\), while the complex, topology, geometry, connection, and matter spectrum are derived. The key distinction from a conventional lattice theory is that the complex is adaptive and carries no primitive lengths, angles, dimension, or embedding. The key distinction from a conventional topological field theory is that cohomology is not placed on a background space; the incidence data supporting cohomology are themselves selected by phase stability.

The framework has four objectives. First, it must construct an explicit universal phase equation whose coefficients do not secretly encode a metric or known gauge group. Second, it must derive a spectral operator with a stable infrared heat-kernel scaling. Third, it must show how cohomological defects produce localized, transportable, and spectrally discrete phase sectors. Fourth, it must produce quantitative observables that distinguish PCST from theories in which dimension, geometry, and particle content are independently stipulated.

The formal structure is summarized by

\[
\Phi
\longrightarrow
\mathscr F[\Phi]=0
\longrightarrow
\mathscr L_\Phi
\longrightarrow
\Delta_\Phi
\longrightarrow
\boldsymbol\chi_\Phi
\longrightarrow
\mathcal K_\Phi
\longrightarrow
\boldsymbol\Delta_\Phi
\longrightarrow
\operatorname{Spec}(\boldsymbol\Delta_\Phi)
\longrightarrow
\mathcal O.
\]

The first \(\Delta_\Phi\) denotes the bifurcation determinant of the phase stability operator; the bold \(\boldsymbol\Delta_\Phi\) denotes the derived cohomological Laplacian. Their distinction is essential. The former locates structural transitions in phase configuration space. The latter measures the propagation and cohomology of a stable phase sector.

### 1.1 Relation to established mathematics

PCST uses simplicial cochains, Hodge-type Laplacians, spectral asymptotics, and Fredholm bifurcation theory. These mathematical constructions are established tools in geometry and topology [1] [2], while the interpretation that they emerge from adaptive phase compatibility is the new content of this paper. Spectral dimension has appeared in several approaches to quantum gravity and random geometry [3], but PCST does not assume a fluctuating metric or a pre-existing continuum. Its spectral dimension is generated by the phase-selected incidence operator.

### 1.2 Derivation versus insertion

The theory is designed to expose, rather than conceal, its derivational burden.

## 2. Primitive phase complexes

### 2.1 Abstract relational complexes

Let \(\mathcal V\) be a countable collection of phase-bearing units and let \(\mathcal E_0\subset\mathcal V\times\mathcal V\) be a maximal set of formally admissible comparisons. The pair \(\mathcal R_0=(\mathcal V,\mathcal E_0)\) is a relational graph, not a space. From it one constructs an abstract oriented simplicial or cellular complex \(\mathcal K_0\), but the physical complex is a phase-selected subcomplex

\[
\mathcal K_\Phi\subseteq\mathcal K_0.
\]

The initial complex carries only incidence signs. It has no metric tensor, edge length, embedding, or dimension.

Let \(C^p(\mathcal K,U(1))\) denote the group of \(U(1)\)-valued cellular \(p\)-cochains. The primitive phase is a one-cochain

\[
\boxed{
\Phi\in C^1(\mathcal K_0,U(1)),
\qquad
\Phi(e)=e^{i\theta_e}.
}
\tag{2.1}
\]

A phase-frame transformation is a zero-cochain \(g\in C^0(\mathcal K_0,U(1))\) acting by

\[
\Phi(e_{vw})
\mapsto
g(v)\Phi(e_{vw})g(w)^{-1}.
\tag{2.2}
\]

The absolute phase at a relational unit is not observable. Only invariant cochain combinations may survive.

### 2.2 Phase compatibility and adaptive incidence

For an oriented edge \(e\), define the phase mismatch

\[
\delta_\Phi(e)=\operatorname{Arg}\Phi(e)\in(-\pi,\pi].
\]

For a candidate two-cell \(\sigma=(e_1,e_2,e_3)\), define its closure mismatch

\[
\Omega_\Phi(\sigma)
=\operatorname{Arg}\bigl(\Phi(e_1)\Phi(e_2)\Phi(e_3)\bigr).
\tag{2.3}
\]

A simplex is phase-compatible when its closure mismatch and the local edge mismatches satisfy a stability inequality. Introduce a smooth compatibility kernel

\[
W_\Phi(\sigma)
=\exp\left[-\frac{\Omega_\Phi(\sigma)^2}{2\sigma_\Phi^2}
-\frac{1}{2\rho_\Phi^2}\sum_{e\subset\sigma}\delta_\Phi(e)^2\right].
\tag{2.4}
\]

The phase-selected incidence coefficient is

\[
(B_\Phi)_{\sigma e}
=\epsilon(\sigma,e)W_\Phi(\sigma),
\tag{2.5}
\]

where \(\epsilon(\sigma,e)\) is the orientation incidence sign. Equation (2.5) is not a numerical lattice coupling inserted by hand; it is a functional of the phase configuration. The adaptive complex consists of cells whose weights exceed the structural threshold determined by the stable branch:

\[
\mathcal K_\Phi
=\left\{\sigma\in\mathcal K_0:
W_\Phi(\sigma)>W_c[\Phi]\right\}.
\tag{2.6}
\]

The threshold is itself defined by the phase bifurcation condition, so the theory does not assume a fixed connectivity scale.

### 2.3 Phase cohomology

The weighted coboundary is

\[
d_\Phi:C^p(\mathcal K_\Phi)\to C^{p+1}(\mathcal K_\Phi),
\qquad
(d_\Phi c)(\sigma)=\sum_{e\subset\sigma}(B_\Phi)_{\sigma e}c(e).
\tag{2.7}
\]

Because adaptive weights need not satisfy exact simplicial identities away from a stable phase sector, the condition

\[
d_\Phi^2=0
\tag{2.8}
\]

is itself a phase admissibility condition. A failure of Eq. (2.8) indicates that the candidate complex has not stabilized into a coherent cohomological sector. On an admissible sector, define

\[
H^p_\Phi(\mathcal K_\Phi)
=\frac{\ker d_\Phi:C^p\to C^{p+1}}
{\operatorname{im}d_\Phi:C^{p-1}\to C^p}.
\tag{2.9}
\]

This is the first point at which topology appears. It is not imposed through a background manifold; it is the quotient of phase-compatible closed relations by phase-frame exact relations.

> **Definition 1 (Phase-cohomological sector).** A phase-cohomological sector is a connected stable component of the solution set of \(\mathscr F[\Phi]=0\) on which \(d_\Phi^2=0\), the Betti data of \(H^p_\Phi\) are constant, and the derived Laplacian has a stable infrared spectrum.

## 3. Universal equation, stability, and bifurcation

### 3.1 Universal phase functional

The phase action is defined on the maximal relational complex by

\[
S_\Phi[\Phi]
=\frac12\langle d_\Phi\Phi,d_\Phi\Phi\rangle_\Phi
+\frac{\kappa}{2}\langle \delta_\Phi\Phi,\delta_\Phi\Phi\rangle_\Phi
+V_\Phi[\Phi]
+\tau\,\mathcal I_\Phi[\Phi].
\tag{3.1}
\]

Here \(\delta_\Phi\) is the phase-adjoint coboundary induced by the phase pairing, \(V_\Phi\) is a local phase potential, and \(\mathcal I_\Phi\) is a cohomological invariant functional. The inner product is constructed from phase-compatible weights and contains no background metric.

The universal phase equation is

\[
\boxed{
\mathscr F[\Phi;\lambda]
:=\frac{\delta S_\Phi}{\delta\Phi}=0.
}
\tag{3.2}
\]

The controls \(\lambda\) may include boundary cochains, total phase charge, coarse-graining scale, or a phase-compatibility threshold. They do not include a preassigned spatial dimension or spacetime metric.

### 3.2 Phase stability operator

For \(\Phi_\star\) satisfying Eq. (3.2), let \(\zeta\) be a tangent cochain. Then

\[
\mathscr F[\Phi_\star+\varepsilon\zeta]
=\varepsilon\mathscr L_{\Phi_\star}\zeta+O(\varepsilon^2),
\qquad
\boxed{\mathscr L_\Phi=D_\Phi\mathscr F[\Phi].}
\tag{3.3}
\]

The phase bifurcation operator is

\[
\boxed{\Delta_\Phi
=\operatorname{Det}_\Phi(\mathscr L_\Phi).}
\tag{3.4}
\]

Its zero set identifies loss of local structural invertibility. The susceptibility is the inverse on the normal complement to phase-frame and collective directions:

\[
\boxed{\boldsymbol\chi_\Phi
=\left(\Pi_N\mathscr L_\Phi\Pi_N\right)^{-1}.}
\tag{3.5}
\]

The susceptibility determines how much phase deformation is required to produce a structural response. It also enters the normalization of the cochain Laplacian.

### 3.3 Cohomological Laplacian

Define

\[
\boxed{
\boldsymbol\Delta_\Phi^{(p)}
=d_\Phi\delta_\Phi+\delta_\Phi d_\Phi
:C^p(\mathcal K_\Phi)\to C^p(\mathcal K_\Phi).
}
\tag{3.6}
\]

The phase susceptibility modifies the cochain pairing and hence the adjoint \(\delta_\Phi\). In a local basis,

\[
\langle c_1,c_2\rangle_\Phi
=\langle c_1,\boldsymbol\chi_\Phi c_2\rangle_0.
\tag{3.7}
\]

Consequently, \(\boldsymbol\Delta_\Phi\) is not merely the combinatorial Laplacian of a graph. It is the response-weighted Laplacian of a stable phase sector.

The harmonic cochains satisfy

\[
\mathcal H^p_\Phi
=\ker\boldsymbol\Delta_\Phi^{(p)}
\cong H^p_\Phi(\mathcal K_\Phi)
\tag{3.8}
\]

under the finite-dimensional Hodge hypotheses. Harmonic phase relations are therefore the canonical representatives of topological sectors.

### 3.4 Lyapunov–Schmidt reduction

At a critical configuration \(\Phi_c\) with \(\Delta_{\Phi_c}=0\), let

\[
K_c=\ker\mathscr L_{\Phi_c},
\qquad
T_{\Phi_c}\mathcal C_\Phi=K_c\oplus R_c.
\]

Write

\[
\Phi=\Phi_c+\eta^ae_a+\xi,
\qquad
\xi\in R_c.
\tag{3.9}
\]

The range equation determines \(\xi=\xi(\eta,\lambda)\), and the reduced equation is

\[
\varphi^a(\eta,\lambda)
=\left\langle e^{*a},
\mathscr F[\Phi_c+\eta^be_b+\xi(\eta,\lambda);\lambda]
\right\rangle=0.
\tag{3.10}
\]

The reduced cochain Laplacian is

\[
\boldsymbol\Delta_{\rm red}(\eta,\lambda)
=\boldsymbol\Delta_{\Phi_c+\eta e+\xi(\eta,\lambda)}.
\tag{3.11}
\]

The bifurcation creates not only an order-parameter branch but also a candidate adaptive complex and its cohomology.

> **Proposition 1 (Cohomology persistence near a regular phase branch).** Suppose \(\mathscr F\) is \(C^3\), \(\mathscr L_{\Phi_c}\) is Fredholm of index zero, and the reduced branch \(\eta_q(\lambda)\) is isolated. If the nonzero spectrum of \(\boldsymbol\Delta_\Phi^{(p)}\) remains separated from zero by a gap \(\gamma_p>0\), then the dimension of \(H^p_\Phi(\mathcal K_\Phi)\) is constant along the branch.

**Proof.** The spectral gap implies that the spectral projection onto the zero eigenspace is continuous under the branch deformation. The rank of a continuous finite-rank projection is locally constant. The harmonic cochain space therefore has constant dimension, and Eq. (3.8) gives constant cohomology dimension. \(\square\)

The proposition establishes the PCST notion of a phase: not merely a connected set of configurations, but a region in which adaptive topology and spectral structure remain stable.

## 4. Emergent spectral geometry

### 4.1 Spectral distance

Let \(\lambda_n^{(0)}[\Phi]\) be the nonzero eigenvalues of \(\boldsymbol\Delta_\Phi^{(0)}\) on zero-cochains. Define the heat operator

\[
\mathcal K_\Phi(t)=e^{-t\boldsymbol\Delta_\Phi^{(0)}}.
\tag{4.1}
\]

A phase spectral distance between two stable relational units \(x,y\in\mathcal V\) is defined by the Green kernel

\[
D_\Phi^2(x,y)
=\int_0^\infty dt\,\mathfrak w_\Phi(t)
\left[
\mathcal K_\Phi(t;x,x)+\mathcal K_\Phi(t;y,y)
-2\mathcal K_\Phi(t;x,y)
\right],
\tag{4.2}
\]

where \(\mathfrak w_\Phi(t)\) is fixed by the phase normalization and converges on the stable sector. No edge length is used. Distance is the accumulated inability of the phase Laplacian to identify two relational units.

For a family of coarse-grained phase controls \(\lambda^i\), define the response metric

\[
\boxed{
 g^\Phi_{ij}
=T_i{}^a\chi^{(+)}_{ab}T_j{}^b,
\qquad
T_i{}^a=\frac{\partial\eta^a}{\partial\lambda^i}.
}
\tag{4.3}
\]

The spectral distance and response metric are required to agree in the infrared up to a phase conformal factor:

\[
D_\Phi^2(x,x+dx)
=\Omega_\Phi^2(x)g^\Phi_{ij}dx^idx^j+O(dx^3).
\tag{4.4}
\]

Equation (4.4) is a consistency condition for a geometric phase sector, not a definition imposed on every configuration.

### 4.2 Spectral dimension

The phase spectral dimension is

\[
\boxed{
 d_s^\Phi(t)
=-2\frac{d\log\operatorname{Tr}\mathcal K_\Phi(t)}{d\log t}.
}
\tag{4.5}
\]

If

\[
\operatorname{Tr}\mathcal K_\Phi(t)
\sim C_\Phi t^{-d_s/2}
\qquad(t\to t_{\rm IR}),
\tag{4.6}
\]

then \(d_s\) is the infrared dimension of the phase sector. At short scales the exponent may differ. The dimension is thus a scale-dependent phase invariant:

\[
 d_s^\Phi(\ell)=d_s^\Phi(t=\ell^2).
\tag{4.7}
\]

This is a central PCST prediction. A theory may possess an infrared four-dimensional response while its ultraviolet phase complex has a different spectral dimension, without requiring a pre-existing dimensional continuum.

### Theorem 1 (Infrared geometric emergence)

Assume a stable phase sector for which: (i) the nonzero spectrum of \(\boldsymbol\Delta_\Phi^{(0)}\) has Weyl-type asymptotics; (ii) the heat kernel satisfies a local Gaussian bound with respect to \(D_\Phi\); and (iii) the response metric in Eq. (4.3) has constant rank. Then the infrared phase sector admits a metric-measure description whose local dimension is \(d_s^\Phi\), and whose geodesic distance agrees with the leading spectral distance in Eq. (4.4).

**Proof.** The heat-kernel asymptotics define the local scaling exponent through Eq. (4.5). The Gaussian bound implies that the heat kernel distinguishes neighborhoods by a quadratic distance function. Constant rank of the response metric supplies a nondegenerate local coordinate chart on the reduced control manifold. The equivalence of the two quadratic forms follows from the uniqueness of the leading short-time coefficient in the Gaussian expansion, up to the phase conformal factor \(\Omega_\Phi\). \(\square\)

The theorem identifies the precise conditions under which PCST produces geometry. It does not claim that every phase configuration is geometric.

### 4.3 Emergent connection

Transport of harmonic representatives along a phase branch defines a connection on the cohomology bundle

\[
\mathscr H^p_\Phi\to\mathcal P_\Phi.
\]

Let \(P_\Phi^{(p)}\) be the spectral projection onto \(\mathcal H^p_\Phi\). The phase-cohomological connection is

\[
\boxed{
\nabla_i^{\rm coh}
=P_\Phi^{(p)}\partial_iP_\Phi^{(p)}.
}
\tag{4.8}
\]

Its curvature is

\[
\mathcal R_{ij}^{\rm coh}
=[\nabla_i^{\rm coh},\nabla_j^{\rm coh}].
\tag{4.9}
\]

This connection is induced by the changing harmonic decomposition, not introduced as an independent gauge potential. Holonomy of \(\nabla^{\rm coh}\) can be non-Abelian when the harmonic sector has rank greater than one.

## 5. Cohomological defects and particle sectors

### 5.1 Relative cohomology

Let \(\mathcal K_\infty\) be the asymptotic phase complex of a stable vacuum sector and \(\mathcal K_q\) a localized deformation. The defect class is a relative cohomology element

\[
q(\Phi_q)
\in H^p_\Phi(\mathcal K_q,\mathcal K_\infty).
\tag{5.1}
\]

A defect is nontrivial if this class is nonzero. The class cannot be removed by a phase-frame coboundary preserving the asymptotic complex.

> **Proposition 2 (Relative cohomological protection).** If \(q(\Phi_q)\neq0\), then no continuous finite-cost path within the fixed asymptotic phase class can deform \(\Phi_q\) to the vacuum configuration.

**Proof.** Such a path would induce a homotopy of relative cochains. Relative cohomology classes are invariant under homotopy preserving the boundary complex. Since the vacuum has the zero relative class, the nonzero class cannot be connected to it by the stated path. \(\square\)

The result is topological, but topology alone does not guarantee a finite or stable representative. Those properties arise from the phase equation and stability operator.

### 5.2 Defect localization

Let \(\psi_q\) be a harmonic or near-harmonic cochain localized around a finite region of \(D_\Phi\). Localization is defined spectrally by exponential decay of the heat-kernel evolution:

\[
|\mathcal K_\Phi(t;x,y)\psi_q(y)|
\le C_q e^{-D_\Phi(x,x_q)/\xi_q}
\tag{5.2}
\]

for \(x\) outside the defect core. The localization length \(\xi_q\) is derived from the gap of the defect Laplacian.

A finite phase excess is

\[
0<\mathcal E_\Phi[\Phi_q|\Phi_0]<\infty.
\tag{5.3}
\]

Normal stability requires

\[
\Pi_{N_q}\mathscr L_{\Phi_q}\Pi_{N_q}
\ge \kappa_q\operatorname{id},
\qquad \kappa_q>0.
\tag{5.4}
\]

### Definition 2 (PCST particle)

A PCST particle is a finite-excess, spectrally localized, normally stable phase configuration \(\Phi_q\) carrying a nonzero relative-cohomology class and an isolated defect-Laplacian spectral sector. Its physical identity is the orbit

\[
\boxed{
\mathfrak P_q=[\Phi_q]_{\mathscr G_\Phi},
}
\tag{5.5}
\]

with invariant labels

\[
q=( [q]_{H^p},\,\operatorname{Spec}_q\boldsymbol\Delta,\,\operatorname{Hol}_q,\,d_s^q,\ldots).
\]

### 5.3 Mass and charge

Let the defect Laplacian have isolated eigenmodes

\[
\boldsymbol\Delta_{\Phi_q}^{(p)}\psi_{q,r}
=\Lambda_{q,r}\psi_{q,r},
\qquad
0<\Lambda_{q,0}<\Lambda_{q,1}<\cdots.
\tag{5.6}
\]

The lowest normal eigenvalue determines the mass scale

\[
\boxed{
 m_q^2=Z_q^{-1}\Lambda_{q,0},
}
\tag{5.7}
\]

where \(Z_q\) is the phase-response normalization obtained from the reduced kinetic form.

Charge is the evaluation of a cohomology class on a derived cycle \(\gamma_q\):

\[
\boxed{
Q_q=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\langle[q(\Phi_q)],[\gamma_q]\rangle.
}
\tag{5.8}
\]

For integral cohomology this is quantized. For a non-Abelian coefficient system it becomes a conjugacy class of the cohomology holonomy representation.

### Proposition 3 (Discrete defect spectrum)

Suppose the defect response Laplacian is self-adjoint with compact resolvent on the normal cochain space, and the relative cohomology class is isolated under admissible deformations. Then the defect has a discrete normal excitation spectrum and a stable discrete set of cohomological labels.

**Proof.** Compact resolvent gives discrete eigenvalues with finite multiplicity. Isolation of the relative class prevents continuous mixing with the vacuum or neighboring classes. The resulting excitation and topological labels are discrete invariants of the defect orbit. \(\square\)

## 6. Dynamics, gauge structure, and interaction

### 6.1 Collective motion

Let \(X^i\) be coordinates on the moduli space of a localized defect. Substitution of \(\Phi_q(X)\) into the phase action gives

\[
S_q^{\rm coll}
=\int d\tau
\left[
\frac12 g^\Phi_{ij}(X)\dot X^i\dot X^j
+\mathcal A^q_i(X)\dot X^i
-\mathcal V_q(X)
\right].
\tag{6.1}
\]

The induced connection is the cohomological Berry connection

\[
\mathcal A^q_i
=\langle\psi_q,\nabla_i^{\rm coh}\psi_q\rangle_\Phi.
\tag{6.2}
\]

The moduli equation is

\[
\ddot X^i+\Gamma^\Phi{}_{jk}{}^i\dot X^j\dot X^k
=g^{\Phi\,ij}\mathcal F^q_{jk}\dot X^k
-g^{\Phi\,ij}\partial_j\mathcal V_q,
\tag{6.3}
\]

where \(\Gamma^\Phi\) is the Levi–Civita connection of the derived response metric and \(\mathcal F^q=d\mathcal A^q+\mathcal A^q\wedge\mathcal A^q\). Thus both inertial geometry and internal transport arise from phase-cohomological response.

### 6.2 Gauge redundancy as cochain exactness

A phase perturbation \(\zeta\) is gauge if

\[
\zeta=d_\Phi\alpha
\tag{6.4}
\]

for a zero-cochain \(\alpha\). Physical phase modes are equivalence classes

\[
[\zeta]\in
\frac{\ker d_\Phi}{\operatorname{im}d_\Phi}.
\tag{6.5}
\]

This is the cohomological origin of gauge redundancy. The gauge structure is not selected from a list of Lie groups. It is the automorphism structure of the coefficient system used by the phase cochains. In the Abelian realization it gives \(U(1)\); in a multi-component phase realization it can generate a subgroup of \(U(N)\) determined by the phase stabilizer.

### 6.3 Interaction from Green functions

The interaction between two distant defects is obtained by integrating out the linear normal cochain \(\zeta\):

\[
S_{\rm int}[q_1,q_2]
=-\frac12\langle J_{q_1}+J_{q_2},
\boldsymbol\Delta_\Phi^{-1}(J_{q_1}+J_{q_2})\rangle_\Phi.
\tag{6.6}
\]

The cross term is

\[
V_{12}(D)
=-\langle J_{q_1},
\boldsymbol\Delta_\Phi^{-1}(D)J_{q_2}\rangle_\Phi.
\tag{6.7}
\]

The force law is therefore a Green-function invariant of the phase Laplacian. If the infrared spectral dimension is \(d_s>2\), then

\[
V_{12}(D)\sim\frac{\mathcal Q_{q_1q_2}}{D^{d_s-2}},
\tag{6.8}
\]

up to phase-curvature corrections. The inverse-square law is not assumed; it is the special case \(d_s=3\) for a static Green function or \(d_s=4\) for a relativistic propagator convention.

## 7. Quantum and statistical completion

### 7.1 Induced complex structure

The primitive cochain need not be complex-linear beyond its \(U(1)\) phase values. A complex structure on the reduced mode space arises if the phase pairing contains a nondegenerate antisymmetric form \(\omega_\Phi\) compatible with the symmetric susceptibility:

\[
\omega_\Phi(u,v)=g^\Phi(J_\Phi u,v),
\qquad
J_\Phi^2=-\operatorname{id}.
\tag{7.1}
\]

Then normal modes may be assembled into complex coefficients \(c_r\), and the quadratic reduced action becomes

\[
S_q^{(2)}
=\int d\tau
\left[
\frac{i}{2}(c_r^*D_\tau c_r-c_rD_\tau c_r^*)
-\omega_r c_r^*c_r
\right].
\tag{7.2}
\]

The complex amplitude is therefore a derived representation of compatible phase quadratures.

### 7.2 Phase measure and probabilities

Define the phase-sector norm

\[
\|c\|_\Phi^2
=\langle c,\chi^{(+)}c\rangle.
\tag{7.3}
\]

If the microscopic phase measure is positive and additive over orthogonal harmonic sectors, then the normalized probability of sector \(q\) is

\[
\mathbb P(q|c)
=\frac{\langle c,P_qc\rangle_\Phi}
{\langle c,c\rangle_\Phi}.
\tag{7.4}
\]

PCST treats Eq. (7.4) as a conditional theorem. Positivity, normalization, and composition must be derived from a specified phase measure. A failure of this derivation limits PCST to a classical or semiclassical theory.

### 7.3 Entropy from cohomological multiplicity

For a coarse-grained phase sector \(\mathcal S\), let \(N_p(\mathcal S)\) be the number of admissible cohomology classes and \(\rho_n\) the normalized spectral occupation of the phase Laplacian. Define

\[
S_\Phi(\mathcal S)
=\log N_p(\mathcal S)
-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho\log\rho).
\tag{7.5}
\]

The first term counts topological alternatives; the second counts spectral occupation. Thermodynamic entropy, if recovered, is therefore a two-component phase invariant rather than a primitive statistical postulate.

## 8. Minimal spectral realization

Consider a translationally invariant phase-selected complex whose infrared zero-cochain Laplacian has symbol

\[
\lambda_\Phi(k)
=\frac{1}{\ell_\Phi^2}
\left[2\sum_{a=1}^{d_*}(1-\cos k_a\ell_\Phi)
+\alpha_\Phi\left(\sum_{a=1}^{d_*}(1-\cos k_a\ell_\Phi)\right)^2\right].
\tag{8.1}
