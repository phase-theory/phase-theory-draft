# Relativity 46.0 — Epistemic-Horizon Relativity  
## The Physical Relativity of Knowability

**White paper / academic preprint**

---

## Abstract

Epistemic-Horizon Relativity is the hypothesis that what can be known is not absolute but relative to causal, thermodynamic, computational, quantum, and observational horizons. An observer \(O\) does not have access to all facts. The observer has access only to an algebra of observables,

\[
\mathcal{A}_O,
\]

determined by causal location, energy, horizon area, measurement capacity, and computational resources. The rest of reality is not merely unknown in a psychological sense. It is operationally inaccessible. Horizon entropy bounds the amount of information accessible to an observer,

\[
S_O
\leq
\frac{A_O}{4G\hbar},
\]

and computational complexity may further restrict what can be decoded even when information is encoded in principle. The central principle is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Knowability is horizon-relative; reality exceeds accessible factuality.}
}
\]

This is not subjectivism. It is a physical doctrine of limits imposed by causality, entropy, quantum noncommutativity, and computation. Epistemic-Horizon Relativity unifies black-hole thermodynamics, de Sitter horizons, Rindler horizons, algebraic quantum field theory, holographic entanglement wedges, quantum information bounds, and complexity horizons into a single framework. It asserts that reality is not exhausted by what any observer can know. Yet what can be known is not arbitrary. It is rigorously constrained by the observer’s physical horizon.

## 1. Introduction

Classical physics imagined an ideal observer who could, in principle, know the state of the entire universe.

If one knew all positions and momenta, one could predict everything.

This Laplacean ideal assumed:

1. no causal horizon,
2. no entropy bound,
3. no quantum limitation,
4. no computational limitation,
5. no observational disturbance,
6. no finite-resource restriction.

Modern physics has dismantled each assumption.

Relativity introduced causal horizons. Black-hole thermodynamics introduced entropy bounds. Quantum mechanics introduced noncommutativity and measurement disturbance. Holography introduced finite horizon information capacity. Computational complexity introduced decoding horizons. Cosmology introduced particle and event horizons.

Thus the classical ideal of total knowability is false.

Epistemic-Horizon Relativity formulates the replacement:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Every observer has a physically bounded horizon of knowability.}
}
\]

This does not mean that reality is created by knowledge.

It means that access to reality is physically structured.

An observer’s facts are not arbitrary. They are the facts contained in the observer’s accessible algebra and decodable within the observer’s resource budget.

Reality may exceed those facts.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Knowability is horizon-relative; reality exceeds accessible factuality.}
}
\]

## 2. Epistemic Horizons

An epistemic horizon is a physical boundary to knowability.

It is not merely ignorance.

Ignorance says:

\[
\text{I do not know } X.
\]

An epistemic horizon says:

\[
\text{No observer in my physical situation can know } X.
\]

Epistemic horizons may be:

1. causal,
2. thermodynamic,
3. computational,
4. quantum,
5. observational,
6. holographic,
7. cosmological,
8. complexity-theoretic.

Each type imposes a different physical limit.

Together they define the observer’s knowable world.

## 3. The Observer as a Physical System

In Epistemic-Horizon Relativity, an observer is not an abstract mind.

An observer is a physical system with finite resources.

An observer may be represented as a tuple,

\[
O
=
\left(
W_O,
\mathcal{A}_O,
\omega_O,
R_O
\right),
\]

where:

- \(W_O\) is the observer’s worldline, causal diamond, or accessible region,
- \(\mathcal{A}_O\) is the algebra of observables accessible to \(O\),
- \(\omega_O\) is the observer’s state or density matrix on \(\mathcal{A}_O\),
- \(R_O\) is the observer’s resource budget: energy, time, memory, and computational capacity.

The accessible facts of \(O\) are propositions in \(\mathcal{A}_O\) that can be measured and decoded within \(R_O\).

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{An observer is a finite physical access structure.}
}
\]

## 4. Accessible Algebra

Let \(\mathcal{A}_O\) be the algebra of observables accessible to observer \(O\).

A physical proposition is represented by an element of \(\mathcal{A}_O\), or by a projection,

\[
P\in\mathcal{A}_O.
\]

The probability that \(O\) assigns to the proposition is

\[
\omega_O(P).
\]

If \(P\notin\mathcal{A}_O\), then the proposition is not accessible to \(O\).

It may still be meaningful relative to another observer \(O'\), with algebra \(\mathcal{A}_{O'}\).

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Facts are relative to accessible algebras.}
}
\]

## 5. Algebraic Quantum Field Theory and Local Access

Algebraic quantum field theory provides a natural framework.

To each spacetime region \(\mathcal{O}\), one assigns an algebra of observables,

\[
\mathcal{O}
\mapsto
\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{O}).
\]

These algebras satisfy isotony:

\[
\mathcal{O}_1\subseteq\mathcal{O}_2
\quad
\Rightarrow
\quad
\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{O}_1)
\subseteq
\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{O}_2).
\]

They satisfy locality:

\[
[\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{O}_1),\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{O}_2)]
=
0
\]

when \(\mathcal{O}_1\) and \(\mathcal{O}_2\) are spacelike separated.

An observer confined to a region \(\mathcal{O}\) has access only to

\[
\mathcal{A}_O
=
\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{O}).
\]

Observables outside \(\mathcal{O}\) are not merely unmeasured.

They are outside the observer’s operational domain.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Locality is an epistemic horizon.}
}
\]

## 6. Causal Horizons

Causal horizons are the most basic epistemic horizons.

A causal horizon separates events that can influence an observer from events that cannot.

Examples include:

1. black-hole event horizons,
2. Rindler horizons,
3. de Sitter cosmological horizons,
4. particle horizons,
5. event horizons in accelerating universes.

For a Schwarzschild black hole, the horizon radius is

\[
r_s
=
\frac{2GM}{c^2}.
\]

Events inside \(r<r_s\) cannot send signals to distant observers.

For de Sitter space, the cosmological horizon is

\[
r_H
=
\frac{c}{H}.
\]

A static observer cannot access events beyond \(r_H\).

For an accelerated observer in Minkowski space, the Rindler horizon arises even though inertial observers see no horizon.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Causal access is observer-relative.}
}
\]

## 7. Rindler Horizons and Observer-Relative Access

\[
ds^2
=

d\rho^2

The accelerated observer has access only to the right Rindler wedge.

\rho_R

The Unruh temperature is

\[
T_{\text{Unruh}}
=
\frac{\hbar a}{2\pi k_{\text{B}}c}.

Thus the accelerated observer’s epistemic horizon is also a thermal horizon.

What an inertial observer calls vacuum, an accelerated observer calls a thermal state.

This is a profound example of Epistemic-Horizon Relativity.

## 8. Black-Hole Horizons and Entropy Bounds

Black holes introduce entropy bounds.

The Bekenstein–Hawking entropy is

\[
S_{\text{BH}}
=
\frac{k_{\text{B}}A}{4G\hbar/c^3}.
\]

In units where \(c=k_{\text{B}}=1\),

\[
S_{\text{BH}}
=
\frac{A}{4G\hbar}.
\]

This entropy is not merely a thermodynamic analogy.

It bounds the information accessible to an exterior observer.

The black-hole interior may contain information, but the exterior observer’s access is limited by the horizon.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Horizon area bounds accessible information.}
}
\]

## 9. The Bekenstein Bound

The Bekenstein bound states that a system of energy \(E\) and radius \(R\) can contain at most

\[
S
\leq
\frac{2\pi k_{\text{B}} E R}{\hbar c}
\]

entropy.

In information units,

\[
I
\leq
\frac{2\pi E R}{\hbar c\ln 2}.
\]

This bound follows from the generalized second law of thermodynamics.

If a system had too much entropy, dropping it into a black hole could decrease total generalized entropy.

Thus physical systems have finite information capacity.

Epistemic-Horizon Relativity generalizes this:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Finite physical systems support finite knowable information.}
}
\]

## 10. The Covariant Entropy Bound

Bousso’s covariant entropy bound states that the entropy passing through a lightsheet \(L\) of a surface \(B\) satisfies

\[
S(L)
\leq
\frac{A(B)}{4G\hbar}.
\]

This bound is covariant and applies in dynamical spacetimes.

It supports the holographic principle:

\[
\boxed{
\text{The information content of a region is bounded by its boundary area.}
}
\]

Thus the knowable information in a region is not proportional to volume.

It is bounded by area.

This is a radical epistemic limit.

\mathcal{A}_{\text{bulk}}(E(A))

within a code subspace.

\boxed{

## 12. Computational Horizons

Even when information is encoded in principle, it may be inaccessible in practice.

Computational complexity imposes a further horizon.

Let \(C_{\text{decode}}(I)\) be the complexity of decoding information \(I\).

Let \(R_O\) be the computational resources available to observer \(O\).

Then \(I\) is accessible to \(O\) only if

\[
C_{\text{decode}}(I)
\leq
R_O.
\]

If

\[
C_{\text{decode}}(I)
>
R_O,
\]

then \(I\) is beyond the observer’s computational horizon.

This is not psychological limitation.

It is physical limitation.

The observer lacks the energy, time, or memory to perform the required computation.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Encoded does not mean accessible.}
}
\]

## 13. Margolus–Levitin and Physical Computation Bounds

The Margolus–Levitin bound states that a system with energy \(E\) above its ground state can perform at most

\[
\nu
\leq
\frac{2E}{\pi\hbar}
\]

elementary operations per second.

Thus computation is bounded by energy.

Landauer’s principle states that erasing one bit costs at least

\[
E_{\text{erase}}
\geq
k_{\text{B}}T\ln 2.
\]

Thus computation is bounded by thermodynamics.

The Bekenstein bound limits memory.

The speed of light limits communication.

Thus an observer’s epistemic horizon is shaped by physical computation.

## 14. Complexity Horizons in Black-Hole Physics

Black-hole interiors illustrate computational horizons.

The interior may be encoded in Hawking radiation.

But decoding it may require exponential complexity:

\[
C_{\text{decode}}
\sim
e^{S_{\text{BH}}}.
\]

For a macroscopic black hole, this is physically impossible.

Thus the interior can be encoded but inaccessible.

This resolves some apparent paradoxes.

The information is not destroyed.

But it is not available to bounded observers.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Black-hole interiors are computational epistemic horizons.}
}
\]

## 15. Quantum Epistemic Horizons

Quantum mechanics introduces intrinsic epistemic limits.

The Heisenberg uncertainty relation states,

\[
\Delta x\,\Delta p
\geq
\frac{\hbar}{2}.
\]

Noncommuting observables cannot be simultaneously sharp:

\[
[A,B]\neq 0.
\]

Measurement generally disturbs the system.

The no-cloning theorem forbids copying arbitrary unknown quantum states.

The Holevo bound limits accessible classical information from quantum states:

\[
I(X;Y)
\leq
\chi,
\]

where

\[
\chi
=
S(\rho)
-
\sum_x p_x S(\rho_x).
\]

Thus quantum states contain more potential information than can be extracted.

This is a quantum epistemic horizon.

## 16. Contextuality and Measurement Access

Quantum contextuality shows that measurement outcomes depend on measurement context.

A proposition may not have a context-independent truth value.

In the Kochen–Specker theorem, no global assignment of values to all projectors can satisfy functional relations in Hilbert spaces of dimension at least three.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Quantum facts are access-relative to measurement contexts.}
}
\]

Epistemic-Horizon Relativity incorporates this.

The accessible algebra \(\mathcal{A}_O\) includes not only causal and computational limits but also contextual limits.

## 19. Reality Versus Accessible Factuality

Epistemic-Horizon Relativity distinguishes:

1. reality,
2. accessible factuality,
3. knowable propositions,
4. decodable information.

Reality is the full physical structure.

Accessible factuality is the subset of facts available to an observer.

Knowable propositions are those in the observer’s algebra.

Decodable information is the subset of knowable propositions that can be extracted within resource bounds.

Thus:

\[
\text{decodable}
\subseteq
\text{knowable}
\subseteq
\text{accessible}
\subseteq
\text{real}.
\]

The central claim is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Reality exceeds accessible factuality.}
}
\]

1. causal structure,
2. horizon area,
3. entropy,
4. energy,
5. quantum noncommutativity,

Different observers may have different accessible facts, but those facts are not arbitrary.

They are determined by the observer’s physical situation.

Thus:

\boxed{
\text{Epistemic relativity is physical, not psychological.}

Thus quantum states cannot be fully read out.

## Appendix F: Complexity Horizon

Let \(R_O\) be an observer’s computational budget.

Information \(I\) is accessible if

\[
C_{\text{decode}}(I)
\leq
R_O.
\]

If

\[
C_{\text{decode}}(I)
>
R_O,
\]

then \(I\) lies beyond the observer’s complexity horizon.
