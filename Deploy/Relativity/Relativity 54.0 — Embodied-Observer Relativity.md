# Relativity 54.0 — Embodied-Observer Relativity  
## The Observable World as a Function of Sensorimotor Embodiment

**White paper / academic preprint**

---

## Abstract

Embodied-Observer Relativity is the hypothesis that observers are not abstract coordinate systems, disembodied viewpoints, or idealized measurement machines outside nature. They are embodied physical agents whose bodies, sensors, actuators, energy supplies, computational capacities, and environmental couplings define what can be observed. An embodiment \(B\) determines an accessible observable set,

\[
\mathcal{O}_B.
\]

Different embodiments yield different observational frames,

\[
\mathcal{O}_B
\neq
\mathcal{O}_{B'},
\]

and therefore different operational worlds. A bat, a human, a telescope, a neutrino detector, a gravitational-wave interferometer, and a quantum computer do not inhabit the same operational world, even if they are embedded in the same physical universe. The central principle is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Observation is embodied; the observable world is relative to sensorimotor capacity.}
}
\]

This is not subjectivism. Physics remains objective because embodied observers can calibrate their frames, translate observations across instruments, and identify invariant structures. Embodied-Observer Relativity unifies quantum reference frames, relational quantum mechanics, cybernetics, sensory ecology, instrumentation theory, and the philosophy of measurement into a single framework. It asserts that the “view from nowhere” is impossible. All observation is view from some body, some instrument, some protocol, and some action capacity. Objectivity is not escape from embodiment; it is the discovery of invariants across embodiments.

## 1. Introduction

Classical physics imagined the observer as a geometrical point.

An observer had a position, a velocity, and perhaps a coordinate system. But the observer had no body, no sensory limitations, no metabolic constraints, no measurement apparatus, no finite memory, no action repertoire.

This ideal observer was nowhere and everywhere.

It could measure position without disturbance.

It could observe without interacting.

It could record without cost.

It could decode without protocol.

Modern physics has dismantled this fiction.

Quantum mechanics shows that measurement is interaction.

Relativity shows that observation is frame-dependent.

Information theory shows that recording has thermodynamic cost.

Cybernetics shows that observation is coupled to action.

Biology shows that organisms inhabit species-specific sensory worlds.

Instrumentation shows that telescopes, detectors, and computers extend and transform observation.

Embodied-Observer Relativity draws the conclusion:

\[
\boxed{
\text{An observer is a physical embodiment, not an abstract viewpoint.}
}
\]

The observable world is not given absolutely.

It is disclosed relative to sensorimotor capacity.

## 2. Embodiment as Physical Access Structure

An embodiment is a physical system equipped to sense and act.

Let an embodiment \(B\) be represented as a tuple,

\[
B
=
\left(
\mathcal{S}_B,
\mathcal{A}_B,
\mathcal{P}_B,
\mathcal{M}_B,
\mathcal{E}_B
\right),
\]

where:

- \(\mathcal{S}_B\) is the sensor apparatus,
- \(\mathcal{A}_B\) is the actuator apparatus,
- \(\mathcal{P}_B\) is the processing or memory system,
- \(\mathcal{M}_B\) is the metabolic or energy supply,
- \(\mathcal{E}_B\) is the environmental coupling structure.

The embodiment defines a sensory map,

\[
S_B:
W
\rightarrow
\mathcal{O}_B,
\]

where \(W\) is the physical world-state and \(\mathcal{O}_B\) is the observation space available to \(B\).

The embodiment also defines an action map,

\[
A_B:
\mathcal{A}_B
\times
W
\rightarrow
W',
\]

describing how the observer can intervene on the world.

Thus observation is not passive reception.

It is embodied coupling.

Every embodiment has an accessible observable set,

\[
\mathcal{O}_B.
\]

This set contains all distinctions the observer can physically make.

For a human eye, \(\mathcal{O}_B\) includes visible wavelengths approximately from 400 to 700 nanometers.

For a bat, \(\mathcal{O}_B\) includes ultrasonic echoes and temporal delay structures.

For a radio telescope, \(\mathcal{O}_B\) includes radio-frequency electromagnetic fields.

For a neutrino detector, \(\mathcal{O}_B\) includes rare weak-interaction events.

For a gravitational-wave interferometer, \(\mathcal{O}_B\) includes differential arm-length strain.

For a quantum computer, \(\mathcal{O}_B\) includes controlled unitary transformations and measurement statistics in chosen bases.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Different embodiments access different observable sets.}
}
\]

The universe may be one, but operational worlds differ.

## 4. Observational Equivalence and the Observer-Relative World

An embodiment does not observe the world-in-itself.

It observes the world through its sensorimotor filter.

Two world-states \(w\) and \(w'\) are observationally equivalent for observer \(B\) if

\[
S_B(w)
=
S_B(w').
\]

More generally, if the observer can perform interventions \(a\in\mathcal{A}_B\), then \(w\) and \(w'\) are operationally equivalent if for all accessible actions and measurements,

\[
P(o\mid w,a,B)
=
P(o\mid w',a,B).
\]

Define the equivalence relation,

\[
w
\sim_B
w'
\quad
\Longleftrightarrow
\quad
\text{no accessible observation by }B\text{ distinguishes }w\text{ from }w'.
\]

The observer-relative world is the quotient,

\[
W_B
=
W/\sim_B.
\]

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{The observable world for }B\text{ is the quotient of reality by }B\text{’s observational indistinguishability.}
}
\]

## 5. Sensor Bandwidth, Resolution, and Noise

Embodiment limits observation through physical constraints.

A sensor has:

1. bandwidth,
2. resolution,
3. dynamic range,
4. noise floor,
5. energy cost,
6. response time,
7. calibration stability,
8. failure modes.

Let the true signal be \(x(t)\).

The sensor produces an output,

\[
y(t)
=
\int K(t-t')x(t')dt'
+
n(t),
\]

where \(K\) is the sensor response kernel and \(n(t)\) is noise.

The observer can only reconstruct \(x\) within the limits imposed by \(K\) and \(n\).

Thus observation is always filtered.

The filter is the body.

## 6. Information-Theoretic Limits of Embodiment

An embodied observer has finite information capacity.

The mutual information between world states \(W\) and observations \(O\) is

\[
I(W;O)
=
\sum_{w,o}
P(w,o)
\log
\frac{P(w,o)}{P(w)P(o)}.
\]

The embodiment’s sensory capacity is bounded by

\[
C_B
=
\max I(W;O),
\]

subject to physical constraints such as energy, bandwidth, and noise.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Embodiment bounds observable information.}
}
\]

No observer can extract more information than its sensorimotor channel permits.

## 7. The Umwelt: Species-Specific Operational Worlds

The biologist Jakob von Uexküll introduced the concept of the Umwelt.

An Umwelt is the self-centered perceptual world of an organism.

A tick senses butyric acid, temperature, and tactile cues.

A bat senses echo delay, Doppler shift, and acoustic texture.

A bee senses ultraviolet patterns and polarization.

A dog senses odor concentration gradients.

A human senses visible light, audible sound, tactile pressure, and symbolic language.

Each organism does not merely live in the same world with different opinions.

It inhabits a different operational world.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Biological embodiment defines a species-relative observable world.}
}
\]

## 11. Neutrino Detectors and Weakly Coupled Worlds

Neutrinos interact weakly with matter.

A neutrino detector does not observe neutrinos directly.

It observes rare interaction events: flashes, charges, energies, timing patterns.

The observable set is sparse and indirect.

The detector embodies a world in which most matter is transparent.

A neutrino observer would inhabit a very different operational world from a photon-based observer.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Interaction strength shapes embodiment.}
}
\]

What can be observed depends on what can physically couple to the observer.

## 14. Embodied Observation in Quantum Mechanics

In quantum mechanics, measurement is not passive.

A measurement context selects an observable algebra.

A system state \(\rho\) yields outcome probabilities,

\[
P(i)
=
\operatorname{Tr}(\rho P_i).
\]

But the projectors \(P_i\) are determined by the measurement apparatus.

The apparatus is an embodied system.

Thus the set of possible outcomes depends on embodiment.

The same quantum state can yield different observational worlds under different measurement protocols.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Quantum observation is embodied protocol-relative.}
}
\]

## 16. Objectivity Through Calibration

If observation is embodied, is objectivity possible?

Yes.

Objectivity is not escape from embodiment.

Objectivity is the discovery of invariant structures across embodiments.

Let two embodiments \(B\) and \(B'\) observe the same physical structure.

Calibration is a map,

\[
T_{B\to B'}:
\mathcal{O}_B
\rightarrow
\mathcal{O}_{B'},
\]

such that relevant content is preserved:

\[
T_{B\to B'}(o_B)
\approx
o_{B'}.
\]

If many embodiments can be calibrated to agree on a structure, that structure is objective in the operational sense.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Objectivity is calibrated invariance across embodiments.}
}
\]

\[

=
T_{\mu\nu}
e_a{}^\mu
e_b{}^\nu.

\boxed{
