## Abstract

Mathematical-Structure Relativity is the hypothesis that physical reality is not one privileged mathematical model but an equivalence class of consistent mathematical structures. It is a relativistic refinement of the mathematical-universe idea: a physical world is not a substance decorated with mathematical properties; it is a mathematical structure, and our world is one structure among many. Let \(\mathcal{S}\) be the class of consistent structures. Physical reality may then be represented as

\[
\mathcal{R}
=
\{S\in\mathcal{S}\mid \mathrm{Consistent}(S)\}/\cong,
\]

where \(\cong\) denotes isomorphism and, more generally, admissible categorical, dual, semantic, or empirical equivalence. The central principle is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Existence is structural consistency; our universe is indexical within structure-space.}
}
\]

The laws of physics are not imposed on mathematics from outside. They are internal regularities of the particular mathematical structure we inhabit. Constants are parameters of that structure. Symmetries are automorphisms. Observers are self-modeling substructures. Actuality is not absolute; it is indexical. Mathematical-Structure Relativity unifies structural realism, modal realism, logical relativity, semantic relativity, and Omega invariance into a single ontological framework. It proposes that the ultimate relativization is not merely of frames, descriptions, or logics, but of the mathematical structure called “our universe.”

## 1. Introduction

Physics has progressively identified reality with structure.

Classical physics described substances: particles, fields, fluids, and bodies. Relativity replaced absolute space and time with geometric structure. Quantum theory replaced definite properties with Hilbert-space structure. Gauge theory replaced potentials with equivalence classes. Holography replaced bulk ontology with boundary information structure. Logical relativity replaced absolute logic with categorical coherence.

Mathematical-Structure Relativity completes this trajectory.

It says:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Reality is not described by mathematics. Reality is mathematical structure.}
}
\]

This does not mean that equations are physically real in a naive Platonic sense. It means that what is physical is invariant structure: relations, symmetries, categories, measures, and consistency conditions.

If this is correct, then the universe is not a thing that happens to satisfy mathematical laws. It is a mathematical structure whose internal relations are what we call laws.

Our universe is then not absolutely privileged. It is one structure within structure-space.

The statement “this is the actual universe” functions like “here” or “now.” It is indexical.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Actuality is self-location within mathematical structure-space.}
}
\]

This is Mathematical-Structure Relativity.

## 2. What Is a Mathematical Structure?

A mathematical structure may be defined in several complementary ways.

### 2.1 Model-Theoretic Definition

In model theory, a structure in a signature \(\Sigma\) is a tuple,

\[
M
=
\left(
|M|,
\{f_i^M\},
\{R_j^M\},
\{c_k^M\}
\right),
\]

where:

- \(|M|\) is a domain of objects,
- \(f_i^M\) are functions,
- \(R_j^M\) are relations,
- \(c_k^M\) are distinguished constants.

A theory \(T\) is a set of sentences in a formal language.

A structure \(M\) is a model of \(T\) if

\[
M \models T.
\]

A theory is consistent if it has a model:

\[
\mathrm{Consistent}(T)
\quad
\Longleftrightarrow
\quad
\exists M
\;
(M\models T).
\]

This is the semantic notion of consistency.

### 2.2 Categorical Definition

In category theory, a structure may be represented as an object in a category of structures.

For example:

- groups are objects in \(\mathbf{Grp}\),
- topological spaces are objects in \(\mathbf{Top}\),
- Hilbert spaces are objects in \(\mathbf{Hilb}\),
- manifolds are objects in a category of smooth spaces,
- toposes are categorical universes with internal logic.

Morphisms preserve structure.

Isomorphic objects represent the same structure:

\[
M \cong N.
\]

Categorical equivalence generalizes isomorphism:

\[
\mathcal{C} \simeq \mathcal{D}.
\]

Thus a mathematical structure is not a particular set-theoretic presentation. It is an invariant pattern under equivalence.

### 2.3 Structural Definition

More generally, a mathematical structure is a coherent system of:

1. objects or elements,
2. relations,
3. operations,
4. axioms,
5. symmetries,
6. invariants,
7. internal logic,
8. possible transformations.

A physical world, under Mathematical-Structure Relativity, is such a structure.

Let

\[
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{con}}
\]

be the subclass of consistent structures.

A structure \(S\) is consistent if it can be instantiated in some coherent mathematical universe:

\[
\mathrm{Consistent}(S).

\[
\boxed{
\text{To exist physically is to be a consistent mathematical structure.}
}

This is the ontological core of the framework.

It does not say that every linguistic description corresponds to reality. It says that every consistent structure is real in the modal-structural sense.

Our universe is one such structure.

## 8. Laws as Internal Regularities

If a physical world is a mathematical structure, what are laws?

Laws are not external commands imposed on the structure.

They are internal regularities of the structure.

For a structure \(S\), its laws may be identified with:

1. axioms true in \(S\),
2. invariant relations under automorphisms,
3. dynamical endomorphisms,
4. conservation laws,
5. symmetry groups,
6. categorical coherence conditions,
7. measure-preserving transformations,
8. stable substructure patterns.

Let \(\mathrm{Aut}(S)\) be the automorphism group of \(S\).

A lawlike relation \(R\) is invariant if

\[
g(R)=R
\quad
\forall g\in\mathrm{Aut}(S).
\]

Thus laws are structural invariants.

For example, in a spacetime structure, diffeomorphism invariance is an automorphism invariance.

In a quantum structure, unitary symmetry is an automorphism of Hilbert space.

In a gauge structure, gauge transformations define equivalence classes.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Laws are invariant relations within a mathematical structure.}
}
\]

## 10. Measure over Structure-Space

If many structures exist, probability requires a measure.

Let \(\mu\) be a measure over equivalence classes of structures:

\[
\mu:
\mathcal{R}
\rightarrow
[0,\infty].
\]

Let \(N_{\mathrm{obs}}(S)\) be the number or measure of observer-moments in structure \(S\).

Then the probability of observing data \(D\) is

\[
P(D)
=
\sum_{[S]\in\mathcal{R}}
\mu([S])
N_{\mathrm{obs}}(S)
P(D\mid S).
\]

Equivalently, in continuous form,

\[
P(D)
=
\int_{\mathcal{R}}
d\mu([S])
N_{\mathrm{obs}}(S)
P(D\mid S).
\]

The posterior probability of inhabiting structure \(S\) given data \(D\) is

\[
P(S\mid D)
\propto
\mu(S)
N_{\mathrm{obs}}(S)
P(D\mid S).
\]

This is the self-locating probability formula of Mathematical-Structure Relativity.

The measure problem is central.

Without a principled measure \(\mu\), the framework risks empirical vacuity.

With a principled measure, it may become predictive.

\[
X
\cong
\mathrm{Hom}(-,X).

Thus an object is not a hidden substratum. It is a node in a web of morphisms.

Physical objects are therefore structural positions.

An electron is not a little substance. It is a stable pattern in a quantum field structure.

An observer is not a soul-substance. It is a self-modeling substructure.

A spacetime point is not an absolute location. It is a relational position in a geometric structure.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{

## 19. Gödel, Tarski, and Self-Reference

Mathematical-Structure Relativity must confront logical limitation theorems.

Gödel’s incompleteness theorems show that sufficiently rich consistent formal systems cannot prove all truths about themselves.

Tarski’s undefinability theorem shows that truth cannot be fully defined within the same language.

Thus no internal description can completely capture its own structure.

But Mathematical-Structure Relativity does not require complete self-transparency.

It requires only that structures exist and that observers inhabit them.

The framework is reflexively stable:

\[
\text{The statement “reality is structure” is itself a structure within structure-space.}
\]

It does not need to stand outside all mathematics.

It is a pattern within the mathematical plenitude.

## 22. Scientific Status

Mathematical-Structure Relativity is not an ordinary empirical theory.

It is a meta-physical framework.

It becomes scientifically meaningful when combined with:

1. a measure over structures,
2. a theory of observers,
3. a specification of equivalence,
4. empirical data,
5. predictive typicality assumptions.

Without these, it is metaphysical.

With these, it may constrain expectations about laws, constants, and typical observations.

For example, if the measure favors simpler structures, one may expect simple laws.

If the measure favors structures with many observers, one may expect observer-typical conditions.

But these predictions depend entirely on the measure.

Thus the framework is powerful but incomplete.
