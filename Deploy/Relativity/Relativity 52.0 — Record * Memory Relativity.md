## Abstract

Record / Memory Relativity is the hypothesis that the past is accessible only through records, and that what counts as “the past” is relative to the record structure available to an observer. A record is a stable physical correlation between a present state and a prior event,

\[
\rho_{SR}
=
\sum_i
p_i
\ket{i}\bra{i}_S
\otimes
\ket{R_i}\bra{R_i}_R.
\]

Without records, there is no operationally accessible past. The probability of a history \(H\) given records \(R\) is

\[
P(H\mid R).
\]

The central principle is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{The past is record-relative; history is reconstruction from traces.}
}
\]

This framework applies to cosmology, archaeology, geology, forensic science, biological memory, quantum measurement, black-hole information, and the thermodynamic arrow of time. It does not deny that past events occurred. It denies that the past is directly present to an observer. The past is inferred from traces, and those traces are physical, finite, degradable, and observer-relative. Record / Memory Relativity unifies Bayesian retrodiction, decoherence, quantum Darwinism, thermodynamic irreversibility, and historical reconstruction into a single framework.

## 2. What Is a Record?

A record is a physical system whose present state is correlated with a prior event.

Let \(S\) represent the system or event to be recorded.

Let \(R\) represent the recording system.

A perfect classical record has the form,

\[
\rho_{SR}
=
\sum_i
p_i
\ket{i}\bra{i}_S
\otimes
\ket{R_i}\bra{R_i}_R.
\]

Here:

- \(\ket{i}_S\) are distinguishable states of the recorded system,
- \(\ket{R_i}_R\) are corresponding record states,
- \(p_i\) are probabilities or weights.

The record states must be stable and distinguishable:

\[
\langle R_i | R_j\rangle
\approx
\delta_{ij}.
\]

If the record states are not orthogonal or not stable, the record is unreliable.

Thus a record is not merely a sign.

It is a physical correlation robust enough to persist and be read.

## 3. Records as Stable Correlations

The strength of a record is measured by correlation.

For classical variables \(S\) and \(R\), mutual information is

\[
I(S:R)
=
\sum_{s,r}
P(s,r)
\ln
\frac{P(s,r)}{P(s)P(r)}.
\]

A good record has high mutual information:

\[
I(S:R)
\approx
H(S),
\]

where \(H(S)\) is the entropy of the recorded variable.

A poor record has low mutual information:

\[
I(S:R)
\approx
0.

A record is robust if the correlation survives noise:

\[
R
\rightarrow
R'
\]

with

\[
I(S:R')
\approx
I(S:R).
\]

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{A record is a stable channel of information from past to present.}
}
\]

## 5. The Past as Inference

Because the past is not directly observed, knowledge of the past is inferential.

Let \(H\) be a candidate history.

Let \(R\) be the available record data.

The posterior probability of the history is

\[
P(H\mid R)
=
\frac{
P(R\mid H)P(H)
}{
P(R)
}.
\]

Here:

- \(P(H)\) is the prior over histories,
- \(P(R\mid H)\) is the likelihood of the records given the history,
- \(P(R)\) normalizes the distribution.

The past is not simply \(H\).

The operational past is the distribution,

\[
P(H\mid R).
\]

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{History is probabilistic reconstruction from records.}
}

## 6. Underdetermination of the Past

Therefore many histories may be compatible with the same records.

\[
P(R\mid H_1)

Define record equivalence:

\[
H_1
\sim_R
H_2
\quad
\Longleftrightarrow
\quad
P(R\mid H_1)
=
P(R\mid H_2)

for all accessible records \(R\).

\[
[H]_R.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{The accessible past is the equivalence class of histories compatible with records.}

## 7. Thermodynamics of Records

Records require thermodynamic irreversibility.

To create a stable record, a system must increase total entropy.

A measurement or recording process correlates system and apparatus:

\[
\ket{s_i}\ket{A_0}
\rightarrow
\ket{s_i}\ket{A_i}.
\]

But stable recording also requires dissipation or amplification.

Landauer’s principle states that erasing one bit costs at least

\[
E
\geq
k_{\text{B}}T\ln 2.
\]

Memory formation is not free.

It requires physical resources.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Records are thermodynamic structures.}
}
\]

This explains why records point toward the past rather than the future.

The universe has a low-entropy past boundary.

Entropy increases toward the future.

Records are entropy-increasing traces of earlier lower-entropy conditions.

Thus the arrow of records is the thermodynamic arrow.

## 8. Why We Have Records of the Past but Not the Future

A natural question is:

\[
\text{Why do records point to the past?}

Records are formed when a system leaves a stable trace in another system.

This process is reliable only in the direction of increasing entropy.

The early universe had low entropy.

Local processes increase entropy.

Therefore present states contain correlations with earlier low-entropy states.

They do not generally contain correlations with future states, because the future is not yet fixed by present boundary conditions in the same way.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{The record arrow is the entropy arrow.}

\boxed{
\text{Quantum records are decohered, redundantly encoded correlations.}
