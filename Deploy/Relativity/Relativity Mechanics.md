# Relativity Mechanics  
## A Mathematical Theory of Invariant Physical Description

**Preprint**

---

## Abstract

Relativity Mechanics develops a general mathematical theory of physical description. Rather than beginning with spacetime, fields, particles, metrics, or quantum states, it begins with a more primitive observation: every physical system admits multiple descriptions related by transformations that preserve physical content. The fundamental object of physics is therefore not an individual description but the equivalence class of admissible descriptions generated by a symmetry action. Physical reality is represented by invariants of these equivalence classes rather than by any particular representative.

The theory introduces the concept of a **relative description space**

\[
\Omega,
\]

whose elements represent all admissible descriptions of a single physical system. A transformation group

\[
G
\]

acts upon this space through

\[
\triangleright : G \times \Omega \rightarrow \Omega,
\]

partitioning \(\Omega\) into equivalence classes, or orbits,

\[
[\omega] = G \cdot \omega.
\]

The physically meaningful state is identified not with a point \(\omega \in \Omega\), but with its orbit under the admissibility group. Observable quantities are functions on the quotient space

\[
\Omega/G,
\]

which constitutes the invariant description space of the theory.

Relativity Mechanics therefore replaces configuration-based ontology with orbit-based ontology. Individual descriptions possess no absolute physical significance apart from the admissibility relations that connect them. The mathematical structure of a physical theory is determined by the choice of admissibility group, the geometry of its action, and the invariant structures induced on the corresponding quotient space. Different physical theories arise by specifying different description spaces, transformation groups, admissibility conditions, and dynamical laws on the resulting orbit manifold.

Within this framework, the theory introduces the **relativity schema**

\[
\mathcal R = (\Omega, G, \triangleright, I),
\]

where \(\Omega\) is the description space, \(G\) is the admissibility group, \(\triangleright\) denotes its action, and

\[
I : \Omega \rightarrow \Omega/G
\]

is the invariant projection. This schema provides a universal mathematical language for constructing physical theories independent of any particular geometric, quantum, or field-theoretic assumptions.

Relativity Mechanics also establishes a hierarchy of descriptive structures obtained by enlarging admissibility groups. Conventional state descriptions correspond to trivial admissibility. More sophisticated structures incorporate additional relational freedoms while preserving invariant content. Theories such as gauge theories, quantum reference-frame theories, local-frame gravitation, and other symmetry-based formulations appear as specializations of the same underlying formalism, differing only in the choice of admissibility group and the geometry of the corresponding orbit space.

Dynamics is formulated directly on invariant structures rather than on arbitrary representatives. Physical evolution is therefore required to commute with admissibility transformations, ensuring that trajectories are well-defined on the quotient space. Conservation laws arise from invariant group actions through generalized Noether principles, while observables are characterized as functions invariant under admissible transformations.

Relativity Mechanics thus provides a universal mathematical foundation for relational physics. It is not itself a theory of gravitation, quantum mechanics, gauge fields, or spacetime, but a theory describing how any physical theory should distinguish invariant physical content from arbitrary descriptive freedom. Existing theories emerge as realizations of this framework once their admissibility groups and associated dynamical structures are specified.

Its central principle is

\[
\boxed{
\text{Physical reality is represented by invariant equivalence classes of admissible descriptions, not by individual descriptions themselves.}
}
\]

In this formulation, relativity becomes a universal mathematical principle governing the relationship between description, symmetry, and physical information. Frame Theory, gauge theories, general relativity, and future relational physical theories arise as particular realizations of this more fundamental architecture, making Relativity Mechanics a candidate foundational framework for the systematic construction of physical theories.

# 1. Introduction

Every physical theory employs descriptions. A description may be a coordinate chart, a choice of reference frame, a gauge potential, a basis in an internal vector space, a quantum state relative to an observer, or a representation of a field configuration on a manifold. In almost all cases, the description contains more information than is physically meaningful. Two distinct descriptions may represent the same physical situation.

The central observation of Relativity Mechanics is that this redundancy is not an accidental feature of particular theories. It is a structural feature of physical description itself.

In Newtonian mechanics, the absolute position of an isolated system is not observable; only relative separations matter. In special relativity, the decomposition of spacetime into space and time depends on the inertial frame. In general relativity, the points of the differentiable manifold possess no physical identity independent of the fields defined upon them. In gauge theory, the local value of a gauge potential is not itself observable; only gauge-invariant quantities possess physical significance. In quantum theory, global phase is redundant, and in quantum reference-frame theory, even the decomposition into subsystems may depend on the chosen perspective.

These examples suggest a common architecture:

1. There exists a space of possible descriptions.
2. A group of transformations relates descriptions that are physically equivalent.
3. Physical reality is represented by the equivalence classes of descriptions under this group.
4. Observables are invariant functions on those equivalence classes.
5. Dynamics must be formulated so that it descends consistently to the quotient.

Relativity Mechanics makes this architecture explicit. It does not begin with spacetime geometry, quantum Hilbert spaces, or field equations. Instead, it begins with the relation between descriptions and the transformations that preserve physical content.

The purpose of this paper is to formulate that idea as a precise mathematical framework.

# 2. The Relativity Schema

## 2.1 Description spaces

Let \(\Omega\) denote the set of all admissible descriptions of a physical system. In different contexts, \(\Omega\) may be a finite-dimensional manifold, an infinite-dimensional function space, a space of sections of a bundle, a space of connections, a Hilbert space, or a categorical object.

**Definition 2.1.**  
A **description space** is a mathematical space \(\Omega\) whose elements \(\omega \in \Omega\) represent complete admissible descriptions of a physical system relative to some descriptive structure.

Examples include:

\[
\Omega = \mathbb{R}^{3N}
\]

for the positions of \(N\) particles,

\[
\Omega = \mathrm{Riem}(M)
\]

for Riemannian metrics on a manifold \(M\),

\[
\Omega = \mathcal{A}
\]

for the space of gauge connections, or

\[
\Omega = \mathcal{H}
\]

for a quantum state space.

A point \(\omega \in \Omega\) is not yet a physical state. It is a description.

---

## 2.2 Admissibility transformations

Let \(G\) be a group representing transformations of descriptions that do not alter physical content.

**Definition 2.2.**  
An **admissibility group** \(G\) is a group whose elements represent transformations between physically equivalent descriptions.

The group acts on \(\Omega\) by a left action

\[
\triangleright : G \times \Omega \rightarrow \Omega
\]

satisfying

\[
e \triangleright \omega = \omega,
\]

and

\[
g_1 \triangleright (g_2 \triangleright \omega)
=
(g_1 g_2) \triangleright \omega,
\]

for all \(g_1,g_2 \in G\) and \(\omega \in \Omega\).

The symbol \(\triangleright\) emphasizes that \(G\) acts on descriptions, not necessarily on physical states directly.

---

## 2.3 Orbits as physical states

The action of \(G\) partitions \(\Omega\) into orbits.

**Definition 2.3.**  
The **orbit** of \(\omega \in \Omega\) is

\[
[\omega] = G \cdot \omega
=
\{ g \triangleright \omega \mid g \in G \}.
\]

Two descriptions \(\omega_1,\omega_2 \in \Omega\) are physically equivalent if and only if there exists \(g \in G\) such that

\[
\omega_2 = g \triangleright \omega_1.
\]

The physical state is then the orbit itself:

\[
[\omega_1] = [\omega_2].
\]

The **isotropy subgroup** of \(\omega\) is

\[
G_\omega
=
\{ g \in G \mid g \triangleright \omega = \omega \}.
\]

If the action is not free, different points may have different isotropy groups. The orbit is then naturally identified with the homogeneous space

\[
[\omega] \cong G/G_\omega.
\]

The dimension of a smooth orbit is

\[
\dim [\omega]
=
\dim G - \dim G_\omega.
\]

---

## 2.4 The invariant projection

Let

\[
\Omega/G
\]

denote the orbit space. The canonical projection is

\[
I : \Omega \rightarrow \Omega/G,
\]

with

\[
I(\omega) = [\omega].
\]

The map \(I\) forgets all descriptive information that is not invariant under \(G\). It is the mathematical operation that extracts physical content from a description.

**Definition 2.4.**  
The space

\[
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{phys}}
=
\Omega/G
\]

is the **physical state space** of the relativity schema.

A point of \(\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{phys}}\) is not a particular description but an equivalence class of descriptions.

---

## 2.5 The relativity schema

The fundamental structure of Relativity Mechanics is the following.

**Definition 2.5.**  
A **relativity schema** is a quadruple

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal{R} = (\Omega, G, \triangleright, I)
}
\]

where:

1. \(\Omega\) is the description space;
2. \(G\) is the admissibility group;
3. \(\triangleright\) is the action of \(G\) on \(\Omega\);
4. \(I:\Omega \to \Omega/G\) is the invariant projection.

The central ontological postulate is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{A physical state is an orbit } [\omega] \in \Omega/G.
}
\]

Equivalently:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Physical reality is represented by invariant equivalence classes of admissible descriptions.}
}
\]

---

# 3. Observables and Invariant Structures

## 3.1 Invariant functions

A physical observable must assign the same value to all descriptions in the same orbit.

Let \(F : \Omega \rightarrow \mathbb{R}\) be a real-valued function. \(F\) is an observable if

\[
F(g \triangleright \omega) = F(\omega)
\]

for all \(g \in G\), \(\omega \in \Omega\).

The algebra of smooth invariant functions is

\[
C^\infty(\Omega)^G
=
\{ F \in C^\infty(\Omega) \mid F \circ g = F,\ \forall g \in G \}.
\]

When the quotient \(\Omega/G\) is a smooth manifold, one has the canonical identification

\[
C^\infty(\Omega/G)
\cong
C^\infty(\Omega)^G.
\]

Thus observables are functions on the invariant description space.

---

## 3.2 Relational observables

In many theories, invariants are most naturally constructed relationally. Given a set of reference quantities \(T^a(\omega)\), one may define relational observables by asking for the value of a quantity \(F\) when the reference quantities take specified values.

A formal expression is

\[
\mathcal{O}_{F,T}(\tau)
=
\left.
F(\omega)
\right|_{T(\omega)=\tau}.
\]

More invariantly, one may write

\[
\mathcal{O}_{F,T}(\tau)
=
\frac{
\displaystyle \int_G dg\,
F(g \triangleright \omega)\,
\delta(T(g \triangleright \omega)-\tau)
}{
\displaystyle \int_G dg\,
\delta(T(g \triangleright \omega)-\tau)
}.
\]

This construction appears in gauge theory, general relativity, and quantum reference-frame theory.

---

## 3.3 Invariant tensors and structures

If \(\Omega\) carries additional structures, physical structures are those invariant under \(G\).

For example, let \(h\) be a metric on \(\Omega\). The metric is admissible if

\[
(g^* h)_\omega
=
h_{g \triangleright \omega},
\]

or equivalently

\[
g^* h = h.
\]

If the action is free and proper, and if a \(G\)-invariant horizontal distribution is chosen, \(h\) induces a metric on the quotient.

Let

\[
V_\omega \Omega
=
\ker I_*
\]

be the vertical subspace tangent to the orbit, and let

\[
H_\omega \Omega
\]

be a horizontal complement:

\[
T_\omega \Omega
=
V_\omega \Omega \oplus H_\omega \Omega.
\]

If \(H\Omega\) is \(G\)-invariant and \(h\) is \(G\)-invariant, then for \(u,v \in T_{[\omega]}(\Omega/G)\), let \(\tilde u, \tilde v \in H_\omega \Omega\) be their horizontal lifts. The induced quotient metric is

\[
\boxed{
h_{\Omega/G}([\omega])(u,v)
=
h_\Omega(\tilde u, \tilde v).
}
\]

This construction is central in gauge theory, geometric mechanics, and the geometry of superspace in general relativity.

# 4. Geometry of the Orbit Space

## 4.1 Free and proper actions

If \(G\) is a Lie group acting smoothly, freely, and properly on a smooth manifold \(\Omega\), then \(\Omega/G\) is a smooth manifold and

\[
I : \Omega \rightarrow \Omega/G
\]

is a principal \(G\)-bundle.

In that case, the vertical bundle is

\[
V\Omega = \ker I_*,
\]

and the tangent bundle to the orbits is generated by fundamental vector fields.

For \(\xi \in \mathfrak{g} = \mathrm{Lie}(G)\), the fundamental vector field \(\xi_\Omega\) is defined by

\[
\xi_\Omega(\omega)
=
\left.
\frac{d}{dt}
\right|_{t=0}
\exp(t\xi) \triangleright \omega.
\]

The vertical space is

\[
V_\omega \Omega
=
\{ \xi_\Omega(\omega) \mid \xi \in \mathfrak{g} \}.
\]

---

## 4.2 Singular orbits and stratification

In many physical theories, the action is not free. For example:

- symmetric field configurations may have nontrivial stabilizers;
- gauge connections may be reducible;
- particle configurations may possess rotational symmetry;
- metrics may admit Killing vectors.

Then \(\Omega/G\) is generally not a smooth manifold but a stratified space.

For a subgroup \(H \subset G\), define the orbit-type stratum

\[
\Omega_{(H)}
=
\{ \omega \in \Omega \mid G_\omega \text{ is conjugate to } H \}.
\]

The quotient decomposes as

\[
\Omega/G
=
\bigsqcup_{(H)}
\Omega_{(H)}/G.
\]

Each stratum may be a manifold, but the full orbit space may possess singularities where isotropy type changes.

Relativity Mechanics does not require the quotient to be a manifold. The invariant projection

\[
I : \Omega \rightarrow \Omega/G
\]

remains well-defined even when \(\Omega/G\) is stratified, orbifold-like, or stacky.

---

## 4.3 Gauge fixing and sections

A gauge choice is a local or global section

\[
\sigma : \Omega/G \rightarrow \Omega
\]

such that

\[
I \circ \sigma = \mathrm{id}_{\Omega/G}.
\]

If a global section exists, the bundle is trivial. In most nontrivial gauge theories, no global section exists, and gauge fixing is only local.

A gauge-fixing condition may be written as

\[
F(\omega) = 0,
\]

where \(F : \Omega \rightarrow \mathfrak{g}^*\) is chosen such that each gauge orbit intersects the surface \(F=0\) once, at least locally.

The Faddeev-Popov determinant is

\[
\Delta_{\mathrm{FP}}(\omega)
=
\det
\left(
\left.
\frac{d}{dt}
\right|_{t=0}
F(\exp(t\xi)\triangleright \omega)
\right).
\]

This determinant measures the density of gauge orbits relative to the gauge-fixing surface.

---

# 5. Dynamics on Invariant Description Spaces

## 5.1 Equivariant evolution

A dynamical law on \(\Omega\) should not depend on the choice of representative within an orbit.

Let \(\Phi_t : \Omega \rightarrow \Omega\) be a flow. The flow is admissible if it commutes with the admissibility group:

\[
\boxed{
\Phi_t(g \triangleright \omega)
=
g \triangleright \Phi_t(\omega)
}
\]

for all \(g \in G\), \(\omega \in \Omega\), and all \(t\) for which the flow is defined.

Infinitesimally, if \(\Phi_t\) is generated by a vector field \(X\) on \(\Omega\), then

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\xi_\Omega} X = 0
\]

for all \(\xi \in \mathfrak{g}\). Equivalently,

\[
[\xi_\Omega, X] = 0.
\]

Such a vector field is \(G\)-invariant. If the action is sufficiently regular, \(X\) projects to a vector field \(X_{\mathrm{red}}\) on \(\Omega/G\) satisfying

\[
I_* X = X_{\mathrm{red}} \circ I.
\]

The reduced dynamics is then

\[
\frac{d}{dt} [\omega(t)]
=
X_{\mathrm{red}}([\omega(t)]).
\]

This is the first dynamical principle of Relativity Mechanics:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Physical evolution must be well-defined on } \Omega/G.
}
\]

---

## 5.2 Lagrangian formulation

Let \(L : T\Omega \rightarrow \mathbb{R}\) be a Lagrangian. The action functional is

\[
S[\gamma]
=
\int_{t_1}^{t_2}
L(\gamma(t), \dot{\gamma}(t))\,dt.
\]

The Lagrangian is admissible if it is invariant under the lifted action of \(G\) on \(T\Omega\):

\[
L(g_* v)
=
L(v),
\]

for all \(v \in T\Omega\).

Equivalently, for an infinitesimal transformation \(\xi \in \mathfrak{g}\),

\[
\delta_\xi q^i
=
\xi^i_\Omega(q),
\]

and

\[
\delta_\xi \dot q^i
=
\frac{d}{dt}\xi^i_\Omega(q).
\]

Invariance means

\[
\delta_\xi L = 0.
\]

The Euler-Lagrange equations are

\[
\frac{d}{dt}
\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q^i}
-
\frac{\partial L}{\partial q^i}
=
0.
\]

Because \(L\) is \(G\)-invariant, the dynamics is equivariant, and the physical trajectory in the quotient space is independent of the representative chosen.

---

## 5.3 Hamiltonian formulation

Let \((\Omega, \omega)\) be a symplectic manifold. Suppose \(G\) acts symplectically:

\[
g^* \omega = \omega.
\]

For \(\xi \in \mathfrak{g}\), the fundamental vector field is \(\xi_\Omega\). If there exists a map

\[
J : \Omega \rightarrow \mathfrak{g}^*
\]

such that

\[
\iota_{\xi_\Omega} \omega
=
d J_\xi,
\]

where

\[
J_\xi(\omega)
=
\langle J(\omega), \xi\rangle,
\]

then \(J\) is the **moment map**.

Let \(H : \Omega \rightarrow \mathbb{R}\) be a Hamiltonian. The Hamiltonian vector field \(X_H\) satisfies

\[
\iota_{X_H} \omega = dH.
\]

If \(H\) is \(G\)-invariant, then

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\xi_\Omega} H = 0.
\]

Using the moment-map identity,

\[
\frac{d}{dt} J_\xi
=
\{ J_\xi, H \}.
\]

Invariance implies

\[
\{ J_\xi, H \} = 0.
\]

Therefore,

\[
\boxed{
\frac{d}{dt} J_\xi = 0.
}
\]

This is the Hamiltonian form of Noether conservation in Relativity Mechanics.

---

## 5.4 Symplectic reduction

If \(J : \Omega \to \mathfrak{g}^*\) is a moment map and \(\mu \in \mathfrak{g}^*\) is a regular value, the Marsden-Weinstein reduced space is

\[
\boxed{
\Omega_\mu
=
J^{-1}(\mu)/G_\mu,
}
\]

where

\[
G_\mu
=
\{ g \in G \mid \mathrm{Ad}^*_g \mu = \mu \}.
\]

This is a special case of the general Relativity Mechanics principle: physical states live on quotient spaces obtained by dividing out admissible descriptive freedom.

---

# 6. Generalized Noether Theorem

## 6.1 Global admissibility symmetries

Let \(q^i(t)\) be coordinates on \(\Omega\), and let \(p_i = \partial L/\partial \dot q^i\). For an infinitesimal transformation generated by \(\xi \in \mathfrak{g}\),

\[
\delta q^i
=
\epsilon \xi^i_\Omega(q).
\]

The variation of the Lagrangian is

\[
\delta L
=
\frac{\partial L}{\partial q^i}
\delta q^i
+
\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q^i}
\delta \dot q^i.
\]

Since

\[
\delta \dot q^i
=
\frac{d}{dt} \delta q^i,
\]

we obtain

\[
\delta L
=
\frac{\partial L}{\partial q^i}
\xi^i_\Omega
+
p_i
\frac{d}{dt}\xi^i_\Omega.
\]

Using the Euler-Lagrange equations,

\[
\frac{\partial L}{\partial q^i}
=
\frac{d}{dt}
\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q^i}
=
\dot p_i,
\]

so

\[
\delta L
=
\dot p_i \xi^i_\Omega
+
p_i
\frac{d}{dt}\xi^i_\Omega
=
\frac{d}{dt}
\left(
p_i \xi^i_\Omega
\right).
\]

If the action is invariant, \(\delta L = dF_\xi/dt\) for some boundary function \(F_\xi\). The conserved Noether charge is

\[
\boxed{
Q_\xi
=
p_i \xi^i_\Omega
-
F_\xi.
}
\]

On-shell,

\[
\frac{d Q_\xi}{dt}
=
0.
\]

For a Lie group \(G\), this yields a set of conserved charges

\[
Q_\xi : T\Omega \rightarrow \mathbb{R},
\]

which assemble into the moment map when a symplectic structure is present.

---

## 6.2 Local admissibility symmetries

If the transformation parameters depend on spacetime or time,

\[
\epsilon = \epsilon(t),
\]

the symmetry is local. The variation becomes

\[
\delta q^i
=
\epsilon^a(t) \xi^i_a(q)
+
\dot \epsilon^a(t) \eta^i_a(q,\dot q)
+
\cdots.
\]

For a local gauge symmetry, invariance of the action for arbitrary \(\epsilon^a(t)\) implies Noether identities rather than ordinary conserved charges.

In field theory, for fields \(\phi^A(x)\), a local gauge transformation has the form

\[
\delta_\epsilon \phi^A
=
R^A_\alpha[\phi]\, \epsilon^\alpha
+
R^{A\mu}_\alpha[\phi]\, \partial_\mu \epsilon^\alpha
+
\cdots.
\]

Invariance implies identities of the form

\[
\frac{\delta S}{\delta \phi^A}
R^A_\alpha
-
\partial_\mu
\left(
\frac{\delta S}{\delta \phi^A}
R^{A\mu}_\alpha
\right)
+
\cdots
=
0.
\]

These identities generate constraints. Thus local admissibility symmetries correspond to constrained dynamics.

The Hamiltonian form is expressed through first-class constraints

\[
C_\alpha \approx 0,
\]

satisfying

\[
\{ C_\alpha, C_\beta \}
=
f_{\alpha\beta}{}^\gamma C_\gamma.
\]

Physical observables \(O\) must satisfy

\[
\boxed{
\{ O, C_\alpha \} \approx 0.
}
\]

This is the Hamiltonian expression of invariance under local admissibility transformations.

# 7. Hierarchy of Admissibility Groups

Relativity Mechanics organizes physical theories according to the size and nature of their admissibility groups.

## 7.1 Trivial admissibility

If

\[
G = \{e\},
\]

then

\[
\Omega/G = \Omega.
\]

Every descriptive detail is treated as physical. This corresponds to an absolute, nonrelational ontology.

---

## 7.2 Spatial translations

Let

\[
\Omega = \mathbb{R}^{3N},
\]

and let

\[
G = \mathbb{R}^3
\]

act by common translations:

\[
x_i \mapsto x_i + a.
\]

The quotient removes absolute position. Invariant quantities are relative separations:

\[
r_{ij}
=
x_i - x_j.
\]

The physical state is not the set of absolute positions \(\{x_i\}\), but the equivalence class under translations.

---

## 7.3 Euclidean transformations

If

\[
G = SE(3) = \mathbb{R}^3 \rtimes SO(3),
\]

then absolute position and absolute orientation are removed. Invariants include distances:

\[
d_{ij}^2
=
\delta_{ab}
(x_i^a - x_j^a)
(x_i^b - x_j^b).
\]

The quotient is the space of shapes of the \(N\)-body system, modulo rigid motions.

---

## 7.4 Galilean relativity

For Galilean inertial frames,

\[
G = \mathrm{Gal}(3),
\]

the transformations include spatial translations, rotations, boosts, and time translations. Invariant quantities include relative accelerations, time intervals, and spatial distances between simultaneous events.

---

## 7.5 Poincaré relativity

For special relativity,

\[
G = ISO(1,3),
\]

the Poincaré group. The fundamental invariant is

\[
s^2
=
\eta_{\mu\nu}
\Delta x^\mu \Delta x^\nu.
\]

The quotient by Poincaré transformations removes absolute inertial frame and origin.

---

## 7.6 Diffeomorphism invariance

In general relativity,

\[
G = \mathrm{Diff}(M),
\]

the group of diffeomorphisms of the spacetime manifold \(M\). Coordinates are descriptive labels. The physical state is not a metric \(g_{\mu\nu}\) on a labeled manifold, but the equivalence class

\[
[g_{\mu\nu}]
=
\{ \phi^* g_{\mu\nu} \mid \phi \in \mathrm{Diff}(M) \}.
\]

---

## 7.7 Gauge transformations

For a principal bundle \(P \to M\) with structure group \(K\), the space of connections is

\[
\Omega = \mathcal{A}.
\]

The gauge group is

\[
G = \mathrm{Gau}(P)
\cong
\{ h : M \to K \}.
\]

Gauge transformations act by

\[
A \mapsto h^{-1} A h + h^{-1} d h.
\]

The physical configuration space is

\[
\mathcal{A}/\mathrm{Gau}(P).
\]

---

## 7.8 Local frame transformations

In tetrad or frame formulations, the description includes a local frame field. The admissibility group may be a local Lorentz group:

\[
G = C^\infty(M, SO(1,3)).
\]

The physical content is invariant under local rotations and boosts of the frame.

---

## 7.9 Quantum reference-frame transformations

In quantum theory, descriptions may include decompositions into subsystems, phases, or reference systems. The admissibility group may act unitarily on a Hilbert space:

\[
\omega \mapsto U(g)\omega.
\]

Physical observables are invariant under the corresponding conjugation:

\[
O \mapsto U(g) O U(g)^\dagger.
\]

Quantum reference-frame theories arise by allowing the reference system itself to be quantum and by treating changes of reference frame as admissibility transformations.

---

# 8. Classical Relational Mechanics as a Realization

Consider \(N\) particles in Euclidean space. Let

\[
\Omega = \mathbb{R}^{3N},
\]

with coordinates

\[
x_i^a,
\qquad
i=1,\dots,N,
\qquad
a=1,2,3.
\]

Let the admissibility group be the Euclidean group:

\[
G = SE(3).
\]

The action is

\[
x_i^a
\mapsto
R^a{}_b x_i^b + a^a,
\]

where \(R \in SO(3)\), \(a \in \mathbb{R}^3\).

The invariant relative vectors are

\[
r_{ij}^a
=
x_i^a - x_j^a.
\]

The invariant distances are

\[
d_{ij}^2
=
\delta_{ab} r_{ij}^a r_{ij}^b.
\]

If the Lagrangian is

\[
L
=
\frac{1}{2}
\sum_{i=1}^N
m_i \delta_{ab}
\dot x_i^a \dot x_i^b
-
V(\{d_{ij}\}),
\]

then \(L\) is invariant under \(SE(3)\). The physical configuration space is

\[
\mathcal{Q}
=
\mathbb{R}^{3N}/SE(3).
\]

For \(N \geq 3\), ignoring collisions and singular configurations, the dimension of shape space is

\[
\dim \mathcal{Q}
=
3N - 6.
\]

Translations give conservation of total momentum:

\[
P^a
=
\sum_i m_i \dot x_i^a.
\]

Rotations give conservation of total angular momentum:

\[
J^a
=
\sum_{i,j}
\epsilon^{a}{}_{bc}
m_i x_i^b \dot x_i^c.
\]

In Relativity Mechanics, these conservation laws are consequences of the admissibility group acting on the description space.

# 9. Special Relativity as a Relativity Schema

In special relativity, descriptions are given relative to inertial coordinate systems. Let

\[
\Omega
\]

contain coordinate representations of events and worldlines. The admissibility group is the Poincaré group:

\[
G = ISO(1,3).
\]

An element \(g = (\Lambda, a)\) acts as

\[
x^\mu
\mapsto
x'^\mu
=
\Lambda^\mu{}_\nu x^\nu
+
a^\mu,
\]

with

\[
\eta_{\mu\nu}
\Lambda^\mu{}_\alpha
\Lambda^\nu{}_\beta
=
\eta_{\alpha\beta}.
\]

The invariant interval is

\[
ds^2
=
\eta_{\mu\nu} dx^\mu dx^\nu.
\]

For a massive point particle, the action may be written

\[
S[x]
=
-mc
\int
\sqrt{
-\eta_{\mu\nu}
\dot x^\mu \dot x^\nu
}
\, d\lambda.
\]

This action is invariant under Poincaré transformations. The physical worldline is not the coordinate tuple \(x^\mu(\lambda)\) but its equivalence class under Poincaré transformations.

The canonical momentum is

\[
p_\mu
=
\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot x^\mu}
=
m
\frac{\eta_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\nu}
{\sqrt{-\eta_{\alpha\beta}\dot x^\alpha \dot x^\beta}}.
\]

The mass-shell invariant is

\[
\eta^{\mu\nu}p_\mu p_\nu
=
-m^2 c^2.
\]

Thus special relativity is a relativity schema with Poincaré admissibility.

# 10. General Relativity as a Relativity Schema

General relativity provides one of the deepest examples of Relativity Mechanics.

Let \(M\) be a smooth four-dimensional manifold. Let

\[
\Omega = \mathrm{Lor}(M)
\]

denote the space of Lorentzian metrics \(g_{\mu\nu}\) on \(M\). The admissibility group is

\[
G = \mathrm{Diff}(M).
\]

A diffeomorphism \(\phi : M \to M\) acts by pullback:

\[
g_{\mu\nu}
\mapsto
\phi^* g_{\mu\nu}.
\]

Infinitesimally, for a vector field \(\xi^\mu\),

\[
\delta_\xi g_{\mu\nu}
=
\mathcal{L}_\xi g_{\mu\nu}
=
\nabla_\mu \xi_\nu
+
\nabla_\nu \xi_\mu.
\]

The Einstein-Hilbert action is

\[
S_{\mathrm{EH}}[g]
=
\frac{1}{2\kappa}
\int_M
d^4x\,
\sqrt{-g}
\left(
R - 2\Lambda
\right),
\]

where

\[
\kappa = 8\pi G.
\]

Variation gives

\[
\delta S_{\mathrm{EH}}
=
\frac{1}{2\kappa}
\int_M
d^4x\,
\sqrt{-g}
\left(
G_{\mu\nu}
+
\Lambda g_{\mu\nu}
\right)
\delta g^{\mu\nu}
+
\text{boundary terms}.
\]

The field equations are

\[
G_{\mu\nu}
+
\Lambda g_{\mu\nu}
=
\kappa T_{\mu\nu}.
\]

Diffeomorphism invariance implies the contracted Bianchi identity:

\[
\nabla_\mu G^{\mu\nu}
=
0.
\]

This is a Noether identity associated with local admissibility transformations.

The physical configuration space is

\[
\mathrm{Superspace}(M)
=
\mathrm{Lor}(M)/\mathrm{Diff}(M).
\]

A point in superspace is not a metric on a manifold with labeled points, but a diffeomorphism equivalence class of geometries.

---

## 10.1 ADM constraints

In the canonical formulation, spacetime is foliated by spatial hypersurfaces \(\Sigma_t\). The spatial metric is \(h_{ij}\), and its conjugate momentum is \(\pi^{ij}\). The Hamiltonian and momentum constraints are

\[
\mathcal{H}
=
\frac{2\kappa}{\sqrt{h}}
\left(
\pi^{ij}\pi_{ij}
-
\frac{1}{2}\pi^2
\right)
-
\frac{\sqrt{h}}{2\kappa}
{}^{(3)}R
\approx 0,
\]

and

\[
\mathcal{H}_i
=
-2 D_j \pi^j{}_i
\approx 0.
\]

These constraints generate diffeomorphism admissibility. Physical observables must satisfy

\[
\{ O, \mathcal{H} \} \approx 0,
\]

and

\[
\{ O, \mathcal{H}_i \} \approx 0.
\]

Thus general relativity is an infinite-dimensional relativity schema.

---

## 10.2 Relational observables in general relativity

Because diffeomorphism-invariant observables cannot depend directly on coordinate labels, physical observables are relational.

Let \(T(x)\) be a scalar field used as a clock, and let \(F(x)\) be another scalar quantity. A relational observable may be defined by

\[
\mathcal{O}_{F,T}(\tau)
=
\int_M d^4x\,
\sqrt{-g}
F(x)
\delta(T(x)-\tau).
\]

More precisely, after appropriate normalization,

\[
\mathcal{O}_{F,T}(\tau)
=
\frac{
\int_M d^4x\,
\sqrt{-g}
F(x)
\delta(T(x)-\tau)
}{
\int_M d^4x\,
\sqrt{-g}
\delta(T(x)-\tau)
}.
\]

This quantity is invariant under diffeomorphisms and therefore qualifies as an observable in Relativity Mechanics.

# 11. Gauge Theory as a Relativity Schema

Let \(P \to M\) be a principal bundle with structure group \(K\). The space of connections is

\[
\Omega = \mathcal{A}.
\]

A connection may be locally represented by a Lie-algebra-valued one-form

\[
A = A_\mu dx^\mu.
\]

The curvature is

\[
F = dA + A \wedge A,
\]

or in components,

\[
F_{\mu\nu}
=
\partial_\mu A_\nu
-
\partial_\nu A_\mu
+
[A_\mu, A_\nu].
\]

The gauge group is

\[
G = \mathrm{Gau}(P).
\]

A local gauge transformation \(h : M \to K\) acts as

\[
A
\mapsto
A^h
=
h^{-1} A h
+
h^{-1} d h.
\]

The curvature transforms covariantly:

\[
F
\mapsto
h^{-1} F h.
\]

The Yang-Mills action is

\[
S_{\mathrm{YM}}[A]
=
-\frac{1}{2g^2}
\int_M
\mathrm{tr}
\left(
F \wedge *F
\right).
\]

The equations of motion are

\[
D * F = 0,
\]

where

\[
D = d + [A, \cdot]
\]

is the gauge-covariant derivative.

The physical configuration space is

\[
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{phys}}
=
\mathcal{A}/\mathrm{Gau}(P).
\]

Gauge-invariant observables include Wilson loops:

\[
W_R(C)
=
\mathrm{Tr}_R
\mathcal{P}
\exp
\left(
\oint_C A
\right).
\]

Here \(C\) is a closed curve, \(R\) is a representation of \(K\), and \(\mathcal{P}\) denotes path ordering.

In canonical gauge theory, the Gauss law constraint is

\[
D_i E^i = 0,
\]

where \(E^i\) is the chromoelectric field. Physical states satisfy

\[
D_i E^i \, |\psi\rangle = 0.
\]

This is the quantum expression of gauge admissibility.

# 12. Frame Theory and Local Frames

Frame theories make the descriptive role of reference structures explicit. Let \(M\) be a spacetime manifold. A local frame field, or tetrad, is a set of four linearly independent vector fields

\[
e_I = e_I{}^\mu \partial_\mu,
\]

or equivalently co-frame one-forms

\[
e^I = e^I{}_\mu dx^\mu.
\]

The metric is constructed from the tetrad:

\[
g_{\mu\nu}
=
\eta_{IJ}
e^I{}_\mu
e^J{}_\nu,
\]

where

\[
\eta_{IJ}
=
\mathrm{diag}(-1,1,1,1).
\]

Local Lorentz transformations act as

\[
e^I{}_\mu
\mapsto
\Lambda^I{}_J(x)
e^J{}_\mu,
\]

with

\[
\Lambda^I{}_K
\Lambda^J{}_L
\eta_{IJ}
=
\eta_{KL}.
\]

The metric is invariant:

\[
\eta_{IJ}
e'^I{}_\mu
e'^J{}_\nu
=
\eta_{IJ}
e^I{}_\mu
e^J{}_\nu.
\]

Thus the local Lorentz frame is a descriptive structure, while the metric and curvature invariants represent physical content.

The spin connection \(\omega_\mu{}^{IJ}\) transforms inhomogeneously:

\[
\omega_\mu
\mapsto
\Lambda \omega_\mu \Lambda^{-1}
+
\Lambda \partial_\mu \Lambda^{-1}.
\]

The curvature two-form is

\[
R^{IJ}
=
d\omega^{IJ}
+
\omega^I{}_K \wedge \omega^{KJ}.
\]

Frame theory is therefore a relativity schema with local Lorentz transformations as part of the admissibility group.

# 13. Quantum Relativity Schemas

In quantum theory, descriptions may be vectors, density matrices, or algebraic states. Let \(\mathcal{H}\) be a Hilbert space. Suppose a group \(G\) acts unitarily:

\[
g \mapsto U(g).
\]

If the action represents descriptive redundancy, then states related by \(U(g)\) are physically equivalent.

For pure states,

\[
|\psi\rangle
\sim
U(g)|\psi\rangle.
\]

For density operators,

\[
\rho
\sim
U(g)\rho U(g)^\dagger.
\]

Physical observables must be invariant:

\[
O
=
U(g)^\dagger O U(g),
\]

or infinitesimally,

\[
[O, \hat{Q}_\xi] = 0,
\]

where \(\hat{Q}_\xi\) are the Hermitian generators of \(G\).

If the admissibility group is generated by constraints \(\hat{C}_\alpha\), physical states satisfy

\[
\boxed{
\hat{C}_\alpha |\psi\rangle = 0.
}
\]

Physical observables satisfy

\[
[\hat{O}, \hat{C}_\alpha] = 0,
\]

at least weakly.

In quantum gauge theory, this becomes the condition that physical states are gauge-invariant. In quantum gravity, it becomes the Wheeler-DeWitt-type condition

\[
\hat{\mathcal{H}} |\Psi\rangle = 0,
\]

and

\[
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_i |\Psi\rangle = 0.
\]

In quantum reference-frame theory, the group \(G\) may relate different quantum decompositions of a total system into observer and observed. The invariant content is the equivalence class of descriptions under those transformations.

# 14. Quantization of Relativity Schemas

There are several routes to quantizing a relativity schema.

## 14.1 Reduce then quantize

First form the classical quotient

\[
\Omega_{\mathrm{phys}}
=
\Omega/G,
\]

or the symplectic quotient

\[
\Omega_\mu
=
J^{-1}(\mu)/G_\mu.
\]

Then quantize the reduced space.

In favorable cases, the quantum Hilbert space is

\[
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{phys}}
=
L^2(\Omega/G, d\mu_{\mathrm{red}}).
\]

This procedure is conceptually direct but may be difficult when \(\Omega/G\) is singular.

---

## 14.2 Quantize then reduce

Alternatively, quantize \(\Omega\) first to obtain a kinematical Hilbert space \(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{kin}}\). Then impose invariance:

\[
U(g)|\psi\rangle = |\psi\rangle,
\]

for all \(g \in G\).

Equivalently, for Lie generators,

\[
\hat{Q}_\xi |\psi\rangle = 0.
\]

For constrained systems,

\[
\hat{C}_\alpha |\psi\rangle = 0.
\]

The physical Hilbert space is

\[
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{phys}}
=
\{ |\psi\rangle \in \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{kin}}
\mid
U(g)|\psi\rangle = |\psi\rangle,
\ \forall g \in G
\}.
\]

---

## 14.3 Path-integral quantization

The formal path integral over descriptions is

\[
Z
=
\int_{\Omega/G}
\mathcal{D}[\omega]\,
e^{\frac{i}{\hbar} S[\omega]}.
\]

In practice, one integrates over \(\Omega\) and divides by the gauge volume:

\[
Z
=
\int_{\Omega}
\frac{\mathcal{D}\omega}{\mathrm{vol}(G)}
e^{\frac{i}{\hbar} S[\omega]}.
\]

After gauge fixing \(F(\omega)=0\), this becomes

\[
Z
=
\int \mathcal{D}\omega\,
\delta(F(\omega))
\Delta_{\mathrm{FP}}(\omega)
e^{\frac{i}{\hbar} S[\omega]}.
\]

The Faddeev-Popov determinant is

\[
\Delta_{\mathrm{FP}}(\omega)
=
\det
\left(
\frac{\delta F(g\triangleright \omega)}
{\delta g}
\right)_{g=e}.
\]

BRST quantization provides a cohomological formulation in which physical states are BRST cohomology classes.

---

## 14.4 Anomalies

A quantum anomaly occurs when a classical admissibility symmetry cannot be implemented in the quantum theory.

Classically, one expects

\[
\{ C_\alpha, C_\beta \}
=
f_{\alpha\beta}{}^\gamma C_\gamma.
\]

Quantum mechanically, one may obtain

\[
[\hat{C}_\alpha, \hat{C}_\beta]
=
i f_{\alpha\beta}{}^\gamma \hat{C}_\gamma
+
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha\beta}.
\]

If

\[
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha\beta} \neq 0,
\]

the admissibility group is anomalous. Consistent physical theories require anomaly cancellation or modification of the admissibility structure.

# 15. Categorical and Higher Extensions

The basic relativity schema can be generalized categorically.

## 15.1 Morphisms of relativity schemas

Let

\[
\mathcal{R}_1 = (\Omega_1, G_1, \triangleright_1, I_1),
\]

and

\[
\mathcal{R}_2 = (\Omega_2, G_2, \triangleright_2, I_2).
\]

A morphism consists of a map

\[
F : \Omega_1 \to \Omega_2
\]

and a group homomorphism

\[
\phi : G_1 \to G_2
\]

such that

\[
F(g \triangleright_1 \omega)
=
\phi(g) \triangleright_2 F(\omega).
\]

This condition says that \(F\) sends equivalent descriptions to equivalent descriptions. It induces a map on quotient spaces:

\[
\bar F : \Omega_1/G_1 \to \Omega_2/G_2.
\]

Thus physical theories may be related by structure-preserving maps between relativity schemas.

---

## 15.2 Groupoids and stacks

In many contexts, the admissibility relation is better described by a groupoid rather than a group. This occurs when transformations are only locally defined, when multiple types of descriptions are involved, or when the quotient is singular.

One may replace \(G\) by an action groupoid

\[
G \ltimes \Omega.
\]

The quotient is then more properly written as the stack

\[
[\Omega/G].
\]

The stack retains stabilizer information lost by the naive orbit space.

Thus, in singular cases, the physical state space is not merely

\[
\Omega/G,
\]

but the quotient stack

\[
[\Omega/G].
\]

This is especially relevant for gauge theories with reducible connections and for moduli spaces with orbifold points.

---

## 15.3 Higher symmetries

In higher gauge theory and in some formulations of quantum gravity, admissibility transformations may act not only on objects but also on morphisms between objects. One then obtains higher relativity schemas:

\[
\mathcal{R}
=
(\Omega, \mathcal{G}, \triangleright, I),
\]

where \(\mathcal{G}\) is a higher group or gauge \(n\)-group.

The quotient becomes a higher quotient,

\[
\Omega/\mathcal{G},
\]

and observables are higher-categorical invariants.

# 16. Theory Construction in Relativity Mechanics

Relativity Mechanics provides a systematic procedure for constructing physical theories.

## Step 1: Choose the description space

Select \(\Omega\), the space of admissible descriptions.

Examples:

\[
\Omega = \mathbb{R}^{3N},\quad
\mathrm{Riem}(M),\quad
\mathcal{A},\quad
\mathcal{H}.
\]

## Step 2: Identify the admissibility group

Choose \(G\) and its action

\[
\triangleright : G \times \Omega \to \Omega.
\]

This determines which differences between descriptions are physically meaningless.

## Step 3: Construct the quotient

Form

\[
\Omega/G.
\]

Identify its smooth, stratified, or stacky structure.

## Step 4: Determine invariant observables

Compute

\[
C^\infty(\Omega)^G.
\]

These are the candidate physical observables.

## Step 5: Define invariant dynamics

Choose an action functional \(S\), Hamiltonian \(H\), or evolution operator that is invariant under \(G\).

Require

\[
S[g\triangleright \omega] = S[\omega],
\]

or

\[
H(g\triangleright \omega) = H(\omega),
\]

or

\[
U(g)^\dagger \hat{H} U(g) = \hat{H}.
\]

## Step 6: Reduce and solve

Either reduce first and then solve the dynamics on \(\Omega/G\), or solve in \(\Omega\) and impose invariance.

## Step 7: Interpret physically

Physical states are orbits:

\[
[\omega] \in \Omega/G.
\]

Physical predictions are invariant relations among observables.

This algorithm replaces the traditional procedure of postulating absolute objects and equations. Instead, one postulates descriptive freedom and invariant content.

# 17. Foundational Principles of Relativity Mechanics

The framework may be summarized by several principles.

## Principle 1: Descriptive pluralism

A physical system may admit many admissible descriptions.

\[
\omega, \omega' \in \Omega.
\]

No description is automatically privileged.

---

## Principle 2: Admissibility equivalence

Descriptions related by the admissibility group are physically equivalent:

\[
\omega' = g \triangleright \omega
\implies
[\omega'] = [\omega].
\]

---

## Principle 3: Orbit ontology

The physical state is the orbit:

\[
[\omega] = G \cdot \omega.
\]

Not the representative, but the equivalence class, is real.

---

## Principle 4: Invariant observables

Observables satisfy

\[
F(g\triangleright \omega) = F(\omega).
\]

Only invariant quantities are physically measurable.

---

## Principle 5: Projectable dynamics

Dynamics must descend to the quotient:

\[
\Phi_t(g\triangleright \omega)
=
g\triangleright \Phi_t(\omega).
\]

Equivalently,

\[
I_* X = X_{\mathrm{red}} \circ I.
\]

---

## Principle 6: Conservation from admissibility

Continuous admissibility transformations imply conserved charges or constraints.

For global symmetries:

\[
\frac{dQ_\xi}{dt} = 0.
\]

For local symmetries:

\[
C_\alpha \approx 0.
\]

---

## Principle 7: Theory relativity

Different physical theories correspond to different choices of \((\Omega,G,\triangleright,I)\).

Thus the same physical system may admit multiple relativity schemas depending on which descriptive freedoms are treated as physically irrelevant.

---

General covariance is a special case of Relativity Mechanics in which the admissibility group contains diffeomorphisms. Relativity Mechanics explains why covariance is not merely a coordinate property but an ontological statement about the equivalence of descriptions.

## 18.2 Gauge symmetry

Gauge symmetry is the statement that local internal descriptions are redundant. Relativity Mechanics treats gauge transformations as admissibility transformations.

## 18.3 Symplectic reduction

Marsden-Weinstein reduction is a special case of quotienting by admissibility transformations in Hamiltonian mechanics.

## 18.4 Shape dynamics and relational mechanics

Shape dynamics and relational particle mechanics arise by choosing large spatial similarity groups as admissibility groups.

## 18.5 Quantum reference frames

Quantum reference-frame theory corresponds to allowing the admissibility group to act on quantum subsystem decompositions.

## 18.6 Background independence

Background independence can be understood as the absence of fixed non-dynamical structures in \(\Omega\), or as the inclusion of transformations of those structures in \(G\).

# 19. Interpretation: Orbit-Based Ontology

The central philosophical consequence of Relativity Mechanics is the replacement of object-based ontology by orbit-based ontology.

In ordinary formulations, one says:

\[
\text{The physical state is } \omega.
\]

Relativity Mechanics instead says:

\[
\text{The physical state is } [\omega].
\]

This has several consequences.

First, quantities that vary along orbits are not physical. They are descriptive coordinates.

Second, physical laws must be formulated as relations between invariants.

Third, apparent paradoxes arising from gauge freedom, coordinate dependence, or observer dependence are resolved by recognizing that the conflicting descriptions lie in the same orbit.

Fourth, the notion of an absolute background structure is reinterpreted as a choice of section or representative. Such a choice may be useful computationally, but it is not ontologically fundamental unless its invariants are physically measurable.

Thus the primitive ontological category is not “thing” or “field value at a point,” but equivalence class under admissible transformations.

# 21. Limitations and Open Problems

Relativity Mechanics is a structural framework, not a complete replacement for all physical content. Several issues remain central in applications.

1. **Choice of admissibility group.**  
   The framework does not by itself determine \(G\). Physical input is required.

2. **Quotient singularities.**  
   When the action is not free, \(\Omega/G\) may be singular. A complete treatment often requires stratified geometry or stacks.

3. **Quantum anomalies.**  
   Classical admissibility may fail to survive quantization.

4. **Observables in generally covariant theories.**  
   Constructing complete sets of relational observables remains difficult in quantum gravity.

5. **Dynamical emergence of admissibility.**  
   In some future theory, the admissibility group itself may be emergent rather than fundamental.

6. **Measurement and partial descriptions.**  
   A full account of measurement requires extending the schema to include observers, records, and information-theoretic constraints.

These are not objections to the framework but research directions within it.

# 22. Conclusion

Relativity Mechanics proposes a universal mathematical foundation for physical theories based on the equivalence of descriptions.

Its basic structure is the relativity schema

\[
\mathcal{R}
=
(\Omega, G, \triangleright, I),
\]

where \(\Omega\) is a space of descriptions, \(G\) is the admissibility group, \(\triangleright\) is its action, and

\[
I : \Omega \to \Omega/G
\]

projects descriptions to their invariant physical content.

The central ontological claim is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Physical states are orbits } [\omega] = G \cdot \omega.
}
\]

The central epistemological claim is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Observables are invariant functions on } \Omega/G.
}
\]

The central dynamical claim is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Physical evolution must descend to } \Omega/G.
}
\]

Within this framework, Galilean mechanics, special relativity, general relativity, gauge theories, frame theories, and quantum reference-frame theories are not unrelated constructions. They are different realizations of the same principle: physical reality is not a particular description, but the invariant equivalence class of admissible descriptions.

Relativity Mechanics therefore elevates relativity from a doctrine about spacetime frames to a universal mathematical principle governing the relation between description, symmetry, and physical content.

The final principle is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Physical reality is represented by invariant equivalence classes of admissible descriptions, not by individual descriptions themselves.}
}
\]
