# Relativity 27.0 — Symmetry Relativity  
## The Relativity of Symmetry, Duality, and Categorical Invariance

## Abstract

Symmetry Relativity is the hypothesis that symmetry itself is not absolute. In modern theoretical physics, symmetry is no longer adequately described by an ordinary group acting on fields. The full symmetry structure of a theory may include higher-form symmetries, higher-group symmetries, non-invertible topological defects, categorical symmetries, duality symmetries, subsystem symmetries, and emergent infrared symmetries. The invariant object is not a particular symmetry group \(G\), but the full symmetry structure,

\[
\mathcal{S}(T)
=
\text{Sym}(T),
\]

including its defect algebra, fusion rules, anomaly class, and categorical coherence data. In this framework, the distinction between symmetry and duality is not fundamental. A symmetry may be invertible or non-invertible, exact or emergent, ultraviolet or infrared, global or gauged, ordinary or generalized. The central principle is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Symmetry is not a fixed background structure; it is part of the relational content of a theory.}
}
\]

Symmetry Relativity extends the relativistic program once more. Just as coordinates, frames, geometry, locality, and descriptions are not privileged, neither is a particular presentation of symmetry privileged. Physical reality is encoded in the invariant categorical and anomalous structure shared by all equivalent symmetry presentations.

## 1. Introduction

Symmetry has long been the organizing principle of modern physics.

Noether’s theorem relates continuous symmetries to conservation laws. Gauge symmetry organizes interactions. Lorentz symmetry organizes spacetime. Conformal symmetry organizes critical phenomena. Supersymmetry relates bosons and fermions. Dualities relate apparently different theories.

But the concept of symmetry has expanded dramatically.

A symmetry is no longer merely a group \(G\) acting on fields. It may be:

1. a higher-form symmetry acting on extended operators,
2. a higher-group mixing symmetries of different form degree,
3. a non-invertible categorical symmetry,
4. a duality symmetry relating different descriptions,
5. a subsystem symmetry acting on lower-dimensional subregions,
6. an emergent infrared symmetry absent in the ultraviolet,
7. an anomalous symmetry that cannot be consistently gauged,
8. a boundary condition of a higher-dimensional symmetry topological field theory.

The modern view is that the symmetry of a theory is not a group. It is a structured algebraic object:

\[
\mathcal{S}(T),
\]

often a fusion category, higher category, or symmetry topological field theory, together with anomaly data.

Symmetry Relativity says that this structure, not any particular presentation of it, is physical.

## 2. Ordinary Symmetries and Noether’s Theorem

An ordinary global symmetry is a group \(G\) acting on fields \(\phi\):

\[
\phi(x)
\rightarrow
g\cdot\phi(x),
\qquad
g\in G.
\]

If the action \(S[\phi]\) is invariant, then for a continuous symmetry there is a conserved Noether current,

\[
J^\mu_a,
\]

satisfying

\[
\partial_\mu J^\mu_a=0.
\]

The conserved charge is

\[
Q_a
=
\int_{\Sigma}
d^{d-1}x\,
J^0_a.
\]

The charge generates the symmetry:

\[
[Q_a,\phi]
=
-i\delta_a\phi.
\]

Equivalently, one may define topological symmetry operators on closed codimension-one manifolds:

\[
U_g(\Sigma_{d-1})
=
\exp
\left(
i
\int_{\Sigma_{d-1}}
\alpha_a J^a
\right),
\]

where \(g=e^{i\alpha_a T^a}\).

Conservation implies topological invariance:

\[
\frac{\delta}{\delta \Sigma}
U_g(\Sigma)
=
0.
\]

The operators obey the group law:

\[
U_g(\Sigma)
U_h(\Sigma)
=
U_{gh}(\Sigma).
\]

This is the classical notion of symmetry.

Symmetry Relativity does not discard this structure. It generalizes it.

## 3. Symmetry as Topological Defects

A deeper viewpoint is that symmetries are topological defects.

An ordinary \(0\)-form symmetry is generated by topological codimension-one operators \(U_g(\Sigma_{d-1})\).

The defining property is topological invariance:

\[
U_g(\Sigma)
=
U_g(\Sigma')
\]

whenever \(\Sigma\) can be continuously deformed to \(\Sigma'\) without crossing charged operators.

The group structure becomes a fusion rule:

\[
U_g
\times
U_h
=
U_{gh}.
\]

Every symmetry operator is invertible:

\[
U_g^{-1}
=
U_{g^{-1}}.
\]

Thus ordinary symmetries are the invertible sector of a larger defect algebra.

Symmetry Relativity begins by recognizing that invertible group-like defects are only the simplest possible topological defects.

## 4. Higher-Form Symmetries

A \(p\)-form symmetry acts on \(p\)-dimensional extended operators rather than local operators.

The charged objects are operators

\[
W(C_p),
\]

supported on \(p\)-dimensional manifolds \(C_p\).

Examples include:

- Wilson lines in gauge theory,
- ’t Hooft lines,
- surface operators,
- string worldsheet operators,
- membrane operators.

A \(p\)-form symmetry has a conserved \((p+1)\)-form current,

\[
J_{p+1},
\]

satisfying

\[
d{*}J_{p+1}=0.
\]

The conserved charge is supported on a closed codimension-\((p+1)\) manifold:

\[
Q(M_{d-p-1})
=
\int_{M_{d-p-1}}
{*}J_{p+1}.
\]

The corresponding topological symmetry operator is

\[
U_g(M_{d-p-1}).
\]

For an abelian \(p\)-form symmetry group \(G^{(p)}\), the operators obey

\[
U_g(M)
U_h(M)
=
U_{gh}(M).
\]

The superscript \((p)\) indicates that the symmetry acts on \(p\)-dimensional operators.

## 5. Example: One-Form Symmetries in Gauge Theory

In four-dimensional pure \(SU(N)\) Yang–Mills theory, there is a center one-form symmetry,

\[
\mathbb{Z}_N^{(1)}.
\]

Wilson lines in representation \(R\) transform under the center of \(SU(N)\). A Wilson line with \(N\)-ality \(k\) transforms as

\[
W_R(C)
\rightarrow
e^{2\pi i k/N}
W_R(C).
\]

The one-form symmetry acts on line operators, not local operators.

The order parameter for confinement is the behavior of Wilson loops:

\[
\langle W(C)\rangle
\sim
e^{-\text{Area}(C)}
\]

in the confining phase,

and

\[
\langle W(C)\rangle
\sim
e^{-\text{Perimeter}(C)}
\]

in the deconfined or Higgs phase.

The realization or breaking of the one-form symmetry distinguishes phases.

Thus generalized symmetries are not abstract. They classify physical phases.

## 6. Electric and Magnetic One-Form Symmetries

Free Maxwell theory in four dimensions has two conserved two-form currents:

\[
J_e
=
{*}F,
\]

\[
J_m
=
F.
\]

Maxwell’s equations imply

\[
d{*}F=0,
\qquad
dF=0.
\]

Therefore the theory has a \(U(1)_e^{(1)}\) electric one-form symmetry and a \(U(1)_m^{(1)}\) magnetic one-form symmetry.

Wilson lines are charged under the electric one-form symmetry.

’t Hooft lines are charged under the magnetic one-form symmetry.

The full symmetry structure includes electric-magnetic duality:

\[
SL(2,\mathbb{Z}),
\]

which mixes electric and magnetic charges.

Thus even classical electromagnetism contains generalized symmetry structure.

## 7. Higher-Group Symmetries

Higher-form symmetries can combine with ordinary symmetries into higher-groups.

A \(2\)-group symmetry mixes a \(0\)-form symmetry \(G\) and a \(1\)-form symmetry \(A\).

The background gauge fields are:

- a \(G\)-connection \(A_1\),
- an \(A\)-valued two-form \(B_2\).

Their field strengths satisfy a twisted Bianchi identity:

\[
d_{A_1}B_2
=
k(A_1),
\]

where

\[
k\in H^3(BG,A)
\]

is the Postnikov class.

This means the \(1\)-form symmetry is not independent of the \(0\)-form symmetry. The symmetries are glued together by topological data.

Thus the symmetry is not a product

\[
G\times A^{(1)},
\]

but a higher-group extension:

\[
A^{(1)}
\rightarrow
\mathcal{G}
\rightarrow
G.
\]

Higher-group symmetries appear in gauge theories, string theory, condensed matter systems, and anomaly inflow constructions.

## 8. Non-Invertible Symmetries

The most radical generalization is non-invertible symmetry.

An ordinary symmetry defect \(U_g\) has an inverse:

\[
U_g U_{g^{-1}}=1.
\]

A non-invertible defect \(D\) has no inverse.

Instead, defects obey fusion rules of the form

\[
D_a
\times
D_b
=
\sum_c
N_{ab}^{c}
D_c,
\]

where the fusion coefficients \(N_{ab}^{c}\) are nonnegative integers.

If every defect has an inverse, the fusion rules define a group.

If not, the fusion rules define a fusion category.

Thus the symmetry structure is not a group but a category.

## 9. Example: Ising Categorical Symmetry

The two-dimensional critical Ising model has an ordinary invertible \(\mathbb{Z}_2\) symmetry generated by a topological line \(\eta\).

It also has a non-invertible Kramers–Wannier duality line \(N\).

The fusion rules are

\[
\eta\times\eta=1,
\]

\[
\eta\times N=N,
\]

\[
N\times N=1+\eta.
\]

The line \(N\) is not invertible because its fusion with itself produces a sum of lines rather than the identity.

This fusion category is the Tambara–Yamagami category associated with \(\mathbb{Z}_2\).

The Ising theory therefore has a categorical symmetry larger than \(\mathbb{Z}_2\).

The distinction between symmetry and duality becomes relative:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Kramers–Wannier duality is not an ordinary symmetry, but it is a topological defect of the theory.}
}
\]

## 10. Duality Symmetries

A duality is an equivalence between theories:

\[
T
\cong
T'.
\]

If \(T'=T\), the duality is a self-duality.

Self-dualities may act as symmetry-like operations, but they need not be invertible as local defects.

Examples include:

1. T-duality in string theory,
2. S-duality in gauge theory,
3. mirror symmetry,
4. Kramers–Wannier duality,
5. Seiberg duality,
6. bosonization,
7. particle-vortex duality,
8. electric-magnetic duality.

In many cases, a duality can be represented by a topological interface or defect.

If the defect is invertible, it behaves like an ordinary symmetry.

If it is non-invertible, it behaves like a categorical symmetry.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Symmetry and duality are not fundamentally distinct. They are different presentations of defect structure.}
}
\]

## 11. Gauging, Orbifolding, and Relative Symmetry

Given a theory \(T\) with symmetry \(G\), one may gauge \(G\) to obtain a new theory:

\[
T/G.
\]

Gauging sums over \(G\)-bundles:

\[
Z_{T/G}
=
\sum_{[A]}
\frac{1}{|\text{Aut}(A)|}
Z_T[A].
\]

The original symmetry may disappear, reappear, or transform into a dual symmetry.

For example, gauging a \(\mathbb{Z}_2\) symmetry of the Ising model maps the theory to itself, but the operation is implemented by the non-invertible Kramers–Wannier defect.

Thus whether a structure appears as a symmetry or a duality can depend on which description or gauging frame one uses.

This is Symmetry Relativity in action.

## 12. Subsystem Symmetries

Subsystem symmetries act on lower-dimensional submanifolds, such as lines, planes, or fractal subsets.

They appear naturally in fracton phases of matter.

For example, a scalar charge theory may conserve not only total charge,

\[
Q
=
\int d^3x\,\rho,
\]

but also dipole moment,

\[
Q^i
=
\int d^3x\,x^i\rho.
\]

The continuity equation becomes

\[
\partial_t\rho
=
\partial_i\partial_j J^{ij}.
\]

This implies conservation of charge and dipole moment:

\[
\frac{dQ}{dt}=0,
\]

\[
\frac{dQ^i}{dt}=0.
\]

The corresponding symmetry operators act on planes or lines rather than entire spatial slices.

Subsystem symmetries are neither ordinary global symmetries nor standard higher-form symmetries. They are a further generalization of the symmetry concept.

## 13. Emergent Symmetries

Symmetries can be emergent.

A microscopic theory may possess a small symmetry group \(G_{\text{UV}}\), while its infrared fixed point possesses a larger symmetry group \(G_{\text{IR}}\):

\[
G_{\text{UV}}
\subset
G_{\text{IR}}.
\]

Examples include:

1. emergent Lorentz symmetry at critical points,
2. emergent conformal symmetry,
3. emergent gauge symmetry,
4. emergent dualities,
5. emergent higher-form symmetries,
6. accidental flavor symmetries.

Thus symmetry is not necessarily fundamental. It may be a low-energy relational structure.

The renormalization group defines a flow of symmetry structures:

\[
\mathcal{S}_{\text{UV}}
\rightarrow
\mathcal{S}_{\text{IR}}.
\]

Sometimes the infrared symmetry is larger, richer, or categorically different from the ultraviolet symmetry.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Symmetry can be scale-relative.}
}
\]

## 14. Anomalies as Invariant Symmetry Data

A symmetry may be anomalous.

An anomaly means that a classical symmetry cannot be consistently gauged in the quantum theory.

For an ordinary symmetry \(G\), the anomaly is often classified by a cohomology class,

\[
[\omega]
\in
H^{d+1}(BG,U(1)).
\]

Equivalently, the anomaly may be encoded in an anomaly polynomial,

\[
I_{d+2}.
\]

Under a gauge transformation parameterized by \(\alpha\), the effective action transforms as

\[
\delta_\alpha W[A]
=
2\pi i
\int_{M_d}
\alpha\cdot\mathcal{A}.
\]

The anomaly can be canceled by inflow from a \((d+1)\)-dimensional symmetry topological field theory:

\[
Z_{d+1}[A].
\]

The anomalous \(d\)-dimensional theory lives on the boundary of this higher-dimensional theory.

Thus the anomaly is not a defect to be discarded. It is invariant physical data.

In Symmetry Relativity, anomalies are among the most important invariants of a symmetry structure.

## 15. Categorical Anomalies

For non-invertible and higher categorical symmetries, anomalies generalize further.

A categorical symmetry may have an obstruction to gauging encoded not by an ordinary cohomology class but by higher categorical or cobordism data.

The full symmetry structure may be represented by a symmetry topological field theory,

\[
\text{SymTFT}(T),
\]

in one higher dimension.

Different boundary conditions of this topological theory correspond to different ways of gauging, breaking, or dualizing the symmetry.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{The symmetry of a theory is a boundary condition of a higher-dimensional topological structure.}
}
\]

This is one of the deepest modern reformulations of symmetry.

## 16. Symmetry Topological Field Theory

Given a theory \(T\) with symmetry structure \(\mathcal{S}\), one may associate a \((d+1)\)-dimensional topological field theory,

\[
Z_{\mathcal{S}}.
\]

The original theory \(T\) is obtained by choosing a boundary condition:

\[
T
=
\partial_{\mathcal{B}} Z_{\mathcal{S}}.
\]

Gauging a subgroup corresponds to changing the boundary condition.

Duality transformations correspond to different boundary conditions of the same symmetry TFT.

Thus dual theories are not separate realities. They are different boundary presentations of the same higher-dimensional invariant structure.

This is a precise realization of Symmetry Relativity.

## 18. Symmetry as Relational Structure

Symmetry Relativity asserts that symmetry is relational.

A symmetry presentation may include:

1. generators,
2. relations,
3. charged operators,
4. defect operators,
5. fusion rules,
6. anomaly data,
7. background gauge fields,
8. gauging choices.

Different presentations may describe the same physical symmetry structure.

Let \(P\) and \(P'\) be two presentations. They are physically equivalent if there exists an equivalence of defect categories preserving fusion and anomaly:

\[
\mathcal{C}_P
\simeq
\mathcal{C}_{P'},
\]

\[
[\omega_P]
=
[\omega_{P'}].
\]

The physical symmetry is the equivalence class:

\[
\mathcal{S}
=
[P].
\]

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Symmetry is not a chosen presentation. It is the invariant equivalence class of presentations.}
}
\]

## 19. The Relativity of Symmetry and Duality

In ordinary language, a symmetry maps a theory to itself, while a duality maps a theory to an equivalent but apparently different theory.

Symmetry Relativity dissolves this distinction.

Both are implemented by topological defects or categorical interfaces.

If the defect is invertible, we call it a symmetry.

If it is non-invertible or maps between different presentations, we call it a duality.

But invertibility and presentation can depend on the frame of description.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{The distinction between symmetry and duality is relative.}
}
\]

The invariant is the full web of equivalences.

## 20. Symmetry and Quantum Gravity

Quantum gravity is expected to have no exact global symmetries in the ordinary sense.

This principle extends to generalized global symmetries, though the precise status of non-invertible and categorical symmetries in quantum gravity remains subtle.

Symmetry Relativity suggests a resolution:

\[
\text{Exact global symmetry structures are not absolute background structures. They are relational, gaugeable, or emergent.}
\]

In holography, boundary symmetries may correspond to bulk gauge redundancies. In quantum gravity, what appears as global symmetry in one description may appear as gauge redundancy in another.

Thus symmetry itself is subject to holographic relativity.

## 21. Axioms of Symmetry Relativity

The framework may be organized around ten axioms.

### Axiom 1: Symmetry Is Structured

Symmetry is not merely a group. It is a categorical, higher-form, higher-group, or defect-theoretic structure.

### Axiom 2: Defects Are Fundamental

Symmetries are topological defects, and their fusion algebra encodes physical structure.

### Axiom 3: Invertibility Is Not Required

Non-invertible defects are legitimate symmetry structures.

### Axiom 4: Duality Is Symmetry-Like

Dualities are equivalences that may be represented as topological interfaces.

### Axiom 5: Symmetry Can Be Emergent

Symmetry structures may appear only in the infrared or in special limits.

### Axiom 6: Anomalies Are Invariant

Anomaly data are physical and presentation-independent.

### Axiom 7: Gauging Changes Presentation

Gauging, orbifolding, and condensation transform symmetry presentations without changing the deeper invariant structure.

### Axiom 8: Higher Symmetries Act on Extended Operators

Higher-form symmetries act on lines, surfaces, and higher-dimensional operators.

### Axiom 9: Subsystem Symmetries Are Relative to Spatial Structure

Subsystem symmetries depend on foliation, geometry, or algebraic constraints.

### Axiom 10: No Symmetry Presentation Is Privileged

The physical symmetry is the equivalence class of all consistent presentations.

## Appendix A: Topological Nature of Ordinary Symmetry Operators

Given a conserved current,

\[
\partial_\mu J^\mu=0,
\]

define a charge on a closed surface \(\Sigma\):

\[
Q(\Sigma)
=
\int_\Sigma J.
\]

If \(\Sigma\) and \(\Sigma'\) bound a region \(V\), then

\[
Q(\Sigma)-Q(\Sigma')
=
\int_V dJ
=
0.
\]

Thus \(Q\) is independent of deformation of \(\Sigma\).

The corresponding operator \(U_g(\Sigma)\) is topological.

## Appendix C: Two-Group Gauge Transformations

A two-group with \(0\)-form group \(G\) and \(1\)-form group \(A\) has background fields

\[
A_1,
\qquad
B_2.
\]

The field strength of \(A_1\) is

\[
F_2=dA_1+A_1\wedge A_1.
\]

The two-form field satisfies

\[
d_{A_1}B_2
=
k(A_1),
\]

where

\[
k\in H^3(BG,A)
\]

is the Postnikov class.

This equation encodes the mixing of \(0\)-form and \(1\)-form symmetries.

## Appendix D: Ising Categorical Fusion

The Ising fusion category has simple objects

\[
\{1,\eta,N\}.
\]

The fusion rules are

\[
\eta\times\eta=1,
\]

\[
\eta\times N=N,
\]

\[
N\times N=1+\eta.
\]

The quantum dimensions satisfy

\[
d_1=1,
\qquad
d_\eta=1,
\qquad
d_N=\sqrt{2}.
\]

The non-integer quantum dimension signals non-invertibility.

## Appendix E: Anomaly Inflow

A \(d\)-dimensional anomalous theory with background field \(A\) has effective action \(W[A]\) transforming as

\[
\delta_\alpha W[A]
=
2\pi i
\int_{M_d}
\alpha\cdot\mathcal{A}.
\]

The anomaly is canceled by a \((d+1)\)-dimensional bulk action \(S_{\text{bulk}}[A]\) satisfying

\[
\delta_\alpha S_{\text{bulk}}[A]
=
-
2\pi i
\int_{M_d}
\alpha\cdot\mathcal{A}.
\]

The combined system is gauge-invariant.

Thus the anomaly is encoded by a higher-dimensional symmetry topological field theory.
