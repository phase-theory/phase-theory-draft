Quantum Mechanics — Structural Sector

S.03 — Geometric Qubits

Information Stored in Geometry Rather Than Matter

Toward a Theory of Quantum-Geometric Information Carriers

⸻

Abstract

The qubit is the fundamental unit of quantum information. In conventional quantum computation, qubits are encoded within physical degrees of freedom such as electron spins, atomic states, superconducting circuits, photons, or topological quasiparticles. The geometry of spacetime acts only as a passive background upon which quantum states evolve.

This paper investigates a more radical possibility: that geometry itself may possess quantum degrees of freedom capable of encoding information. If spacetime exhibits discrete structure, quantized areas, non-commutative coordinates, curvature excitations, or topological defects—as suggested by multiple approaches to quantum gravity—then these geometric structures may function as information-bearing states.

We introduce the concept of the Geometric Qubit: a two-state quantum system encoded directly within microscopic geometric degrees of freedom rather than matter fields. We develop a mathematical framework for geometric Hilbert spaces, geometric entanglement, geometric gate operations, error correction, holographic information encoding, and quantum-geometric computation.

The resulting framework suggests that information, geometry, and quantum mechanics may represent different manifestations of a deeper informational substrate.

4. Definition of a Geometric Qubit

A Geometric Qubit (GQ) is a quantum two-state system whose logical basis states are encoded in geometric degrees of freedom.

Define

|0_G⟩

and

|1_G⟩

as two distinct geometric configurations.

The general state becomes

|ψ_G⟩ = α|0_G⟩ + β|1_G⟩

with

|α|² + |β|² = 1.

This is mathematically identical to a conventional qubit.

The difference lies in the physical realization.

Conclusions

The Geometric Qubit extends quantum information theory beyond matter-based encoding schemes by proposing that quantum geometry itself may carry information. Motivated by generalized uncertainty principles, non-commutative geometry, discrete spacetime models, holography, and topological structures, geometric qubits provide a unified framework in which information is encoded directly within microscopic geometric degrees of freedom.

Although no experimentally realized geometric qubit currently exists, numerous analogue systems—including topological materials, quantum Hall systems, photonic lattices, and synthetic geometries—already display many of the required features. These systems suggest plausible pathways toward geometric information processing.

If validated, geometric qubits would represent a new class of information carriers, expanding quantum computation beyond matter and fields into the structure of geometry itself. Such a development would fundamentally alter our understanding of information, computation, and spacetime, placing geometry at the center of quantum information science
