# Universal Compatibility Theory

**A Relational Foundation for Inference, Optimization, and Coordination**

**Preprint**

---

## Abstract

Universal Compatibility Theory (UCT) proposes a structural replacement for equality as the primitive relation of mathematical discourse. Rather than taking

\[
a=b
\]

as foundational, UCT takes a graded, measurable relation

\[
a\sim_c b
\]

with an associated compatibility strength

\[
\kappa(a,b)\in Q,
\]

where \(Q\) is a compatibility quantale, typically \(Q=[0,1]\). Equality is recovered as the limiting case of maximal compatibility together with separation and substitutivity. The theory develops: (i) an axiomatics of compatibility spaces; (ii) a universal closure construction giving the free compatibility structure generated by an arbitrary precompatibility relation; (iii) a tensorial and differential calculus of compatibility kernels; (iv) a categorical semantics in terms of enriched relations; (v) a logical calculus with composition rules for compatibility strength; and (vi) applications to optimization, multi-agent systems, database theory, and logic. The central claim is that many constructions traditionally expressed through equality, constraints, joins, identities, or equilibrium conditions are more naturally expressed as compatibility constraints with measurable strength.

**Keywords:** compatibility, graded equality, quantale, enriched category, fuzzy equivalence, metric geometry, database dependencies, potential games, relaxed optimization.

**MSC 2020:** 03E99, 03B52, 06D20, 18D20, 54E25, 68P15, 91A10, 90C30.

# 1. Introduction

Classical mathematics treats equality as primitive. Two objects are either identical or not:

\[
a=b \quad \text{or} \quad a\neq b.
\]

This binary idealization is powerful but often too brittle for applications in which objects are only partially interchangeable, approximately equal, contextually equivalent, or interoperable to a measurable degree. In numerical analysis, one uses approximations. In database theory, one uses joins and keys. In distributed systems, one uses consistency conditions. In statistical learning, one uses similarity kernels. In multi-agent systems, one uses coordination constraints. In logic, one uses equivalence or bisimulation. Each of these is a shadow of a more primitive notion: compatibility.

Universal Compatibility Theory begins from the following thesis.

> **Thesis.** Compatibility, not equality, is the primitive relational concept. Equality is the special case of maximal, separated, substitutive compatibility.

We write

\[
a\sim_c b
\]

to mean that \(a\) and \(b\) are compatible. In UCT, this is not a Boolean relation alone. It carries a strength

\[
\kappa(a,b)\in Q,
\]

where \(Q\) is an ordered monoidal value system. In the standard probabilistic model,

\[
Q=[0,1],\qquad \kappa(a,b)\in[0,1],
\]

where \(\kappa(a,b)=1\) denotes maximal compatibility and \(\kappa(a,b)=0\) denotes total incompatibility.

The central structural axiom is that compatibility strength is composable: if \(a\) is compatible with \(b\), and \(b\) is compatible with \(c\), then \(a\) is compatible with \(c\) to at least the composite strength. In the standard multiplicative model,

\[
\kappa(a,c)\geq \kappa(a,b)\kappa(b,c).
\]

Taking logarithms converts this into the triangle inequality for a distance-like quantity

\[
d(a,b)=-\log \kappa(a,b).
\]

Thus compatibility theory contains metric geometry as a logarithmic shadow, but it is more general: compatibility may be non-symmetric, non-separated, context-dependent, valued in arbitrary quantales, and may fail to induce a genuine metric before closure.

This paper develops UCT as a self-contained mathematical theory.

The principal contributions are as follows.

1. **Axiomatic foundations.** We define precompatibility structures, compatibility spaces, separated compatibility spaces, and the equality relation induced by maximal compatibility.

2. **Universal closure.** Given any reflexive symmetric precompatibility kernel \(\rho\), we construct the least compatibility kernel \(\rho^*\) above it. This gives a universal property justifying the name Universal Compatibility Theory.

3. **Tensor calculus.** For finite systems, compatibility kernels become tensors. We develop max-times idempotence, product systems, compatibility curvature, and a differential limit in which compatibility induces a Riemannian metric.

4. **Categorical semantics.** Compatibility spaces are symmetric categories enriched over a quantale. Compatibility-preserving maps are enriched functors.

5. **Logic.** We introduce a formal calculus of graded compatibility assertions with reflexivity, symmetry, weakening, composition, and substitution rules.

6. **Applications.** We show how equality-based constraints, equijoins, consensus conditions, and optimization constraints become compatibility conditions with measurable strength.

# 2. Compatibility Quantales

A rigorous theory of graded compatibility requires an algebra of strengths. We use quantales.

## Definition 2.1: Compatibility quantale

A **compatibility quantale** is a tuple

\[
Q=(Q,\leq,\otimes,1,0)
\]

such that:

1. \((Q,\leq)\) is a complete lattice.
2. \((Q,\otimes,1)\) is a commutative monoid.
3. \(1\) is the top element of \(Q\).
4. \(0\) is the bottom element of \(Q\).
5. For every \(a\in Q\), the map \(b\mapsto a\otimes b\) preserves arbitrary joins:

\[
a\otimes \bigvee_{i\in I} b_i
=
\bigvee_{i\in I}(a\otimes b_i).
\]

The element \(\kappa(a,b)\in Q\) is interpreted as the strength of compatibility between \(a\) and \(b\). Larger elements mean stronger compatibility.

## Example 2.2: Boolean compatibility

Let

\[
Q=\{0,1\},\qquad \otimes=\wedge,\qquad 1=\top,\qquad 0=\bot.
\]

Then compatibility is classical: either compatible or not.

## Example 2.3: Probabilistic compatibility

Let

\[
Q=[0,1],\qquad a\otimes b=ab,\qquad 1=1,\qquad 0=0.
\]

This is the standard continuous strength model.

## Example 2.4: Gödel compatibility

Let

\[
Q=[0,1],\qquad a\otimes b=\min(a,b).
\]

This models compatibility strength under bottleneck composition.

## Example 2.5: Cost quantale

One may equivalently work with distances using

\[
Q=[0,\infty],\qquad a\otimes b=a+b,
\]

with order reversed. In this paper we primarily use strength quantales, where \(1\) is maximal compatibility.

# 3. Precompatibility and Compatibility Spaces

Let \(X\) be a set of objects, states, agents, tuples, or terms.

## Definition 3.1: Precompatibility structure

A **precompatibility structure** on \(X\) is a map

\[
\kappa:X\times X\to Q
\]

satisfying:

### Axiom C0: Reflexivity

\[
\kappa(x,x)=1.
\]

### Axiom C1: Symmetry

\[
\kappa(x,y)=\kappa(y,x).
\]

We write

\[
x\sim_{\alpha} y
\]

to mean

\[
\kappa(x,y)\geq \alpha.
\]

When the strength is not specified, we write

\[
x\sim_c y
\]

to mean that \(\kappa(x,y)>0\), or more generally that compatibility is recognized at some contextually specified threshold.

A precompatibility structure need not be transitive. Many empirical similarity or interoperability relations are precompatibility structures but not yet compatibility spaces.

## Definition 3.2: Compatibility space

A **compatibility space** is a precompatibility structure \((X,\kappa)\) satisfying the additional composability axiom.

### Axiom C2: Composability

For all \(x,y,z\in X\),

\[
\kappa(x,z)\geq \kappa(x,y)\otimes \kappa(y,z).
\]

In the standard multiplicative case \(Q=[0,1]\),

\[
\kappa(x,z)\geq \kappa(x,y)\kappa(y,z).
\]

In the Gödel case,

\[
\kappa(x,z)\geq \min(\kappa(x,y),\kappa(y,z)).
\]

This axiom expresses the fact that compatibility can be transported through intermediaries.

## Definition 3.3: Separated compatibility space

A compatibility space is **separated** if

\[
\kappa(x,y)=1
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
x=y.
\]

Separation is not assumed by default. In UCT, equality is not prior to compatibility; equality is recovered from maximal compatibility after quotienting.

# 4. Equality as Derived Compatibility

In classical mathematics, equality is primitive. In UCT, equality is a derived notion.

Given a compatibility space \((X,\kappa)\), define

\[
x\equiv y
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\kappa(x,y)=1.
\]

We call \(\equiv\) the **indiscernibility relation** induced by \(\kappa\).

## Proposition 4.1: Indiscernibility is an equivalence relation

Let \((X,\kappa)\) be a compatibility space. Then \(\equiv\) is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive.

### Proof

Reflexivity follows from \(\kappa(x,x)=1\).

Symmetry follows from \(\kappa(x,y)=\kappa(y,x)\).

For transitivity, suppose \(x\equiv y\) and \(y\equiv z\). Then

\[
\kappa(x,y)=1,
\qquad
\kappa(y,z)=1.
\]

By composability,

\[
\kappa(x,z)
\geq
\kappa(x,y)\otimes \kappa(y,z)
=
1\otimes 1
=
1.
\]

Since \(1\) is top,

\[
\kappa(x,z)=1.
\]

Thus \(x\equiv z\). ∎

## Proposition 4.2: Maximal compatibility implies substitutivity

Let \((X,\kappa)\) be a compatibility space. If \(x\equiv y\), then for every \(z\in X\),

\[
\kappa(x,z)=\kappa(y,z).
\]

### Proof

Assume \(\kappa(x,y)=1\). By composability,

\[
\kappa(y,z)
\geq
\kappa(y,x)\otimes \kappa(x,z)
=
1\otimes \kappa(x,z)
=
\kappa(x,z).
\]

Similarly,

\[
\kappa(x,z)
\geq
\kappa(x,y)\otimes \kappa(y,z)
=
\kappa(y,z).
\]

Therefore,

\[
\kappa(x,z)=\kappa(y,z).
\]

∎

This proposition is crucial. It shows that maximal compatibility behaves like equality with respect to all compatibility profiles.

## Definition 4.3: Compatibility quotient

Let

\[
\overline{X}=X/{\equiv}.
\]

Define

\[
\overline{\kappa}([x],[y])=\kappa(x,y).
\]

By Proposition 4.2, \(\overline{\kappa}\) is well-defined.

## Proposition 4.4: The quotient is separated

The compatibility space \((\overline{X},\overline{\kappa})\) is separated.

### Proof

If

\[
\overline{\kappa}([x],[y])=1,
\]

then

\[
\kappa(x,y)=1,
\]

so \(x\equiv y\), hence \([x]=[y]\). ∎

Thus UCT recovers equality as the separated quotient of maximal compatibility.

# 5. Universal Compatibility Closure

The central universal construction of UCT is the passage from an arbitrary precompatibility structure to the least compatibility space containing it.

Let \(\rho:X\times X\to Q\) be a precompatibility kernel.

## Definition 5.1: Path strength

A finite path from \(x\) to \(y\) is a sequence

\[
x=x_0,x_1,\dots,x_n=y.
\]

Its \(\rho\)-strength is

\[
w_\rho(x_0,\dots,x_n)
=
\bigotimes_{r=0}^{n-1}\rho(x_r,x_{r+1}).
\]

For \(n=0\), the empty path from \(x\) to itself has strength \(1\).

## Definition 5.2: Universal compatibility closure

Define

\[
\rho^*(x,y)
=
\bigvee
\left\{
w_\rho(x_0,\dots,x_n)
\;\middle|\;
n\geq 0,\ x_0=x,\ x_n=y
\right\}.
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\rho^*(x,y)
=
\bigvee_{n\geq 0}
\bigvee_{x=x_0,\dots,x_n=y}
\bigotimes_{r=0}^{n-1}\rho(x_r,x_{r+1}).
\]

## Theorem 5.3: Universal closure theorem

Let \(\rho\) be a precompatibility kernel on \(X\). Then:

1. \(\rho^*\) is a compatibility space.
2. \(\rho\leq \rho^*\).
3. If \(\lambda\) is any compatibility space kernel on \(X\) with \(\rho\leq \lambda\), then

\[
\rho^*\leq \lambda.
\]

Thus \(\rho^*\) is the least compatibility space above \(\rho\).

### Proof

First, \(\rho^*(x,x)\geq 1\) because of the empty path. Since \(1\) is top,

\[
\rho^*(x,x)=1.
\]

Symmetry follows because \(\rho\) is symmetric: reversing a path preserves its strength.

Also, \(\rho(x,y)\leq \rho^*(x,y)\) by taking the path of length one.

For composability, let \(p\) be a path from \(x\) to \(y\) and \(q\) a path from \(y\) to \(z\). Their concatenation \(p*q\) is a path from \(x\) to \(z\), and

\[
w(p*q)=w(p)\otimes w(q).
\]

Therefore,

\[
\rho^*(x,z)
\geq
w(p)\otimes w(q).
\]

Taking joins over all such \(p,q\), and using distributivity of \(\otimes\) over joins,

\[
\rho^*(x,z)
\geq
\rho^*(x,y)\otimes \rho^*(y,z).
\]

Thus \(\rho^*\) is a compatibility space.

Now let \(\lambda\) be a compatibility space with \(\rho\leq \lambda\). For any path

\[
x=x_0,x_1,\dots,x_n=y,
\]

iterating composability gives

\[
\lambda(x,y)
\geq
\bigotimes_{r=0}^{n-1}\lambda(x_r,x_{r+1})
\geq
\bigotimes_{r=0}^{n-1}\rho(x_r,x_{r+1}).
\]

Taking the join over all paths,

\[
\lambda(x,y)\geq \rho^*(x,y).
\]

Hence \(\rho^*\leq \lambda\). ∎

This universal property is one of the foundational reasons for the name Universal Compatibility Theory.

## Corollary 5.4: Finite closure

If \(X\) is finite and \(Q=[0,1]\) with multiplication, then

\[
\rho^*
=
\bigvee_{n=0}^{|X|-1} \rho^{\odot n},
\]

where

\[
(A\odot B)_{ik}
=
\max_j A_{ij}B_{jk}.
\]

This is the max-times transitive closure.

# 6. Metric Representation

A compatibility space with strictly positive strengths has a canonical metric shadow.

Assume

\[
\kappa(x,y)>0
\]

for all \(x,y\) in the component under consideration. Define

\[
d(x,y)=-\log \kappa(x,y).
\]

If \(\kappa(x,y)=0\), set

\[
d(x,y)=+\infty.
\]

## Proposition 6.1: Compatibility becomes a generalized metric

Let \((X,\kappa)\) be a compatibility space over \(([0,1],\cdot)\). Then

\[
d(x,y)=-\log \kappa(x,y)
\]

satisfies:

1. \(d(x,x)=0\).
2. \(d(x,y)=d(y,x)\).
3. \(d(x,z)\leq d(x,y)+d(y,z)\).

If the space is separated, then

\[
d(x,y)=0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
x=y.
\]

Thus \(d\) is an extended pseudometric, or a metric after quotienting.

### Proof

Since \(\kappa(x,x)=1\),

\[
d(x,x)=-\log 1=0.
\]

Symmetry of \(\kappa\) gives symmetry of \(d\).

By composability,

\[
\kappa(x,z)\geq \kappa(x,y)\kappa(y,z).
\]

Taking \(-\log\), which reverses inequalities,

\[
-\log \kappa(x,z)
\leq
-\log \kappa(x,y)-\log \kappa(y,z).
\]

Hence

\[
d(x,z)\leq d(x,y)+d(y,z).
\]

Separation gives \(d(x,y)=0\) iff \(\kappa(x,y)=1\) iff \(x=y\) in the quotient. ∎

Thus UCT contains metric geometry as a logarithmic specialization.

# 7. Tensorial Calculus of Compatibility

For finite \(X=\{1,\dots,n\}\), a compatibility kernel is a symmetric tensor

\[
C_{ij}=\kappa(i,j),
\qquad
1\leq i,j\leq n.
\]

We use index notation, with repeated indices summed only when explicitly stated.

## 7.1 Compatibility matrices

The axioms become

\[
C_{ii}=1,
\]

\[
C_{ij}=C_{ji},
\]

and

\[
C_{ik}\geq C_{ij}C_{jk}
\]

for every \(i,j,k\), with no summation over \(j\).

Define the max-times product

\[
(A\odot B)_{ik}
=
\max_j A_{ij}B_{jk}.
\]

Then a compatibility matrix satisfies

\[
C\odot C=C.
\]

## Proposition 7.1: Idempotence

Let \(C\) be reflexive and symmetric. Then \(C\) is a compatibility matrix iff

\[
C\odot C=C.
\]

### Proof

If \(C\) is a compatibility matrix, then for every \(j\),

\[
C_{ik}\geq C_{ij}C_{jk}.
\]

Hence

\[
C_{ik}\geq \max_j C_{ij}C_{jk}
=
(C\odot C)_{ik}.
\]

Conversely, taking \(j=i\),

\[
(C\odot C)_{ik}
\geq
C_{ii}C_{ik}
=
C_{ik}.
\]

Thus if \(C\odot C=C\), both inequalities hold, and

\[
C_{ik}
=
\max_j C_{ij}C_{jk}
\geq
C_{ij}C_{jk}
\]

for every \(j\). ∎

In distance notation,

\[
D_{ij}=-\log C_{ij},
\]

and the max-times product becomes min-plus convolution:

\[
(D\oplus D)_{ik}
=
\min_j (D_{ij}+D_{jk}).
\]

Compatibility is equivalent to

\[
D\oplus D=D.
\]

This is the algebraic expression of the triangle inequality.

---

## 7.2 Product systems

Let \(A\) and \(B\) be compatibility spaces with kernels

\[
C^A_{ij},
\qquad
C^B_{\alpha\beta}.
\]

Their product compatibility tensor is

\[
C^{A\otimes B}_{i\alpha,j\beta}
=
C^A_{ij}C^B_{\alpha\beta}.
\]

In logarithmic distance,

\[
D^{A\otimes B}_{i\alpha,j\beta}
=
D^A_{ij}+D^B_{\alpha\beta}.
\]

Thus independent compatibility costs add and independent compatibility strengths multiply.

## Proposition 7.2: Stability of product compatibility

If \(C^A\) and \(C^B\) are compatibility matrices, then \(C^A\otimes C^B\) is a compatibility matrix.

### Proof

Reflexivity:

\[
C^{A\otimes B}_{i\alpha,i\alpha}
=
C^A_{ii}C^B_{\alpha\alpha}
=
1.
\]

Symmetry is immediate.

For composability,

\[
C^A_{ik}\geq C^A_{ij}C^A_{jk},
\qquad
C^B_{\alpha\gamma}\geq C^B_{\alpha\beta}C^B_{\beta\gamma}.
\]

Multiplying,

\[
C^A_{ik}C^B_{\alpha\gamma}
\geq
(C^A_{ij}C^B_{\alpha\beta})
(C^A_{jk}C^B_{\beta\gamma}).
\]

Thus

\[
C^{A\otimes B}_{i\alpha,k\gamma}
\geq
C^{A\otimes B}_{i\alpha,j\beta}
C^{A\otimes B}_{j\beta,k\gamma}.
\]

∎

---

## 7.3 Higher-order compatibility tensors

For \(n\) systems with indices \(i_1,\dots,i_n\), a general compatibility tensor is

\[
C_{i_1\dots i_n}.
\]

Pairwise compatibility is recovered by marginals:

\[
C^{(ab)}_{i_a i_b}
=
\max_{\{i_c:c\neq a,b\}}
C_{i_1\dots i_n}.
\]

A pairwise-generated compatibility tensor has the form

\[
C_{i_1\dots i_n}
=
\bigotimes_{a<b} C^{(ab)}_{i_a i_b}.
\]

In the probabilistic case,

\[
C_{i_1\dots i_n}
=
\prod_{a<b} C^{(ab)}_{i_a i_b}.
\]

This is the compatibility analogue of a factorized Boltzmann weight.

## 7.4 Compatibility curvature

Given a triple \((i,j,k)\), define the compatibility slack

\[
\Theta_{ijk}
=
C_{ik}-C_{ij}C_{jk}.
\]

For a compatibility space,

\[
\Theta_{ijk}\geq 0.
\]

In logarithmic distance,

\[
\tau_{ijk}
=
D_{ij}+D_{jk}-D_{ik}\geq 0.
\]

The quantity \(\tau_{ijk}\) measures the failure of the path \(i\to j\to k\) to be a shortest path. We call it the **triangle slack**.

For a cycle

\[
\gamma=(i_0,i_1,\dots,i_m=i_0),
\]

define loop compatibility

\[
L(\gamma)
=
\bigotimes_{r=0}^{m-1} C_{i_r i_{r+1}}.
\]

Since \(C_{i_0i_0}=1\), composability implies

\[
L(\gamma)\leq 1.
\]

The loop defect is

\[
\Delta(\gamma)
=
1-L(\gamma).
\]

In logarithmic distance,

\[
\ell(\gamma)
=
\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}D_{i_r i_{r+1}}
\geq 0.
\]

Thus compatibility curvature is measured by the excess length of nontrivial loops.

## 7.5 Differential compatibility and induced metrics

Let \(M\) be a smooth manifold and let

\[
\kappa:M\times M\to (0,1]
\]

be smooth near the diagonal, with

\[
\kappa(x,x)=1.
\]

Define

\[
F(x,y)=\log \kappa(x,y).
\]

Then

\[
F(x,x)=0.
\]

Since \(\kappa(x,y)\leq 1\), \(F\leq 0\), and the diagonal is a maximum.

Define a covariant tensor field

\[
g_{\mu\nu}(x)
=
-
\left.
\frac{\partial^2}{\partial y^\mu\partial y^\nu}
F(x,y)
\right|_{y=x}.
\]

If \(g_{\mu\nu}\) is positive definite, it is a Riemannian metric induced by compatibility.

Indeed, for small displacement \(dx^\mu\),

\[
\log\kappa(x,x+dx)
=
-\frac{1}{2}g_{\mu\nu}(x)dx^\mu dx^\nu
+
O(|dx|^3).
\]

Thus locally,

\[
\kappa(x,x+dx)
=
\exp\left(
-\frac{1}{2}g_{\mu\nu}(x)dx^\mu dx^\nu
+
O(|dx|^3)
\right).
\]

This shows that differential geometry arises as the infinitesimal limit of compatibility theory.

# 8. Categorical Semantics

UCT admits a natural categorical formulation.

Let \(Q\) be a compatibility quantale, regarded as a monoidal category:

- objects are elements \(q\in Q\);
- there is a unique morphism \(q\to r\) iff \(q\leq r\);
- tensor product is \(\otimes\).

## Definition 8.1: Compatibility category

A compatibility space \((X,\kappa)\) is a symmetric category enriched over \(Q\):

- objects are elements of \(X\);
- the hom-object from \(x\) to \(y\) is \(\kappa(x,y)\);
- composition is the inequality

\[
\kappa(x,y)\otimes \kappa(y,z)\leq \kappa(x,z);
\]

- identities are

\[
1\leq \kappa(x,x).
\]

Since \(\kappa(x,x)=1\), identities are strict.

## Definition 8.2: Compatibility-preserving map

A function \(f:X\to Y\) between compatibility spaces is **compatible** if

\[
\kappa_X(x,x')
\leq
\kappa_Y(f(x),f(x'))
\]

for all \(x,x'\in X\).

In distance notation, this says that \(f\) is non-expanding:

\[
d_Y(f(x),f(x'))
\leq
d_X(x,x').
\]

## Proposition 8.3: Universal closure as left adjoint

Let \(\mathbf{PreComp}\) be the category of precompatibility structures and compatible maps. Let \(\mathbf{Comp}\) be the full subcategory of compatibility spaces.

The universal closure construction

\[
\rho\mapsto \rho^*
\]

is left adjoint to the inclusion

\[
\mathbf{Comp}\hookrightarrow \mathbf{PreComp}.
\]

That is, for every precompatibility structure \(\rho\) and every compatibility space \(\lambda\),

\[
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbf{Comp}}(\rho^*,\lambda)
\cong
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbf{PreComp}}(\rho,\lambda).
\]

This is the categorical expression of universality.

# 9. Compatibility Logic

We now develop a logical calculus in which compatibility statements carry strength.

## 9.1 Syntax

Let \(\mathcal{L}\) be a first-order language with terms. For terms \(s,t\) and strength \(\alpha\in Q\), introduce atomic formulas

\[
s\sim_{\alpha} t.
\]

Intuitively,

\[
s\sim_{\alpha} t
\]

means that the compatibility strength between \(s\) and \(t\) is at least \(\alpha\).

## 9.2 Semantics

An interpretation assigns to each sort a compatibility space \((X,\kappa)\), and to each term \(t\) an element \(\llbracket t\rrbracket\in X\).

Then

\[
\mathcal{M}\models s\sim_{\alpha} t
\]

iff

\[
\kappa(\llbracket s\rrbracket,\llbracket t\rrbracket)\geq \alpha.
\]

## 9.3 Deductive rules

The basic rules are as follows.

### Reflexivity

\[
\frac{}{\Gamma\vdash t\sim_{1} t}
\]

### Symmetry

\[
\frac{\Gamma\vdash s\sim_{\alpha} t}
{\Gamma\vdash t\sim_{\alpha} s}
\]

### Weakening

If \(\beta\leq \alpha\),

\[
\frac{\Gamma\vdash s\sim_{\alpha} t}
{\Gamma\vdash s\sim_{\beta} t}
\]

### Compatibility composition

\[
\frac{
\Gamma\vdash s\sim_{\alpha} t
\qquad
\Delta\vdash t\sim_{\beta} u
}{
\Gamma,\Delta\vdash s\sim_{\alpha\otimes\beta} u
}
\]

### Substitution for compatible functions

If \(f\) is an \(n\)-ary function symbol interpreted as a compatibility-preserving map, then

\[
\frac{
\Gamma_i\vdash s_i\sim_{\alpha_i} t_i
}{
\bigcup_i\Gamma_i
\vdash
f(s_1,\dots,s_n)
\sim_{\alpha_1\otimes\cdots\otimes\alpha_n}
f(t_1,\dots,t_n)
}
\]

## Theorem 9.1: Soundness

The above deductive system is sound with respect to compatibility semantics.

### Proof

Each rule preserves validity in every compatibility space.

Reflexivity follows from \(\kappa(x,x)=1\). Symmetry follows from symmetry of \(\kappa\). Weakening is monotonicity of \(\geq\). Composition follows from

\[
\kappa(s,t)\geq \alpha,
\quad
\kappa(t,u)\geq \beta
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\kappa(s,u)\geq \alpha\otimes\beta.
\]

Substitution follows from compatibility preservation of function interpretations. ∎

## Theorem 9.2: Atomic completeness

Let \(T\) be a set of atomic compatibility assertions. The deductive closure of \(T\) coincides with the universal compatibility closure of the precompatibility kernel generated by \(T\).

### Proof sketch

The rules generate exactly:

1. reflexive strengths;
2. symmetrization;
3. pathwise tensor composition;
4. joins over alternative derivations.

These are precisely the operations defining \(\rho^*\). Hence an assertion \(s\sim_\alpha t\) is derivable iff

\[
\alpha\leq \rho^*(s,t).
\]

∎

# 10. Database Theory via Compatibility Joins

Classical relational database theory relies heavily on equality. Joins are equijoins. Keys enforce equality. Entity resolution assumes exact matching. UCT replaces these with compatibility constraints.

## 10.1 Domains with compatibility

Let each attribute \(A\) have a domain \(D_A\) equipped with a compatibility kernel

\[
\kappa_A:D_A\times D_A\to Q.
\]

For attribute sets \(U=\{A_1,\dots,A_n\}\), define tuple compatibility by

\[
\kappa_U(t,u)
=
\bigotimes_{A\in U}\kappa_A(t[A],u[A]).
\]

The empty product is \(1\).

## 10.2 Compatible joins

Let \(R\) be a relation over schema \(U\), and \(S\) a relation over schema \(V\). Let

\[
W=U\cap V.
\]

For threshold \(\tau\in Q\), define the **compatible join**

\[
R\bowtie^{\tau}_{\kappa} S
=
\left\{
(t,u)\in R\times S
\;\middle|\;
\kappa_W(t[W],u[W])\geq \tau
\right\}.
\]

The annotated compatible join returns pairs with strength

\[
\eta(t,u)=\kappa_W(t[W],u[W]).
\]

## Proposition 10.1: Classical join as a limit

If each \(\kappa_A\) is the discrete equality kernel,

\[
\kappa_A(a,b)=
\begin{cases}
1,&a=b,\\
0,&a\neq b,
\end{cases}
\]

and \(\tau=1\), then

\[
R\bowtie^1_{\kappa} S
\]

is the ordinary natural join.

### Proof

For shared attributes \(W\),

\[
\kappa_W(t[W],u[W])=1
\]

iff \(t[A]=u[A]\) for all \(A\in W\). Hence the condition is exactly the equijoin condition. ∎

## Proposition 10.2: Monotonicity

If \(\tau_1\leq \tau_2\), then

\[
R\bowtie^{\tau_2}_{\kappa} S
\subseteq
R\bowtie^{\tau_1}_{\kappa} S.
\]

If \(\kappa_A\leq \lambda_A\) pointwise for all shared attributes, then

\[
R\bowtie^{\tau}_{\kappa} S
\subseteq
R\bowtie^{\tau}_{\lambda} S.
\]

### Proof

Both statements follow immediately from monotonicity of the condition

\[
\kappa_W(t,u)\geq \tau.
\]

∎

## 10.3 Compatibility dependencies

Classical functional dependencies have a graded analogue.

Let \(R\) be a relation over attribute set \(U\). For attribute subsets \(A,B\subseteq U\) and strength parameter \(\lambda\in Q\), define a **compatibility dependency**

\[
A\longrightarrow_{\lambda} B
\]

to mean

\[
\forall t,u\in R,
\qquad
\kappa_A(t,u)\otimes \lambda
\leq
\kappa_B(t,u).
\]

When \(\lambda=1\), compatibility on \(A\) implies compatibility on \(B\).

## Proposition 10.3: Sound dependency rules

The following rules are sound.

### Reflexivity

If \(B\subseteq A\), then

\[
A\longrightarrow_{1} B.
\]

### Augmentation

If

\[
A\longrightarrow_{\lambda} B,
\]

then for any \(C\),

\[
AC\longrightarrow_{\lambda} BC.
\]

### Transitivity

If

\[
A\longrightarrow_{\lambda} B
\]

and

\[
B\longrightarrow_{\mu} C,
\]

then

\[
A\longrightarrow_{\lambda\otimes\mu} C.
\]

### Proof

For reflexivity, since \(B\subseteq A\),

\[
\kappa_A(t,u)
=
\kappa_B(t,u)\otimes \kappa_{A\setminus B}(t,u)
\leq
\kappa_B(t,u).
\]

Hence

\[
\kappa_A(t,u)\leq \kappa_B(t,u).
\]

For augmentation,

\[
\kappa_{AC}(t,u)
=
\kappa_A(t,u)\otimes \kappa_C(t,u).
\]

Using \(A\to_\lambda B\),

\[
\kappa_A(t,u)\otimes \lambda
\leq
\kappa_B(t,u).
\]

Tensoring with \(\kappa_C(t,u)\),

\[
\kappa_A(t,u)\otimes \kappa_C(t,u)\otimes \lambda
\leq
\kappa_B(t,u)\otimes \kappa_C(t,u).
\]

Thus

\[
\kappa_{AC}(t,u)\otimes \lambda
\leq
\kappa_{BC}(t,u).
\]

For transitivity,

\[
\kappa_A\otimes\lambda
\leq
\kappa_B,
\qquad
\kappa_B\otimes\mu
\leq
\kappa_C.
\]

Therefore,

\[
\kappa_A\otimes\lambda\otimes\mu
\leq
\kappa_C.
\]

∎

Classical functional dependencies are recovered when all kernels are discrete and \(\lambda=1\).

# 11. Optimization with Compatibility Constraints

Classical constrained optimization often uses equality constraints:

\[
g_i(x)=h_i(x).
\]

UCT replaces equality by compatibility.

## 11.1 Compatibility-constrained programs

Let \(X\) be a decision space. For \(i=1,\dots,m\), let

\[
\kappa_i:Y_i\times Y_i\to Q
\]

be compatibility kernels, and let

\[
g_i,h_i:X\to Y_i.
\]

A compatibility-constrained optimization problem has the form

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{minimize}
&\quad f(x)\\
\text{subject to}
&\quad \kappa_i(g_i(x),h_i(x))\geq \tau_i,
\qquad i=1,\dots,m.
\end{aligned}
\]

The feasible set is

\[
\mathcal{F}_{\tau}
=
\left\{
x\in X
\;\middle|\;
\kappa_i(g_i(x),h_i(x))\geq \tau_i
\ \forall i
\right\}.
\]

The threshold \(\tau_i\) is the required compatibility strength.

## 11.2 Logarithmic cost form

Assume \(Q=[0,1]\) with multiplication and \(\kappa_i>0\). Define

\[
c_i(x)
=
-\log \kappa_i(g_i(x),h_i(x)).
\]

Let

\[
b_i=-\log \tau_i.
\]

Then the constraints become

\[
c_i(x)\leq b_i.
\]

Thus compatibility constraints become ordinary inequality constraints in logarithmic incompatibility.

The Lagrangian is

\[
\mathcal{L}(x,\lambda)
=
f(x)
+
\sum_{i=1}^m \lambda_i(c_i(x)-b_i),
\]

with \(\lambda_i\geq 0\).

The Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions include

\[
\nabla f(x)
+
\sum_{i=1}^m \lambda_i \nabla c_i(x)
=
0,
\]

\[
\lambda_i(c_i(x)-b_i)=0,
\]

\[
c_i(x)\leq b_i,
\qquad
\lambda_i\geq 0.
\]

Since

\[
c_i(x)=-\log \kappa_i(x),
\]

we have

\[
\nabla c_i(x)
=
-\frac{\nabla \kappa_i(x)}{\kappa_i(x)}.
\]

Thus the gradient flow of compatibility penalization is

\[
\dot{x}
=
-
\nabla f(x)
+
\sum_i \lambda_i
\frac{\nabla \kappa_i(x)}{\kappa_i(x)}.
\]

## 11.3 Gaussian compatibility and least squares

A particularly important case is Gaussian compatibility. Suppose

\[
\kappa_i(y,z)
=
\exp\left(
-\frac{\|y-z\|^2}{2\sigma_i^2}
\right).
\]

Then

\[
\kappa_i(g_i(x),h_i(x))
=
\exp\left(
-\frac{\|g_i(x)-h_i(x)\|^2}{2\sigma_i^2}
\right).
\]

Therefore,

\[
c_i(x)
=
\frac{\|g_i(x)-h_i(x)\|^2}{2\sigma_i^2}.
\]

The compatibility constraint

\[
\kappa_i\geq \tau_i
\]

becomes

\[
\|g_i(x)-h_i(x)\|^2
\leq
-2\sigma_i^2\log \tau_i.
\]

As \(\tau_i\to 1\), the right-hand side tends to \(0\), and the compatibility constraint converges to the equality constraint

\[
g_i(x)=h_i(x).
\]

Thus equality-constrained optimization is the sharp limit of compatibility-constrained optimization.

## Theorem 11.1: Convergence to equality-constrained optima

Let \(X\) be compact. Let \(f\) and all \(c_i\) be continuous. Suppose

\[
\mathcal{F}_1
=
\{x\in X:c_i(x)=0\ \forall i\}
\]

is nonempty. Let \(\tau^{(n)}\to 1\), equivalently \(b_i^{(n)}\to 0\). Define

\[
v(\tau)
=
\inf_{x\in \mathcal{F}_\tau} f(x).
\]

Then

\[
\lim_{n\to\infty} v(\tau^{(n)})
=
v(1).
\]

### Proof

Since \(\mathcal{F}_1\subseteq \mathcal{F}_{\tau^{(n)}}\),

\[
v(\tau^{(n)})\leq v(1).
\]

Thus

\[
\limsup_{n\to\infty}v(\tau^{(n)})\leq v(1).
\]

For the reverse inequality, choose \(x_n\in \mathcal{F}_{\tau^{(n)}}\) such that

\[
f(x_n)\leq v(\tau^{(n)})+\frac{1}{n}.
\]

By compactness, a subsequence converges to \(x^*\in X\). Continuity gives

\[
c_i(x^*)\leq \lim_{n\to\infty} b_i^{(n)}=0.
\]

Since \(c_i\geq 0\), \(c_i(x^*)=0\). Hence \(x^*\in \mathcal{F}_1\). Therefore,

\[
v(1)\leq f(x^*)
\leq
\liminf_{n\to\infty} v(\tau^{(n)}).
\]

Combining the inequalities gives the result. ∎

# 12. Multi-Agent Compatibility Systems

UCT provides a natural language for coordination among heterogeneous agents.

Let there be \(n\) agents indexed by \(a=1,\dots,n\). Agent \(a\) has local state space \(X_a\). For finite \(X_a\), write local states as indices \(i_a\).

## 12.1 Joint compatibility tensors

A joint compatibility tensor is a map

\[
C_{i_1\dots i_n}\in[0,1].
\]

For pairwise compatibility kernels

\[
C^{(ab)}_{i_a i_b},
\]

the factorized joint compatibility is

\[
C_{i_1\dots i_n}
=
\prod_{a<b} C^{(ab)}_{i_a i_b}.
\]

The compatibility of a pure joint state \((i_1,\dots,i_n)\) is exactly \(C_{i_1\dots i_n}\).

## 12.2 Mixed compatibility

Let agent \(a\) choose a probability distribution

\[
p_a^{i_a}\in \Delta(X_a).
\]

The expected global compatibility is the tensor contraction

\[
Z(p_1,\dots,p_n)
=
C_{i_1\dots i_n}
p_1^{i_1}\cdots p_n^{i_n},
\]

with summation over repeated indices.

The marginal compatibility field acting on agent \(a\) is

\[
M_a^{i_a}(p_{-a})
=
C_{i_1\dots i_n}
\prod_{b\neq a}p_b^{i_b}.
\]

Thus agent \(a\)'s expected compatibility under mixed strategy \(p_a\) is

\[
U_a(p_a;p_{-a})
=
p_a^{i_a}M_a^{i_a}(p_{-a}).
\]

## 12.3 Compatible equilibria

A mixed profile \(p=(p_1,\dots,p_n)\) is a **compatible equilibrium** if for every agent \(a\),

\[
p_a
\in
\arg\max_{q_a\in \Delta(X_a)}
q_a^{i_a}M_a^{i_a}(p_{-a}).
\]

Equivalently, no agent can increase expected compatibility by unilaterally changing its distribution.

## 12.4 Pairwise log-compatibility potential games

In many applications, it is more natural to maximize expected log-compatibility. Define

\[
\Phi(p)
=
\sum_{a<b}
\sum_{i,j}
p_a^i p_b^j
\log C^{(ab)}_{ij}.
\]

Assume \(C^{(ab)}_{ij}>0\). The local payoff to agent \(a\) for choosing state \(i\) is

\[
u_a(i;p_{-a})
=
\sum_{b\neq a}
\sum_j
p_b^j
\log C^{(ab)}_{ij}.
\]

The mixed payoff is

\[
\widehat{u}_a(p_a;p_{-a})
=
\sum_i p_a^i u_a(i;p_{-a}).
\]

## Proposition 12.1: Existence of pure compatible equilibrium

If the pairwise compatibility kernels are symmetric,

\[
C^{(ab)}_{ij}=C^{(ba)}_{ji},
\]

then the finite compatibility game with potential \(\Phi\) is an exact potential game. Therefore it admits at least one pure Nash equilibrium.

### Proof

For any unilateral change by agent \(a\), the change in \(\Phi\) equals the change in \(\widehat{u}_a\), because terms not involving \(a\) are constant and pairwise symmetry ensures that each interaction term is counted once. Hence \(\Phi\) is an exact potential.

Since the pure strategy space is finite, any sequence of strict improving moves increases \(\Phi\) and must terminate. A terminal state is a pure Nash equilibrium. ∎

## 12.5 Consensus as compatibility maximization

Let all agents share a common state space \(X\), and let pairwise compatibility be given by a symmetric kernel

\[
C_{ij}.
\]

A distribution \(p^i\) over states has expected pairwise compatibility

\[
S(p)
=
C_{ij}p^i p^j.
\]

The consensus problem is

\[
\max_{p\in \Delta(X)} S(p).
\]

## Proposition 12.2: Stationary consensus condition

If \(p\) is a local maximizer of \(S\) over the simplex, then there exists \(\lambda\) such that

\[
(Cp)_i
=
\lambda
\quad
\text{for all }i\text{ with }p^i>0,
\]

and

\[
(Cp)_i
\leq
\lambda
\quad
\text{for all }i\text{ with }p^i=0.
\]

### Proof

The objective is

\[
S(p)=C_{ij}p^i p^j.
\]

Its gradient is

\[
\frac{\partial S}{\partial p^i}
=
2(Cp)_i
\]

when \(C\) is symmetric. The KKT conditions for maximizing over the simplex give the stated support equalities and inequalities. ∎

Thus consensus is not necessarily concentration on a single state. It is concentration on a maximally compatible cluster.

# 13. Measurement of Compatibility Strength

A central claim of UCT is that compatibility strength is measurable.

There are three principal measurement paradigms.

## 13.1 Test-based compatibility

Let \(\mathcal{T}\) be a family of tests. A test \(T\in\mathcal{T}\) produces observations when applied to objects \(x\) and \(y\). Define

\[
\kappa(x,y)
=
\inf_{T\in\mathcal{T}}
\operatorname{Agree}_T(x,y),
\]

where \(\operatorname{Agree}_T(x,y)\in[0,1]\) is the probability or degree to which \(x\) and \(y\) produce indistinguishable outcomes under \(T\).

This yields an operational compatibility kernel.

## 13.2 Statistical kernel estimation

Given paired observations \((x_k,y_k)\), one may estimate

\[
\widehat{\kappa}(x,y)
=
\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{k=1}^N
K(x,x_k)K(y,y_k),
\]

or use positive-definite kernel methods. If the estimated kernel violates transitivity, universal closure produces the corrected compatibility

\[
\widehat{\kappa}^*.
\]

## 13.3 Axiomatic measurement

Suppose there is a qualitative comparative relation on pairs:

\[
(x,y)\preceq (u,v)
\]

meaning “\((x,y)\) is no more compatible than \((u,v)\).”

Under standard order-theoretic assumptions—total preorder, independence, continuity, and Archimedeanity—there exists a representation

\[
\kappa:X\times X\to[0,1]
\]

such that

\[
(x,y)\preceq (u,v)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\kappa(x,y)\leq \kappa(u,v).
\]

Thus measurable compatibility strength is not an extra assumption but a representational consequence of coherent comparative judgment.

# 14. Equality, Identity, and Leibniz Law

Classical identity is governed by Leibniz’s law:

\[
x=y
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\forall P\,(P(x)\leftrightarrow P(y)).
\]

UCT has a compatibility-theoretic analogue.

Let \(\mathcal{O}\) be a class of observables or compatibility-preserving maps

\[
f:X\to Y_f.
\]

Define

\[
x=_\mathcal{O} y
\]

iff for every \(f\in\mathcal{O}\),

\[
\kappa_{Y_f}(f(x),f(y))=1.
\]

Thus equality is indistinguishability under all allowed observations.

If \(\mathcal{O}\) contains enough maps to separate points of the separated quotient, then

\[
x=_\mathcal{O} y
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\kappa_X(x,y)=1.
\]

Equality is therefore maximal observational compatibility.

# 16. Conclusion

Universal Compatibility Theory replaces the classical primacy of equality with a graded, measurable, composable relation of compatibility. The theory shows that equality is not the foundational relation it is traditionally taken to be. Rather, equality is the separated, maximal limit of compatibility.

The central constructions are:

\[
a\sim_c b
\quad\text{with strength}\quad
\kappa(a,b)\in Q,
\]

\[
\kappa(x,z)\geq \kappa(x,y)\otimes \kappa(y,z),
\]

\[
\rho\mapsto \rho^*,
\]

\[
d=-\log\kappa,
\]

and the tensorial composition of compatibility structures.

The resulting framework applies uniformly to optimization, database theory, logic, and multi-agent coordination. In optimization, equality constraints become compatibility thresholds. In databases, equijoins become compatible joins. In logic, substitutivity becomes graded compatibility composition. In multi-agent systems, equilibrium becomes compatibility maximization.

The broader consequence is a change in mathematical perspective: identity is not the starting point of structure. Compatibility is.
