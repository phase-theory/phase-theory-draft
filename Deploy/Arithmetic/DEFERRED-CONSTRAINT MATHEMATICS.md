DEFERRED-CONSTRAINT MATHEMATICS

A New Mathematical Theory of Unresolved Structure, Constraint Accumulation, and Resolution Dynamics

Foundational Preprint

August 2026

⸻

Abstract

This paper introduces Deferred-Constraint Mathematics (DCM), a proposed mathematical framework in which the primitive state of a mathematical object is not a fully resolved value, point, set, function, or relation, but a structured collection of unresolved constraints together with a history of how those constraints were generated, transformed, coupled, and resolved.

Conventional mathematics generally represents incompleteness indirectly. An equation may have unknown variables; an optimization problem may contain feasible and infeasible states; a differential equation may possess unresolved boundary conditions; a probabilistic model may represent uncertainty; and a computational process may contain partially evaluated expressions. These phenomena are normally treated by separate mathematical disciplines.

DCM instead takes deferredness itself as a primitive mathematical quantity.

The central object of the theory is a deferred state

[
\mathfrak D=(X,\mathcal C,\mu,\rho,\sigma),
]

where (X) is an underlying state space, (\mathcal C) is a structured constraint family, (\mu) measures unresolved constraint mass, (\rho) describes resolution pathways, and (\sigma) records structural history.

The theory introduces a new algebra of constraint generation and discharge, a geometry of deferred states, a calculus of resolution, a topology of constraint compatibility, and a dynamics in which mathematical evolution is represented as the transfer of unresolved structure through a state space.

A principal result is the Deferred Conservation Principle: under a broad class of admissible transformations, unresolved structure cannot simply disappear; it must either be transferred, transformed, compensated, or explicitly discharged through a resolution event.

DCM therefore distinguishes three mathematically different phenomena that conventional notation often conflates:

[
\boxed{
\text{unknown}
\neq
\text{undetermined}
\neq
\text{deferred}.
}
]

An unknown is a missing value.
An undetermined quantity has multiple admissible values.
A deferred quantity carries unresolved structural obligations whose future resolution depends on the transformation history.

The framework is developed from first principles and extended to algebra, geometry, topology, analysis, dynamical systems, computation, information theory, optimization, and theoretical physics.

The theory is presented as a newly formulated mathematical proposal, not as a claim that no historically related concept exists anywhere in mathematics.

Part I — Foundational Motivation

1. The Problem of Resolved Mathematics

A large fraction of conventional mathematics is written in a resolved form.

We write

[
x=3,
]

rather than

[
x\rightsquigarrow {3,\ldots}.
]

We write

[
f:X\rightarrow Y,
]

rather than explicitly representing the conditions under which (f) remains admissible.

We write

[
A+B=C,
]

without representing the structural obligations generated by the operation.

This creates a hidden asymmetry.

A mathematical transformation is usually represented by its result, while the unresolved requirements produced along the way are discarded from the formal state.

DCM proposes that this discarded structure can itself be mathematically meaningful.

The theory therefore begins from the principle

[
\boxed{
\text{A mathematical state may contain structure that has not yet resolved.}
}
]

Such structure is called deferred structure.

Part II — Primitive Concepts

2. Deferredness

Let (X) be a mathematical state space.

A conventional state is

[
x\in X.
]

A deferred state is

[
\mathfrak d=(x,\mathcal C),
]

where

[
\mathcal C={C_1,C_2,\ldots,C_n}
]

is a collection of unresolved constraints.

A constraint is a condition

[
C_i:X\rightarrow \mathbb R
]

with admissibility condition

[
C_i(x)=0.
]

More generally,

[
C_i(x)\in A_i,
]

where (A_i) is the admissible region.

The key difference is that the state is not completely characterized by (x).

It is characterized by

[
(x,\mathcal C).
]

3. Constraint Mass

DCM introduces a scalar measure of unresolved structure:

[
\mu(\mathcal C)\geq 0.
]

We call

[
\boxed{\mu(\mathcal C)=\text{deferred mass}}
]

the amount of unresolved structure contained in a state.

The simplest form is additive:

[
\mu(\mathcal C)

\sum_i w_i,
]

where (w_i\geq0) is the weight of constraint (C_i).

More generally,

[
\mu:
\mathfrak D\rightarrow \mathbb R_{\geq0}.
]

A completely resolved state satisfies

[
\mu(\mathcal C)=0.
]

A maximally unresolved state has large (\mu).

Importantly,

[
\mu
]

is not necessarily entropy, uncertainty, complexity, or energy.

It measures structural obligation awaiting resolution.

Part III — The Five Fundamental Axioms

Axiom I — Deferred State Axiom

Every mathematical transformation may be represented as acting on a state containing both resolved and unresolved structure:

[
T:(x,\mathcal C)\rightarrow(x’,\mathcal C’).
]

Axiom II — Constraint Persistence Axiom

A constraint cannot vanish from the mathematical description without one of three events:

1. satisfaction,
2. transformation into another constraint,
3. explicit cancellation by a compensating structure.

Thus

[
C_i
\not\rightarrow
\varnothing
]

without a mathematically identifiable resolution mechanism.

Axiom III — Resolution Axiom

A constraint is resolved when the state enters its admissible manifold:

[
C_i(x)=0.

[
R_i:
(x,\mathcal C)
\rightarrow
(x’,\mathcal C\setminus{C_i}).

Axiom IV — Transformation Axiom

Transformations transport constraints according to

[
T_\ast C_i=C_i\circ T^{-1},

whenever (T^{-1}) exists.

For noninvertible transformations, DCM replaces ordinary pullback with a generalized constraint transport operator

[
\mathscr T_T.
]

⸻

Axiom V — Historical Axiom

Two states with identical instantaneous values may nevertheless be mathematically distinct if their unresolved structures have different histories.

Thus

[
x_1=x_2
]

does not imply

[
\mathfrak d_1=\mathfrak d_2.

This establishes history-sensitive mathematical identity.

[

[
\mu_{n+1}>\mu_n,
