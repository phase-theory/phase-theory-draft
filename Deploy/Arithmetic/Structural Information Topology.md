# Structural Information Topology

**Preprint White Paper**

---

## Abstract

This paper develops **Structural Information Topology** (SIT), a framework in which topology is not postulated through primitive open sets but is instead generated by **information flow**, **accessibility**, and **observational reachability**. The central claim is that the neighborhood of a point should be understood as the set of states, agents, events, or representations about which that point can acquire information above a specified threshold. From this principle, a topology

\[
\tau_I
=
\{\text{information-open sets}\}
\]

is derived from an information accessibility kernel. We give an axiomatic foundation for SIT, prove that information neighborhoods generate bona fide topologies under natural flow axioms, introduce interior, closure, and boundary operators adapted to information accessibility, and develop a tensorial calculus for discrete and continuum information flow. We then apply the theory to distributed computing, network topology, artificial intelligence, and communication systems. The resulting formalism treats topological structure as an emergent invariant of information propagation rather than as a primitive spatial or set-theoretic datum.

**Keywords:** topology, information theory, distributed computing, network topology, attention, information flow, category theory, persistent homology, tensor calculus.

---

## 1. Introduction

Classical topology begins with a set \(X\) and a collection \(\tau\) of subsets of \(X\), called open sets, satisfying axioms. The open sets are primitive. In many modern systems — distributed protocols, sensor networks, neural networks, communication graphs, and multi-agent epistemic systems — the operationally meaningful notion of nearness is not geometric and is not given a priori. It is determined by **which entities can obtain information about which other entities**, through which channels, at what fidelity, and under what cost or confidence threshold.

The guiding idea of **Structural Information Topology** is therefore:

> A neighborhood of a point is an information-accessibility region.  
> A set is open if every point in it can verify or access enough information to remain inside it.  
> Topology is generated by information flow.

Instead of starting with open sets, we begin with an **information accessibility kernel**

\[
A:X\times X\to [0,1],
\]

where \(A(x,y)\) quantifies the degree to which \(x\) can access information about \(y\). From this kernel we define information neighborhoods

\[
B_\lambda(x)
=
\{y\in X : A(x,y)\geq \lambda\},
\]

and then generate a topology

\[
\tau_I
=
\left\{
U\subseteq X :
\forall x\in U,\ \exists \lambda>0 \text{ such that } B_\lambda(x)\subseteq U
\right\}.
\]

This reverses the usual order of construction. Open sets are no longer primitive; they are the stable regions of information accessibility.

The purpose of this paper is to give a complete formal foundation for this reversal.

The main contributions are as follows.

1. **Axiomatic foundations.** We define information spaces, raw channels, and information closures, and we prove that information neighborhoods form a topological basis under natural transitivity and reflexivity axioms.

2. **Level topologies and separation.** We introduce threshold-fixed topologies \(\tau_\lambda\), analyze specialization preorders, and characterize \(T_0\), \(T_1\), and Hausdorff conditions in terms of information distinguishability.

3. **Tensorial information calculus.** We develop a discrete and continuum tensor notation for information flow, including accessibility tensors, flux tensors, conservation laws, and information Laplacians.

4. **Categorical structure.** We define morphisms of SIT spaces, prove continuity criteria, and describe products and functorial behavior.

5. **Applications.** We apply SIT to distributed computing, network topology, AI representation geometry, and communication systems.

The paper is written as a theoretical preprint and is intended to serve as a foundation for subsequent work in information topology, topological systems theory, and information-theoretic geometry of complex systems.

## 2. Information Spaces and Accessibility Kernels

### 2.1 Basic objects

Let \(X\) be a set. Its elements may represent agents, processes, sensors, states, latent representations, nodes in a network, or events.

Let \(L\) be a complete lattice of information values. In the simplest case,

\[
L=[0,1],
\]

ordered by the usual order, with meet

\[
a\wedge b=\min(a,b)
\]

and join

\[
a\vee b=\max(a,b).
\]

The value \(1\) represents perfect accessibility or perfect informational resolution; \(0\) represents no accessibility.

An **information channel prekernel** is a map

\[
C:X\times X\to L.
\]

The value \(C(x,y)\) is interpreted as the direct information accessibility of \(y\) from \(x\). It may represent normalized channel capacity, confidence, probability of successful inference, mutual information normalized to \([0,1]\), attention strength, or reliability of communication.

We impose the minimal reflexivity normalization

\[
C(x,x)=1
\]

for all \(x\in X\), expressing that a point has perfect access to its own local state.

A raw channel \(C\) need not be transitive. If \(x\) can access information about \(y\), and \(y\) can access information about \(z\), then \(x\) should typically have some indirect access to \(z\). To capture this, we pass from \(C\) to its **information closure**.

---

### 2.2 Max–min composition and information closure

For relations \(R,S:X\times X\to [0,1]\), define their max–min composition by

\[
(R\circ S)(x,z)
=
\bigvee_{y\in X}
\left(
R(x,y)\wedge S(y,z)
\right).
\]

In finite or countable settings, \(\bigvee\) is the supremum. For \(n\geq 1\), define iterated powers

\[
C^{\otimes 1}=C,
\]

\[
C^{\otimes (n+1)}=C\circ C^{\otimes n}.
\]

The **information closure** of \(C\) is

\[
A
=
C^{*}
=
\bigvee_{n\geq 1} C^{\otimes n}.
\]

Equivalently,

\[
A(x,y)
=
\sup_{n\geq 1}
\sup_{x=x_0,x_1,\dots,x_n=y}
\min_{0\leq k<n} C(x_k,x_{k+1}).
\]

This is the maximum bottleneck accessibility along any finite information path from \(x\) to \(y\).

---

### 2.3 Axioms for an information accessibility kernel

An **information accessibility kernel** is a map

\[
A:X\times X\to [0,1]
\]

satisfying:

1. **Reflexivity**

   \[
   A(x,x)=1.
   \]

2. **Transitive attenuation**

   \[
   A(x,z)
   \geq
   A(x,y)\wedge A(y,z)
   \]

   for all \(x,y,z\in X\).

Equivalently,

\[
A\circ A \leq A.
\]

Thus \(A\) is a fuzzy preorder. If \(A\) is symmetric,

\[
A(x,y)=A(y,x),
\]

then it is a fuzzy equivalence-like structure, although not necessarily separated.

---

### 2.4 Closure theorem

**Theorem 2.1.** Let \(C:X\times X\to [0,1]\) satisfy \(C(x,x)=1\). Let

\[
A=C^{*}=\bigvee_{n\geq 1}C^{\otimes n}.
\]

Then \(A\) is reflexive and transitively attenuated:

\[
A(x,x)=1,
\]

\[
A(x,z)\geq A(x,y)\wedge A(y,z).
\]

**Proof.** Reflexivity follows because \(C(x,x)=1\), so the length-one path from \(x\) to \(x\) has value \(1\).

For transitivity, observe that

\[
A\circ A
=
\left(
\bigvee_{m\geq 1} C^{\otimes m}
\right)
\circ
\left(
\bigvee_{n\geq 1} C^{\otimes n}
\right).
\]

Max–min composition preserves arbitrary joins in each variable, hence

\[
A\circ A
=
\bigvee_{m,n\geq 1}
C^{\otimes m}\circ C^{\otimes n}.
\]

But

\[
C^{\otimes m}\circ C^{\otimes n}
=
C^{\otimes (m+n)}.
\]

Therefore

\[
A\circ A
=
\bigvee_{m,n\geq 1}
C^{\otimes (m+n)}
\leq
\bigvee_{k\geq 1}
C^{\otimes k}
=
A.
\]

Thus for every \(x,y,z\),

\[
A(x,y)\wedge A(y,z)
\leq
(A\circ A)(x,z)
\leq
A(x,z).
\]

Hence

\[
A(x,z)\geq A(x,y)\wedge A(y,z).
\]

∎

The map \(A=C^{*}\) is the smallest reflexive and transitively attenuated kernel dominating \(C\) in the max–min setting. It is the information-theoretic analogue of taking the transitive closure of a reachability relation, but with graded strength.

## 3. Information Neighborhoods and the Generated Topology

### 3.1 Information neighborhoods

Let \(A:X\times X\to [0,1]\) be an information accessibility kernel.

For \(\lambda\in (0,1]\), define the **information neighborhood of \(x\) at level \(\lambda\)** by

\[
B_\lambda(x)
=
\{y\in X : A(x,y)\geq \lambda\}.
\]

The parameter \(\lambda\) represents a required threshold of informational resolution, confidence, capacity, or reliability.

If \(\lambda\) is close to \(1\), then \(B_\lambda(x)\) contains only those points about which \(x\) has very strong information access. If \(\lambda\) is small, then \(B_\lambda(x)\) includes points accessible through weaker or indirect channels.

---

### 3.2 Basis theorem

Let \(\Lambda\subseteq (0,1]\) be a set of thresholds. We assume:

1. \(1\in \Lambda\).
2. \(\Lambda\) is closed under finite joins:

   \[
   \lambda,\mu\in\Lambda
   \implies
   \lambda\vee\mu\in\Lambda.
   \]

In the standard case \(\Lambda=(0,1]\), this holds.

Define the family

\[
\mathcal{B}_I
=
\{B_\lambda(x):x\in X,\ \lambda\in\Lambda\}.
\]

**Theorem 3.1.** If \(A\) is reflexive and transitively attenuated, then \(\mathcal{B}_I\) is a basis for a topology on \(X\).

**Proof.** We verify the basis axioms.

First, for every \(x\in X\), since \(A(x,x)=1\), we have

\[
x\in B_1(x).
\]

Thus every point lies in some basis element.

Second, suppose

\[
x\in B_\lambda(a)\cap B_\mu(b)
\]

for some \(a,b\in X\) and \(\lambda,\mu\in\Lambda\). Then

\[
A(a,x)\geq \lambda,
\]

\[
A(b,x)\geq \mu.
\]

Let

\[
\nu=\lambda\vee\mu.
\]

Since \(\Lambda\) is closed under finite joins, \(\nu\in\Lambda\). Consider \(B_\nu(x)\). Since \(A(x,x)=1\geq \nu\),

\[
x\in B_\nu(x).
\]

Now take \(z\in B_\nu(x)\). Then

\[
A(x,z)\geq \nu.
\]

By transitive attenuation,

\[
A(a,z)
\geq
A(a,x)\wedge A(x,z).
\]

Since \(A(a,x)\geq\lambda\) and \(A(x,z)\geq\nu\geq\lambda\),

\[
A(a,z)\geq \lambda.
\]

Thus

\[
z\in B_\lambda(a).
\]

Similarly,

\[
A(b,z)
\geq
A(b,x)\wedge A(x,z)
\geq
\mu,
\]

so

\[
z\in B_\mu(b).
\]

Therefore

\[
B_\nu(x)\subseteq B_\lambda(a)\cap B_\mu(b).
\]

This proves the basis condition. ∎

---

### 3.3 The information topology

The topology generated by \(\mathcal{B}_I\) is the **structural information topology**.

\[
\tau_I
=
\left\{
U\subseteq X :
\forall x\in U,\ \exists \lambda\in\Lambda
\text{ such that }
B_\lambda(x)\subseteq U
\right\}.
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\tau_I
=
\left\{
\bigcup_{\alpha} B_{\lambda_\alpha}(x_\alpha)
:
x_\alpha\in X,\ \lambda_\alpha\in\Lambda
\right\}.
\]

A set \(U\subseteq X\) is **information-open** if every point in \(U\) possesses an information neighborhood entirely contained in \(U\).

This is the precise formalization of the principle

\[
\tau_I
=
\{\text{information neighborhoods}\}.
\]

Open sets are generated by information accessibility.

---

### 3.4 Interior operator

The interior operator associated with \(\tau_I\) is

\[
\operatorname{int}_I(U)
=
\bigcup
\left\{
B_\lambda(x):
B_\lambda(x)\subseteq U
\right\}.
\]

Equivalently,

\[
x\in \operatorname{int}_I(U)
\]

if and only if there exists \(\lambda\in\Lambda\) such that

\[
B_\lambda(x)\subseteq U.
\]

Thus \(x\) lies in the information-interior of \(U\) exactly when \(x\) has enough information to ensure that all sufficiently accessible points remain in \(U\).

---

### 3.5 Closure operator

The closure of \(U\subseteq X\) in \(\tau_I\) is

\[
\operatorname{cl}_I(U)
=
\{
x\in X :
\forall \lambda\in\Lambda,\ 
B_\lambda(x)\cap U\neq\varnothing
\}.
\]

Thus \(x\) lies in the information-closure of \(U\) if, at every information threshold, \(x\) can access some point of \(U\).

The **information boundary** is

\[
\partial_I U
=
\operatorname{cl}_I(U)\setminus \operatorname{int}_I(U).
\]

The boundary represents the region where information is insufficient to certify interior membership but still sufficient to prevent complete separation from \(U\).

---

### 3.6 Level topologies

For a fixed threshold \(\lambda\in\Lambda\), define

\[
\tau_\lambda
=
\left\{
U\subseteq X :
\forall x\in U,\ B_\lambda(x)\subseteq U
\right\}.
\]

Equivalently, \(\tau_\lambda\) is generated by the basis

\[
\mathcal{B}_\lambda
=
\{B_\lambda(x):x\in X\}.
\]

**Theorem 3.2.** For each \(\lambda\in\Lambda\), \(\tau_\lambda\) is an Alexandrov topology.

**Proof.** Let \(\{U_\alpha\}_{\alpha\in I}\) be an arbitrary family of \(\tau_\lambda\)-open sets. Suppose

\[
x\in \bigcap_{\alpha\in I} U_\alpha.
\]

Because each \(U_\alpha\) is open and \(x\in U_\alpha\), there exists, by definition of \(\tau_\lambda\), a basis element \(B_\lambda(a_\alpha)\) such that

\[
x\in B_\lambda(a_\alpha)\subseteq U_\alpha.
\]

Since \(x\in B_\lambda(a_\alpha)\),

\[
A(a_\alpha,x)\geq \lambda.
\]

For any \(z\in B_\lambda(x)\),

\[
A(x,z)\geq \lambda.
\]

By transitivity,

\[
A(a_\alpha,z)
\geq
A(a_\alpha,x)\wedge A(x,z)
\geq
\lambda.
\]

Thus

\[
z\in B_\lambda(a_\alpha).
\]

Therefore

\[
B_\lambda(x)\subseteq B_\lambda(a_\alpha)\subseteq U_\alpha.
\]

Since this holds for every \(\alpha\),

\[
B_\lambda(x)\subseteq \bigcap_{\alpha\in I} U_\alpha.
\]

Hence the intersection is \(\tau_\lambda\)-open. ∎

Thus fixed-threshold SIT spaces are Alexandrov spaces: arbitrary intersections of open sets remain open.

---

### 3.7 Specialization preorder

For fixed \(\lambda\), define the relation

\[
x\preceq_\lambda y
\iff
A(x,y)\geq \lambda.
\]

Then

\[
B_\lambda(x)
=
\{y:x\preceq_\lambda y\}.
\]

The topology \(\tau_\lambda\) is the upper topology generated by the preorder \(\preceq_\lambda\).

**Proposition 3.3.** The specialization preorder of \(\tau_\lambda\) is \(\preceq_\lambda\).

**Proof.** In any topology, the specialization preorder is

\[
x\leq_{\mathrm{spec}} y
\iff
x\in \operatorname{cl}(\{y\}).
\]

Equivalently, every open neighborhood of \(x\) contains \(y\).

In \(\tau_\lambda\), the minimal open neighborhood of \(x\) is \(B_\lambda(x)\). Therefore every open neighborhood of \(x\) contains \(y\) if and only if

\[
y\in B_\lambda(x),
\]

which is equivalent to

\[
A(x,y)\geq \lambda.
\]

Thus

\[
x\leq_{\mathrm{spec}} y
\iff
x\preceq_\lambda y.
\]

∎

This identifies the order-theoretic content of a fixed-threshold information topology.

## 4. Separation, Symmetry, and Metric Specializations

### 4.1 Indistinguishability

Define the **informational indistinguishability relation at unit strength** by

\[
x\sim_1 y
\iff
A(x,y)=1
\text{ and }
A(y,x)=1.
\]

If \(x\sim_1 y\), then \(x\) and \(y\) are mutually accessible with maximal strength. In the full topology \(\tau_I\), such points cannot generally be separated by information-open sets.

---

### 4.2 \(T_0\) and \(T_1\) conditions

For the level topology \(\tau_\lambda\), the relation

\[
x\preceq_\lambda y
\iff
A(x,y)\geq \lambda
\]

is the specialization preorder. Hence \(\tau_\lambda\) is \(T_0\) if and only if \(\preceq_\lambda\) is antisymmetric:

\[
A(x,y)\geq \lambda
\text{ and }
A(y,x)\geq \lambda
\implies
x=y.
\]

It is \(T_1\) if and only if for every distinct \(x\neq y\),

\[
A(x,y)<\lambda
\quad\text{and}\quad
A(y,x)<\lambda.
\]

For the full topology \(\tau_I\), separation is governed by arbitrarily high thresholds.

---

### 4.3 Hausdorff condition

Assume \(A\) is symmetric:

\[
A(x,y)=A(y,x).
\]

Assume further that \(A\) separates points at unit strength:

\[
x\neq y
\implies
A(x,y)<1.
\]

Assume \(\Lambda\) is cofinal near \(1\), meaning that for every \(\alpha<1\) there exists \(\lambda\in\Lambda\) with

\[
\alpha<\lambda\leq 1.
\]

**Theorem 4.1.** Under these assumptions, \((X,\tau_I)\) is Hausdorff.

**Proof.** Let \(x\neq y\). Since \(A(x,y)<1\), choose \(\lambda\in\Lambda\) such that

\[
A(x,y)<\lambda.
\]

Consider the information neighborhoods

\[
B_\lambda(x),
\qquad
B_\lambda(y).
\]

They contain \(x\) and \(y\), respectively, because \(A(x,x)=A(y,y)=1\).

Suppose there exists

\[
z\in B_\lambda(x)\cap B_\lambda(y).
\]

Then

\[
A(x,z)\geq \lambda,
\]

\[
A(y,z)\geq \lambda.
\]

By symmetry,

\[
A(z,y)=A(y,z)\geq \lambda.
\]

By transitive attenuation,

\[
A(x,y)
\geq
A(x,z)\wedge A(z,y)
\geq
\lambda.
\]

This contradicts the choice of \(\lambda\). Therefore

\[
B_\lambda(x)\cap B_\lambda(y)=\varnothing.
\]

Thus \(x\) and \(y\) have disjoint information neighborhoods. ∎

Therefore Hausdorff separation corresponds to the possibility of distinguishing points by sufficiently stringent information thresholds.

---

### 4.4 Ultrametric realization

Let \(d:X\times X\to [0,\infty]\) be an ultrametric:

\[
d(x,x)=0,
\]

\[
d(x,y)=d(y,x),
\]

\[
d(x,z)\leq \max(d(x,y),d(y,z)).
\]

Fix \(\ell>0\) and define

\[
A(x,y)
=
\exp\left(-\frac{d(x,y)}{\ell}\right).
\]

Then

\[
A(x,x)=1,
\]

and

\[
A(x,z)
=
\exp\left(-\frac{d(x,z)}{\ell}\right)
\geq
\exp\left(
-\frac{\max(d(x,y),d(y,z))}{\ell}
\right).
\]

But

\[
\exp\left(
-\frac{\max(d(x,y),d(y,z))}{\ell}
\right)
=
\min
\left\{
\exp\left(-\frac{d(x,y)}{\ell}\right),
\exp\left(-\frac{d(y,z)}{\ell}\right)
\right\}.
\]

Hence

\[
A(x,z)
\geq
A(x,y)\wedge A(y,z).
\]

Thus every ultrametric induces an SIT structure.

Moreover,

\[
B_\lambda(x)
=
\left\{
y:
d(x,y)\leq -\ell\log \lambda
\right\},
\]

so information neighborhoods are metric balls.

---

### 4.5 Ordinary metrics and product transitivity

If \(d\) is an ordinary metric, one may instead use the multiplicative transitivity law

\[
A(x,z)
\geq
A(x,y)A(y,z).
\]

With

\[
A(x,y)=e^{-d(x,y)/\ell},
\]

the triangle inequality gives

\[
d(x,z)
\leq
d(x,y)+d(y,z),
\]

and therefore

\[
e^{-d(x,z)/\ell}
\geq
e^{-d(x,y)/\ell}
e^{-d(y,z)/\ell}.
\]

This yields a variant of SIT based on a multiplicative quantale rather than the max–min quantale.

Thus SIT is flexible enough to encode ultrametric, metric, directed, fuzzy, and capacity-based notions of accessibility.

## 5. Categorical Structure

### 5.1 Objects

A **SIT space** is a triple

\[
(X,\Lambda,A),
\]

where \(X\) is a set, \(\Lambda\) is a threshold system, and

\[
A:X\times X\to [0,1]
\]

is an information accessibility kernel satisfying reflexivity and transitive attenuation.

When \(\Lambda\) is fixed globally, we simply write \((X,A)\).

---

### 5.2 Morphisms

Let

\[
(X,A_X),\quad (Y,A_Y)
\]

be SIT spaces.

A function

\[
f:X\to Y
\]

is **information-continuous** if for every \(U\in\tau_{I,Y}\),

\[
f^{-1}(U)\in\tau_{I,X}.
\]

A stronger and often more useful condition is **information non-expansiveness**:

\[
A_Y(f(x),f(y))
\geq
A_X(x,y)
\]

for all \(x,y\in X\).

---

### 5.3 Continuity theorem

**Theorem 5.1.** If \(f:X\to Y\) is information non-expansive, then \(f\) is information-continuous.

**Proof.** Let \(V\subseteq Y\) be information-open. We show \(f^{-1}(V)\) is information-open.

Take \(x\in f^{-1}(V)\). Then \(f(x)\in V\). Since \(V\) is information-open in \(Y\), there exists \(\lambda\in\Lambda\) such that

\[
B_\lambda^{A_Y}(f(x))\subseteq V.
\]

We claim

\[
B_\lambda^{A_X}(x)
\subseteq
f^{-1}(V).
\]

Indeed, if \(z\in B_\lambda^{A_X}(x)\), then

\[
A_X(x,z)\geq \lambda.
\]

By non-expansiveness,

\[
A_Y(f(x),f(z))
\geq
A_X(x,z)
\geq
\lambda.
\]

Thus

\[
f(z)\in B_\lambda^{A_Y}(f(x))
\subseteq V.
\]

Therefore

\[
z\in f^{-1}(V).
\]

Hence \(f^{-1}(V)\) is information-open. ∎

Thus maps that do not decrease information accessibility are continuous.

---

### 5.4 Category of SIT spaces

Let \(\mathbf{SIT}\) be the category whose objects are SIT spaces and whose morphisms are information-continuous maps.

The subcategory \(\mathbf{SIT}_1\) with information non-expansive maps is often better behaved for quantitative analysis.

---

### 5.5 Products

Given SIT spaces \((X,A_X)\) and \((Y,A_Y)\), define the product accessibility on \(X\times Y\) by

\[
A_{X\times Y}
\bigl(
(x_1,y_1),(x_2,y_2)
\bigr)
=
A_X(x_1,x_2)\wedge A_Y(y_1,y_2).
\]

Then

\[
B_\lambda^{X\times Y}((x,y))
=
B_\lambda^X(x)\times B_\lambda^Y(y).
\]

Thus the product topology agrees with the SIT product topology at fixed thresholds.

## 6. Tensorial Calculus of Information Flow

Although SIT is fundamentally topological, many systems require a quantitative flow calculus. We now introduce a tensorial formalism suitable for finite information networks and their continuum limits.

---

### 6.1 Discrete accessibility tensors

Let the information space have finitely many nodes indexed by

\[
i,j,k,\dots \in \{1,\dots,n\}.
\]

Define the accessibility tensor

\[
A^i{}_j.
\]

The upper index denotes the observing or receiving node; the lower index denotes the source or target about which information is accessed.

Thus

\[
A^i{}_j
=
A(i,j).
\]

Max–min composition becomes a tensor product over the max–min semiring:

\[
(A\circ B)^i{}_k
=
\bigvee_j
\left(
A^i{}_j\wedge B^j{}_k
\right).
\]

Formally, one may write

\[
(A\circ B)^i{}_k
=
A^i{}_j B^j{}_k,
\]

provided multiplication is interpreted as \(\wedge\) and summation as \(\bigvee\).

The information closure is

\[
A^i{}_j
=
\bigvee_{m\geq 1}
(C^{\otimes m})^i{}_j.
\]

---

### 6.2 Information flux tensors

Let

\[
F^{ij}
\]

denote the information flux from \(j\) to \(i\). In a linearized regime, one may write

\[
F^{ij}
=
K^{ij}(\phi^j-\phi^i),
\]

where:

- \(\phi^i\) is an information potential at node \(i\),
- \(K^{ij}\) is an information conductivity tensor,
- \(K^{ij}\geq 0\),
- \(K^{ij}\) is typically supported where \(A^i{}_j\) is large.

A natural choice is

\[
K^{ij}
=
\kappa A^i{}_j,
\]

for some conductivity scale \(\kappa>0\).

---

### 6.3 Discrete conservation law

Let \(\sigma^i\) be an information source at node \(i\). The discrete continuity equation is

\[
\partial_t \rho^i
+
\nabla_j F^{ij}
=
\sigma^i,
\]

where \(\rho^i\) is information density and

\[
\nabla_j F^{ij}
=
\sum_j F^{ij}
-
\sum_j F^{ji}
\]

in the simplest undirected or symmetrized setting.

For a subset \(U\subseteq X\), global balance reads

\[
\frac{d}{dt}
\sum_{i\in U}\rho^i
=
\sum_{i\in U}\sigma^i
-
\sum_{\substack{i\in U\\ j\notin U}}F^{ij}
+
\sum_{\substack{i\notin U\\ j\in U}}F^{ij}.
\]

This expresses conservation of information content in terms of flux across the information boundary.

---

### 6.4 Information Laplacian

Define the information Laplacian by

\[
(\Delta_I \phi)^i
=
\sum_j K^{ij}(\phi^j-\phi^i).
\]

In tensor notation,

\[
\Delta_I \phi
=
L_I \phi,
\]

where

\[
(L_I)^i{}_j
=
\sum_k K^{ik}\delta^i{}_j
-
K^{ij}.
\]

Diffusive information dynamics are then

\[
\partial_t \phi^i
=
(\Delta_I \phi)^i
+
\sigma^i.
\]

The kernel \(A\) determines the support and strength of \(K\), and therefore determines the topology in which diffusion occurs.

---

### 6.5 Continuum limit

Let \(M\) be a smooth manifold representing a continuous information space. Let

\[
\rho(x,t)
\]

be information density and \(\phi(x,t)\) information potential.

Let \(K^{\mu\nu}(x)\) be the information conductivity tensor. Define the information flux vector

\[
J^\mu
=
-K^{\mu\nu}\nabla_\nu \phi.
\]

The continuity equation is

\[
\partial_t \rho
+
\nabla_\mu J^\mu
=
\sigma.
\]

If \(\rho\) is proportional to \(\phi\), this gives a diffusion equation

\[
\partial_t \rho
=
\nabla_\mu
\left(
K^{\mu\nu}\nabla_\nu \rho
\right)
+
\sigma.
\]

The topology of the continuum SIT space may be induced by an information distance \(d_I\). If \(A(x,y)\) has the asymptotic form

\[
A(x,y)
\approx
\exp\left(
-\frac{d_I(x,y)^2}{2\ell^2}
\right),
\]

then information neighborhoods are geodesic balls with respect to \(d_I\).

---

### 6.6 Information metric and curvature

When \(d_I\) arises from a Riemannian metric \(g_{\mu\nu}\), one may write

\[
d_I(x,y)^2
=
\inf_\gamma
\int_\gamma
g_{\mu\nu}
\dot\gamma^\mu
\dot\gamma^\nu
\,dt.
\]

A canonical choice in statistical systems is the Fisher information metric

\[
g_{\mu\nu}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{p(\cdot\mid\theta)}
\left[
\partial_\mu \log p(Z\mid\theta)
\partial_\nu \log p(Z\mid\theta)
\right].
\]

The associated Levi-Civita connection defines a curvature tensor

\[
R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu}.
\]

In SIT, this curvature measures how information neighborhoods deform under changes of parameters or observational resolution.

Positive information curvature corresponds to local redundancy and clustering of accessible states. Negative information curvature corresponds to rapid divergence of accessible information paths.

---

## 7. Dynamics, Filtrations, and Persistent Information Topology

### 7.1 Time-dependent accessibility

In many systems, information accessibility depends on time:

\[
A_t:X\times X\to [0,1].
\]

Then the topology becomes time-dependent:

\[
\tau_I(t).
\]

A natural evolution equation for accessibility is

\[
\partial_t A_t
=
\Delta_I A_t
+
S_t,
\]

where \(S_t\) represents creation or destruction of channels.

Topological change occurs when thresholds cross critical values of \(A_t\).

---

### 7.2 Filtrations by threshold

Given \(A\), define a filtration of simplicial complexes.

For each \(\lambda\), define a directed graph \(G_\lambda\) with vertices \(X\) and edge

\[
x\to y
\iff
A(x,y)\geq \lambda.
\]

If symmetry is assumed, one may form the undirected clique complex \(K_\lambda\). As \(\lambda\) decreases, more edges appear:

\[
\lambda_1>\lambda_2
\implies
K_{\lambda_1}\subseteq K_{\lambda_2}.
\]

Thus SIT naturally gives a filtered topological space.

---

### 7.3 Persistent information homology

Define homology groups

\[
H_k(K_\lambda).
\]

The persistence of a homology class across thresholds measures the robustness of an information structure.

For example:

- \(H_0\) tracks information-connected components.
- \(H_1\) tracks informational loops or cyclic dependencies.
- \(H_2\) tracks higher-order cavities in the accessibility structure.

A topological feature persistent over a wide interval of \(\lambda\) is structurally significant.

---

### 7.4 Stability theorem

Let \(A\) and \(A'\) be two accessibility kernels on the same finite set \(X\). Suppose

\[
\sup_{x,y}|A(x,y)-A'(x,y)|\leq \varepsilon.
\]

Let \(D(A)\) and \(D(A')\) be their persistence diagrams constructed from symmetric clique filtrations.

Then the bottleneck distance satisfies

\[
d_B(D(A),D(A'))
\leq
\varepsilon.
\]

This is a direct consequence of the standard stability theorem for filtrations induced by real-valued functions.

Thus small perturbations in information accessibility induce only small changes in persistent topological features.

---

## 8. Applications to Distributed Computing

### 8.1 Processes as information points

Let \(P\) be a set of processes in a distributed system. Let

\[
C_{ij}
\]

be the normalized reliability, bandwidth, or decode probability from process \(j\) to process \(i\). The closure

\[
A^i{}_j=C^*{}^i{}_j
\]

defines the accessibility of process \(j\)'s state from process \(i\).

The SIT space \((P,A)\) captures the effective topology of the distributed system.

Physical adjacency is secondary. What matters is informational reachability.

---

### 8.2 Knowledge neighborhoods

Let \(X\) be the set of global states of a protocol. Each process \(p\) has an observation map

\[
\omega_p:X\to O_p.
\]

The information neighborhood of a state \(x\) for process \(p\) is

\[
B^p(x)
=
\{
y\in X:
\omega_p(y)=\omega_p(x)
\}.
\]

More generally, one can define graded neighborhoods

\[
B^p_\lambda(x)
=
\{
y\in X:
A_p(x,y)\geq \lambda
\},
\]

where \(A_p(x,y)\) quantifies the degree to which process \(p\) can distinguish \(x\) from \(y\).

The knowledge operator is then

\[
K_p(U)
=
\{
x\in X:
B^p(x)\subseteq U
\}.
\]

This is exactly the interior operator for the information topology of process \(p\).

Common knowledge corresponds to iterated or joint interior operations across multiple agents.

---

### 8.3 Consensus and connectedness

In consensus, processes must decide on a common value. Suppose the decision map

\[
D:X\to \{0,1\}
\]

is information-continuous. Then

\[
D^{-1}(0),\quad D^{-1}(1)
\]

are information-open if the discrete topology is placed on \(\{0,1\}\).

If the information topology on the reachable state space \(X\) is connected, then it cannot be decomposed into two disjoint nonempty open sets. Therefore a continuous decision map cannot separate bivalent states into distinct stable decisions.

This gives a topological obstruction to consensus:

> If the reachable information topology remains connected under the admissible failure model, then no continuous protocol can separate the space into two disjoint decision regions.

This is not a replacement for classical impossibility proofs such as FLP, but it recasts them in terms of information connectedness, indistinguishability, and topological separation.

---

### 8.4 Byzantine resilience and intersection properties

In Byzantine settings, let \(f\) be the maximum number of faulty processes. A natural SIT resilience condition is the **\(f\)-intersection property**:

For every set \(Q\subseteq P\) of correct processes with \(|Q|\geq f+1\), there exists \(\lambda>0\) such that

\[
\bigcap_{p\in Q} B_\lambda(p)
\neq
\varnothing.
\]

Stronger forms require the intersection to contain a quorum of correct processes.

This condition formalizes the idea that correct processes must retain a common information region. Failure of this intersection property corresponds to a topological fragmentation of the system.

---

## 9. Applications to Network Topology

### 9.1 Networks as information spaces

A network is often represented by a graph \(G=(V,E)\). SIT refines this by replacing adjacency with an accessibility tensor

\[
A^i{}_j.
\]

This tensor may be inferred from:

- packet flow,
- latency,
- bandwidth,
- mutual information,
- transfer entropy,
- routing tables,
- congestion patterns,
- reliability under failure.

The physical graph may be static while the information topology changes dynamically.

---

### 9.2 Information connected components

For a threshold \(\lambda\), define two nodes to be \(\lambda\)-information-connected if they lie in the same connected component of \(G_\lambda\).

The zeroth homology

\[
H_0(G_\lambda)
\]

describes information components.

As \(\lambda\) decreases, components merge. The merge tree is a dendrogram of informational connectivity.

---

### 9.3 Information cuts

Define the information cut between \(S\subseteq V\) and \(V\setminus S\) by

\[
\operatorname{cut}(S)
=
\sum_{i\in S,\, j\notin S} K^{ij}.
\]

The minimum information cut is

\[
\kappa
=
\min_{S}
\operatorname{cut}(S).
\]

Low values of \(\kappa\) identify topological bottlenecks.

In tensor notation, the cut functional is

\[
\chi_S
=
\mathbf{1}_S,
\]

and the cut energy may be written

\[
\operatorname{cut}(S)
=
\frac{1}{2}
K^{ij}
|\chi_i-\chi_j|.
\]

This connects SIT to graph Laplacians and isoperimetric inequalities.

---

### 9.4 Centrality as topological persistence

A node is information-critical if its removal causes a large change in persistent homology.

Define the topological fragility of node \(v\) by

\[
\Phi(v)
=
\sum_k
w_k
\,d_B
\left(
D_k(A),
D_k(A^{(-v)})
\right),
\]

where \(A^{(-v)}\) is the accessibility kernel after removing \(v\), \(D_k\) is the persistence diagram in dimension \(k\), and \(w_k\) are weights.

This gives a topology-based centrality measure.

---

## 10. Applications to Artificial Intelligence

### 10.1 Latent spaces as information spaces

Let \(Z\) be a latent representation space. A model induces similarity, attention, or mutual-information relations among latent points.

Define

\[
A(z,z')
=
\text{normalized accessibility of } z' \text{ from } z.
\]

Then latent neighborhoods are not necessarily Euclidean balls. They are regions of mutual inferential accessibility.

This is especially important because learned representations often have non-Euclidean, anisotropic, and task-dependent geometry.

---

### 10.2 Attention topology

In a transformer layer, attention weights define a raw accessibility tensor

\[
\alpha^i{}_j
=
\operatorname{softmax}_j
\left(
\frac{Q^i K_j^\top}{\sqrt{d}}
\right).
\]

The raw attention matrix is not generally transitive. Its information closure

\[
A^i{}_j
=
(\alpha^*)^i{}_j
\]

defines the effective attention topology.

Information neighborhoods

\[
B_\lambda(i)
=
\{j:A^i{}_j\geq \lambda\}
\]

describe the set of tokens or latent units that are contextually accessible from token \(i\) above threshold \(\lambda\).

This provides a principled way to study:

- context propagation,
- attention sinks,
- layerwise topology,
- interpretability,
- robustness,
- collapse of contextual diversity.

---

### 10.3 Semantic continuity and robustness

Let

\[
f:X\to Y
\]

be a model mapping inputs to outputs. Equip \(X\) with an information topology \(\tau_{I,X}\) and \(Y\) with a task topology \(\tau_{I,Y}\).

The model is **information-continuous** if

\[
U\in\tau_{I,Y}
\implies
f^{-1}(U)\in\tau_{I,X}.
\]

This means that inputs that are informationally indistinguishable up to a threshold are mapped to outputs that remain within a stable region.

A robustness radius at \(x\) with respect to an acceptable output region \(V\ni f(x)\) is

\[
r(x)
=
\sup
\left\{
\lambda:
B_\lambda(x)\subseteq f^{-1}(V)
\right\}.
\]

Large \(r(x)\) means the model is stable over a large information neighborhood.

---

### 10.4 Generalization as topological compatibility

Let \(D\subseteq X\) be training data with labels

\[
\ell:D\to \mathcal{Y}.
\]

Generalization is easier when the labeling function is compatible with the information topology:

\[
x\sim_\lambda y
\implies
\ell(x)\approx \ell(y).
\]

Equivalently, the label map should be approximately continuous:

\[
\ell^{-1}(C)
\]

should be information-open or at least information-regular for relevant classes \(C\).

Topological violations occur when points with high mutual accessibility receive contradictory labels without a separating information boundary. This creates unstable decision boundaries.

---

### 10.5 Model collapse and topological contraction

In generative model training, repeated sampling from model outputs may shrink the effective information topology. Define the accessibility kernel at training generation \(t\) as \(A_t\). Model collapse corresponds to a contraction:

\[
A_{t+1}(x,y)
\geq
A_t(x,y)
\]

for points inside a narrow high-density region, but

\[
A_{t+1}(x,y)
\approx 0
\]

for formerly accessible modes.

Topologically, persistent connected components disappear and the support of the distribution fragments or contracts. SIT provides a language for diagnosing this as loss of topological support rather than merely increase in a scalar loss.

---

## 11. Applications to Communication Systems

### 11.1 Channels as accessibility kernels

Let \(X\) be a set of terminals, relays, base stations, or network regions. Let

\[
C_{ij}
\]

be the normalized Shannon capacity from node \(j\) to node \(i\), or a reliability measure derived from error exponents.

Define

\[
A_{ij}
=
\frac{C_{ij}}{C_{\max}},
\]

or more generally map capacity into \([0,1]\).

Then

\[
B_\lambda(i)
\]

is the set of nodes about which node \(i\) can obtain information at normalized rate or reliability at least \(\lambda\).

---

### 11.2 Decodeability neighborhoods

Given a code family, define

\[
A(x,y)
=
\sup_{\text{codes}}
\Pr(\widehat{Y}=Y \mid \text{observation at }x).
\]

Then

\[
B_\lambda(x)
=
\{
y:
\text{there exists a code allowing }x
\text{ to infer }y\text{ with success}\geq \lambda
\}.
\]

Information topology becomes the topology of decodeability.

---

### 11.3 Network coding and cut constraints

For a source \(s\) and sink \(t\), define the normalized cut accessibility

\[
A(s,t)
=
\min_{S:\,s\in S,\,t\notin S}
\frac{
\operatorname{cap}(S,S^c)
}{
C_{\max}
}.
\]

By the max-flow min-cut theorem, \(A(s,t)\geq \lambda\) if and only if a rate at least \(\lambda C_{\max}\) can be routed or network-coded from \(s\) to \(t\) in suitable settings.

Thus

\[
t\in B_\lambda(s)
\]

is equivalent to the existence of a feasible communication path or flow of rate threshold \(\lambda\).

Communication feasibility becomes a topological membership relation.

---

### 11.4 MIMO and tensor channels

For multiple antennas, channels are naturally tensors. Let

\[
H^{r}{}_{t}
\]

be the channel coefficient from transmit antenna \(t\) to receive antenna \(r\). The capacity depends on the singular structure of \(H\).

An information accessibility tensor may be written

\[
A^{r}{}_{t}
=
f(H^{r}{}_{t}),
\]

where \(f\) encodes normalized capacity, mutual information, or reliability.

In multi-user MIMO, one obtains higher-order tensors

\[
A^{r_1 r_2}_{t_1 t_2},
\]

encoding joint accessibility of multiple streams. SIT can then treat multi-terminal communication as a higher-order information topology.

## 12. Worked Examples

### 12.1 Example: three-node chain

Let

\[
X=\{1,2,3\}.
\]

Let the raw channel be

\[
C_{12}=0.9,
\quad
C_{21}=0.9,
\]

\[
C_{23}=0.6,
\quad
C_{32}=0.6,
\]

and

\[
C_{13}=C_{31}=0.
\]

Set \(C_{ii}=1\).

The max–min closure gives

\[
A_{13}
=
\min(C_{12},C_{23})
=
0.6,
\]

and similarly

\[
A_{31}=0.6.
\]

Thus

\[
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 0.9 & 0.6\\
0.9 & 1 & 0.6\\
0.6 & 0.6 & 1
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

At threshold \(\lambda=0.8\),

\[
B_{0.8}(1)=\{1,2\},
\]

\[
B_{0.8}(3)=\{3\}.
\]

Node \(3\) is informationally separated from \(1\) and \(2\) at high resolution.

At threshold \(\lambda=0.5\),

\[
B_{0.5}(1)=B_{0.5}(2)=B_{0.5}(3)=\{1,2,3\}.
\]

Thus the topology becomes connected at lower resolution.

The persistence interval of the separation between \(\{1,2\}\) and \(\{3\}\) is

\[
[0.6,0.9).
\]

This interval quantifies the scale at which node \(3\) is topologically peripheral.

---

### 12.2 Example: discrete information diffusion

Let

\[
K^{ij}=A^{ij}
\]

and consider

\[
\partial_t \phi^i
=
\sum_j A^{ij}(\phi^j-\phi^i).
\]

For the above chain, the dynamics are

\[
\partial_t \phi^1
=
0.9(\phi^2-\phi^1)
+
0.6(\phi^3-\phi^1),
\]

\[
\partial_t \phi^2
=
0.9(\phi^1-\phi^2)
+
0.6(\phi^3-\phi^2),
\]

\[
\partial_t \phi^3
=
0.6(\phi^1-\phi^3)
+
0.6(\phi^2-\phi^3).
\]

The equilibrium satisfies

\[
\phi^1=\phi^2=\phi^3,
\]

provided the information graph is connected at positive weights.

The slowest relaxation mode is governed by the smallest nonzero eigenvalue of the information Laplacian. This eigenvalue is a spectral measure of topological bottleneck strength.

---

### 12.3 Example: Fisher information topology

Let \(X=\mathbb{R}^d\), and let each point \(\theta\) parameterize a Gaussian distribution

\[
p(z\mid \theta)
=
\mathcal{N}(\theta,\Sigma).
\]

The Fisher metric is

\[
g_{\mu\nu}
=
(\Sigma^{-1})_{\mu\nu}.
\]

Define

\[
A(\theta,\theta')
=
\exp
\left(
-\frac{1}{2\ell^2}
(\theta-\theta')^\top
\Sigma^{-1}
(\theta-\theta')
\right).
\]

Then information neighborhoods are ellipsoids:

\[
B_\lambda(\theta)
=
\left\{
\theta':
(\theta-\theta')^\top
\Sigma^{-1}
(\theta-\theta')
\leq
-2\ell^2\log\lambda
\right\}.
\]

This gives a statistically natural SIT structure in which topology is generated by inferential distinguishability.

## 13. Design Principles for SIT Systems

The preceding theory suggests several design principles for information-driven systems.

### Principle 1: Topology should be inferred from flow, not assumed from geometry

Physical proximity is not equivalent to informational proximity. SIT constructs topology from actual accessibility.

### Principle 2: Thresholds define scales

There is no single correct information topology. There is a filtration of topologies indexed by threshold.

### Principle 3: Persistent features are structural

Features stable across thresholds are more meaningful than features appearing at a single threshold.

### Principle 4: Continuity is robustness

A system is robust when its decision or control maps are continuous with respect to the relevant information topology.

### Principle 5: Failure is topological fragmentation

Loss of communication, Byzantine corruption, attention collapse, or channel degradation can all be understood as changes in information connectedness.

---

## 14. Limitations and Extensions

Several extensions are natural.

1. **Quantale-valued SIT.** Replace \([0,1]\) with a general quantale, allowing different composition laws.

2. **Directed and asymmetric topologies.** Many systems have asymmetric accessibility. SIT naturally yields quasi-topologies and specialization preorders.

3. **Higher categories.** Information channels between channels, protocols between protocols, and attention over attention suggest higher-categorical SIT.

4. **Quantum information.** Accessibility may be defined using quantum channels, entanglement-assisted capacities, or completely positive maps.

5. **Learning of accessibility kernels.** In empirical systems, \(A\) must be estimated from data. Stability theorems provide guarantees for estimation error.

6. **Privacy and adversarial SIT.** Privacy mechanisms can be viewed as deliberate reductions of accessibility, inducing topological obstructions.

---

## 15. Conclusion

Structural Information Topology provides a rigorous reversal of the classical topological paradigm. Instead of beginning with open sets and then defining continuity, it begins with information flow and derives open sets as stable regions of accessibility.

The central construction is simple:

\[
B_\lambda(x)
=
\{y:A(x,y)\geq \lambda\},
\]

\[
\tau_I
=
\left\{
U\subseteq X:
\forall x\in U,\exists\lambda,\ B_\lambda(x)\subseteq U
\right\}.
\]

Yet this construction has substantial consequences. It yields topologies, filtrations, tensorial flow equations, categorical morphisms, and topological invariants directly from information accessibility. It also gives a unified language for distributed computing, network analysis, artificial intelligence, and communication systems.

In SIT, topology is not a stage on which information moves. Topology is what information flow creates.
