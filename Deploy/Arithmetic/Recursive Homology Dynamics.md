# Recursive Homology Dynamics (RHD)

## A Functorial Theory of Evolving Topological Invariants

**White paper / academic preprint**

---

## Abstract

Classical homology assigns to a space \(X\) a static graded algebraic invariant \(H_*(X;R)\). Many natural and engineered systems, however, are not single spaces but sequences of spaces generated by repeated transformation: developmental processes, evolutionary dynamics, microstructural evolution, shape deformation, filtration growth, and iterative geometric refinement. This paper develops **Recursive Homology Dynamics** (RHD), a formal theory in which homological states evolve according to a recursive law

\[
\mathbf{H}_{n+1}=\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{H}_n),
\]

where \(\mathbf{H}_n=H_*(C_n)\) is the homology of a chain complex \(C_n\), and \(\mathcal{R}\) is an evolution operator induced by a chain-level recursion \(C_{n+1}=\Phi(C_n)\). We formulate RHD in the language of filtered chain complexes, derived categories, and persistence modules. A central construction is the **kernel recursion**

\[
C_{n+1}=(K\otimes C_n)\oplus L[1],
\]

for fixed filtered chain complexes \(K\) and \(L\). Over a field, the persistent Künneth theorem yields an explicit recursive law for homology:

\[
H_m(C_{n+1})
\cong
\bigoplus_{p+q=m} H_p(K)\otimes H_q(C_n)
\oplus
H_{m-1}(L).
\]

At the level of Betti numbers this becomes a linear dynamical system

\[
\beta_m(n+1)=\sum_{p+q=m}\kappa_p\,\beta_q(n)+\lambda_{m-1},
\]

with generating-function evolution

\[
B_{n+1}(z)=K(z)B_n(z)+zL(z).
\]

For persistent homology, the recursion acts on persistence diagrams through a barcode convolution. We prove stability bounds in terms of interleaving distance, define recursive Lyapunov exponents and homological entropy, and develop applications to persistent topology, biological evolution, materials science, and shape analysis. The resulting framework treats topology not as a fixed invariant but as a dynamical variable subject to recursion, feedback, stability, and asymptotic growth.

**Keywords:** recursive homology, persistent homology, filtered chain complexes, topological dynamics, persistence modules, barcode convolution, homological entropy, topological data analysis.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Static homology and dynamical reality

Homology is one of the central invariants of algebraic topology. To a topological space \(X\), or to a chain complex \(C_*(X)\), one associates graded groups

\[
H_q(X;R)=\ker \partial_q / \operatorname{im}\partial_{q+1},
\]

which encode connected components, tunnels, cavities, and higher-dimensional voids. In its classical form, homology is static: a space is given, and its homology is computed.

Many systems of scientific interest are not static. They evolve through discrete or continuous transformations:

\[
X_0 \longrightarrow X_1 \longrightarrow X_2 \longrightarrow \cdots.
\]

Examples include:

1. **Persistent topology**, where a filtration produces a family \(X_t\) indexed by scale \(t\).
2. **Biological evolution and development**, where organismal form, regulatory networks, or phenotypic spaces change across generations.
3. **Materials science**, where microstructures evolve under annealing, fracture, deposition, sintering, or phase separation.
4. **Shape analysis**, where objects undergo deformation, erosion, smoothing, growth, or registration.

In all such cases, homology is not merely a single invariant but a sequence

\[
\mathbf{H}_n = H_*(X_n).
\]

Recursive Homology Dynamics (RHD) studies this sequence as a dynamical system.

### 1.2 Core equation

The central formal law of RHD is

\[
\mathbf{H}_{n+1}=\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{H}_n),
\]

where \(\mathbf{H}_n\) is a graded homology object and \(\mathcal{R}\) is a recursive evolution operator.

This equation must be interpreted carefully. Homology is a functor

\[
H_*:\mathbf{Ch}_R \longrightarrow \mathbf{GrMod}_R,
\]

and not every chain-level transformation \(C_n\mapsto C_{n+1}\) factors through homology alone. In general, the state must include more than the bare groups \(H_q\); it may include chain homotopy type, persistence module structure, cup products, Steenrod operations, or an \(A_\infty\)-structure. RHD therefore distinguishes:

1. **Strict homological recursion**, where a functor \(\mathcal{R}\) on graded modules exists such that
   \[
   H_*(C_{n+1})\cong \mathcal{R}(H_*(C_n)).
   \]

2. **Derived recursion**, where the true state is the quasi-isomorphism class \([C_n]\) in a derived category, and homology is the observable projection.

The strict case is mathematically clean and sufficient for many applications. The derived case is the general setting.

### 1.3 Contributions

This paper develops RHD as a rigorous mathematical framework. The main contributions are:

1. A categorical definition of recursive homology dynamics.
2. A chain-level recursion formalism using endofunctors \(\Phi\) on filtered chain complexes.
3. An explicit kernel recursion
   \[
   C_{n+1}=(K\otimes C_n)\oplus L[1],
   \]
   leading to closed recursive formulas for homology and Betti numbers.
4. A persistent version of RHD based on barcode convolution.
5. Stability theorems under interleaving and bottleneck distances.
6. Dynamical notions: fixed points, periodic topological phases, recursive Lyapunov exponents, and homological entropy.
7. Applications to persistent topology, biological evolution, materials science, and shape analysis.

## 2. Algebraic and categorical preliminaries

### 2.1 Chain complexes

Let \(R\) be a commutative ring, often a field \(k\) or a principal ideal domain such as \(\mathbb{Z}\). A chain complex \(C\) over \(R\) is a sequence of \(R\)-modules

\[
\cdots \xrightarrow{\partial_{q+1}} C_q \xrightarrow{\partial_q} C_{q-1} \xrightarrow{\partial_{q-1}} \cdots
\]

with

\[
\partial_{q-1}\partial_q=0.
\]

The homology of \(C\) in degree \(q\) is

\[
H_q(C)=\ker \partial_q / \operatorname{im}\partial_{q+1}.
\]

In indexed notation, if \(\{e_i^q\}\) is a basis or generating set for \(C_q\), write

\[
\partial_q e_i^q = D^{q,q-1}_{ji} e_j^{q-1}.
\]

The chain condition becomes

\[
D^{q-1,q-2}_{kj}D^{q,q-1}_{ji}=0,
\]

using summation over repeated indices.

A chain map \(f:C\to D\) is a collection of module maps \(f_q:C_q\to D_q\) satisfying

\[
\partial^D_q f_q=f_{q-1}\partial^C_q.
\]

A chain map is a quasi-isomorphism if it induces isomorphisms on all homology groups.

### 2.2 Filtered chain complexes and persistence modules

A filtered chain complex over \(\mathbb{R}\) is a family

\[
C=\{C^t\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
\]

with inclusions \(C^s\hookrightarrow C^t\) for \(s\leq t\), and differentials

\[
\partial^t:C^t_q\to C^t_{q-1}
\]

compatible with inclusions.

The \(q\)-th persistent homology module is

\[
V_q(C)^t=H_q(C^t).
\]

The structure maps

\[
v_q^{s,t}:V_q(C)^s\to V_q(C)^t,\qquad s\leq t,
\]

are induced by filtration inclusions.

A persistence module \(V\) is **pointwise finite-dimensional** if each \(V^t\) is finite-dimensional. Under standard tame hypotheses, it decomposes as a direct sum of interval modules:

\[
V \cong \bigoplus_{I\in \mathcal{B}(V)} k_I,
\]

where \(\mathcal{B}(V)\) is a multiset of intervals, called the **barcode**. Equivalently, one may represent the barcode as a persistence diagram

\[
\operatorname{Dgm}(V)=\{(b,d)\},
\]

where \(b\) is a birth time and \(d\) a death time.

### 2.3 Interleaving distance

Given \(\epsilon\geq 0\), two persistence modules \(V,W\) are \(\epsilon\)-interleaved if there exist natural maps

\[
f^t:V^t\to W^{t+\epsilon},\qquad g^t:W^t\to V^{t+\epsilon}
\]

such that the obvious diagrams commute up to \(2\epsilon\). The interleaving distance is

\[
d_I(V,W)=\inf\{\epsilon\geq 0: V,W \text{ are }\epsilon\text{-interleaved}\}.
\]

For pointwise finite persistence modules, the bottleneck distance \(d_B\) between diagrams satisfies

\[
d_B(\operatorname{Dgm}(V),\operatorname{Dgm}(W))\leq d_I(V,W).
\]

This inequality is central for stability analysis.

## 3. Recursive Homology Dynamics

### 3.1 Recursive topological systems

Let \(\mathbf{Ch}^{\mathrm{filt}}_R\) denote a suitable category of filtered chain complexes over \(R\). A **recursive topological system** is a pair

\[
(C_0,\Phi),
\]

where \(C_0\in \mathbf{Ch}^{\mathrm{filt}}_R\) and

\[
\Phi:\mathbf{Ch}^{\mathrm{filt}}_R\to \mathbf{Ch}^{\mathrm{filt}}_R
\]

is an endofunctor or, more generally, an operation on objects and morphisms.

The recursive chain dynamics is

\[
C_{n+1}=\Phi(C_n),\qquad n=0,1,2,\dots
\]

Equivalently,

\[
C_n=\Phi^n(C_0).
\]

The homology state at step \(n\) is the graded module

\[
\mathbf{H}_n=H_*(C_n).
\]

The central question of RHD is whether there exists an operator

\[
\mathcal{R}:\mathbf{GrMod}_R\to \mathbf{GrMod}_R
\]

such that

\[
\mathbf{H}_{n+1}\cong \mathcal{R}(\mathbf{H}_n).
\]

When such an operator exists naturally, we say the system satisfies a **strict homological recursion**.

### 3.2 Homological Markov property

A recursive system has the **homological Markov property** if the homology of \(C_{n+1}\) depends naturally only on the homology of \(C_n\), not on hidden chain-level data.

Formally, \(\Phi\) has the homological Markov property if there exists a functor

\[
\mathcal{R}:\mathbf{GrMod}_R\to \mathbf{GrMod}_R
\]

and a natural isomorphism

\[
H_*(\Phi(C))\cong \mathcal{R}(H_*(C))
\]

for all \(C\) in the relevant subcategory.

This property is strong. Homology alone does not determine chain homotopy type, and therefore arbitrary topological recursions do not descend to homology. When the Markov property fails, the minimal state is not \(\mathbf{H}_n\) but a richer object, such as:

1. the quasi-isomorphism class \([C_n]\) in the derived category \(D(R)\);
2. the persistent module \(H_*(C_n)\) with structure maps;
3. a differential graded algebra or \(A_\infty\)-algebra;
4. a spectral sequence associated to the recursion.

Thus the equation

\[
\mathbf{H}_{n+1}=\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{H}_n)
\]

is best understood either as a strict recursion in favorable cases or as an observable projection of a derived recursion.

### 3.3 Kernel recursions

A broad and important class of strict recursions is obtained from tensor kernels.

Let \(K\) and \(L\) be fixed filtered chain complexes. Define

\[
\Phi_{K,L}(C)=(K\otimes C)\oplus L[1],
\]

where \([1]\) denotes degree shift:

\[
(L[1])_q=L_{q-1}.
\]

The differential on \(K\otimes C\) is the usual tensor differential

\[
\partial_{K\otimes C}(x\otimes y)
=
\partial_K x\otimes y
+
(-1)^{|x|}x\otimes \partial_C y.
\]

In indexed notation, if \(e_\alpha^p\in K_p\) and \(f_i^q(n)\in (C_n)_q\), then

\[
\begin{aligned}
\partial_{n+1}(e_\alpha^p\otimes f_i^q(n))
&=
K^{p,p-1}_{\beta\alpha} e_\beta^{p-1}\otimes f_i^q(n) \\
&\quad+
(-1)^p e_\alpha^p\otimes D^{q,q-1}_{ji}(n) f_j^{q-1}(n).
\end{aligned}
\]

The recursion is therefore

\[
C_{n+1}=(K\otimes C_n)\oplus L[1].
\]

Topologically, if \(K\) and \(C_n\) are realized by spaces, the tensor product corresponds, up to quasi-isomorphism, to a product construction via the Eilenberg–Zilber theorem:

\[
C_*(K_{\mathrm{top}}\times X_n)\simeq C_*(K_{\mathrm{top}})\otimes C_*(X_n).
\]

The summand \(L[1]\) models the addition of new topological features through attachment or innovation.

### 3.4 Recursive homology formula

Assume for simplicity that \(R=k\) is a field and that all complexes are of finite type.

**Theorem 3.1 (Recursive homology formula).**  
Let

\[
C_{n+1}=(K\otimes C_n)\oplus L[1].
\]

Then for each degree \(m\),

\[
H_m(C_{n+1})
\cong
\bigoplus_{p+q=m} H_p(K)\otimes H_q(C_n)
\oplus
H_{m-1}(L).
\]

Equivalently, if \(\mathbf{H}_n=H_*(C_n)\), then

\[
\mathbf{H}_{n+1}=\mathcal{R}_{K,L}(\mathbf{H}_n),
\]

where

\[
\mathcal{R}_{K,L}(G)_m
=
\bigoplus_{p+q=m} H_p(K)\otimes G_q
\oplus
L_{m-1},
\]

with \(L_{m-1}=H_{m-1}(L)\).

**Proof.**  
By the Künneth theorem over a field,

\[
H_m(K\otimes C_n)
\cong
\bigoplus_{p+q=m} H_p(K)\otimes H_q(C_n).
\]

Since homology commutes with finite direct sums,

\[
H_m((K\otimes C_n)\oplus L[1])
\cong
H_m(K\otimes C_n)\oplus H_m(L[1]).
\]

Finally,

\[
H_m(L[1])\cong H_{m-1}(L).
\]

Combining these gives the result. \(\square\)

### 3.5 Torsion and universal coefficient refinements

When \(R\) is a principal ideal domain rather than a field, torsion contributes through Tor terms. The Künneth theorem gives a natural short exact sequence

\[
0
\to
\bigoplus_{p+q=m} H_p(K)\otimes_R H_q(C_n)
\to
H_m(K\otimes C_n)
\to
\bigoplus_{p+q=m-1} \operatorname{Tor}_1^R(H_p(K),H_q(C_n))
\to
0.
\]

Over a PID this sequence splits noncanonically, giving

\[
\begin{aligned}
H_m(C_{n+1})
&\cong
\bigoplus_{p+q=m} H_p(K)\otimes_R H_q(C_n) \\
&\quad\oplus
\bigoplus_{p+q=m-1} \operatorname{Tor}_1^R(H_p(K),H_q(C_n)) \\
&\quad\oplus
H_{m-1}(L).
\end{aligned}
\]

Thus torsion can be dynamically generated even if the initial complex is torsion-free. This gives rise to **torsion cascades**, in which recursive tensoring with a Moore-type kernel produces torsion classes in progressively higher degrees.

### 3.6 Betti-number dynamics

Let

\[
\kappa_p=\dim_k H_p(K),\qquad
\lambda_q=\dim_k H_q(L),\qquad
\beta_m(n)=\dim_k H_m(C_n).
\]

Taking dimensions in Theorem 3.1 gives

\[
\boxed{
\beta_m(n+1)
=
\sum_{p+q=m}\kappa_p\,\beta_q(n)
+
\lambda_{m-1}.
}
\]

With the convention \(\lambda_{-1}=0\), this is a discrete linear dynamical system.

Introduce the Betti generating polynomial

\[
B_n(z)=\sum_{m\geq 0}\beta_m(n)z^m,
\]

the kernel polynomial

\[
K(z)=\sum_{p\geq 0}\kappa_p z^p,
\]

and the innovation polynomial

\[
L(z)=\sum_{q\geq 0}\lambda_q z^q.
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{
B_{n+1}(z)=K(z)B_n(z)+zL(z).
}
\]

Iterating,

\[
\boxed{
B_n(z)
=
K(z)^n B_0(z)
+
z\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}K(z)^j\right)L(z).
}
\]

If \(L=0\), this reduces to the pure multiplicative recursion

\[
B_n(z)=K(z)^n B_0(z).
\]

### 3.7 Matrix formulation

Truncate degrees to \(0,\dots,D\). Define the Betti vector

\[
\boldsymbol{\beta}(n)=
\begin{pmatrix}
\beta_0(n)\\
\beta_1(n)\\
\vdots\\
\beta_D(n)
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Define the recursion matrix \(M_K\) by

\[
(M_K)_{m q}=
\begin{cases}
\kappa_{m-q}, & m\geq q,\\
0, & m<q.
\end{cases}
\]

Define

\[
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=
\begin{pmatrix}
0\\
\lambda_0\\
\lambda_1\\
\vdots\\
\lambda_{D-1}
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{
\boldsymbol{\beta}(n+1)=M_K\boldsymbol{\beta}(n)+\boldsymbol{\lambda}.
}
\]

In tensorial index notation,

\[
\beta^m_{n+1}=\kappa^m{}_q\,\beta^q_n+\lambda^m,
\]

where

\[
\kappa^m{}_q=\kappa_{m-q}.
\]

The formal solution is

\[
\boldsymbol{\beta}(n)
=
M_K^n\boldsymbol{\beta}(0)
+
\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}M_K^j\boldsymbol{\lambda}.
\]

### 3.8 Asymptotic growth and homological entropy

Assume \(M_K\) is a finite matrix. Its spectral radius

\[
\rho(M_K)=\max_i |\mu_i|
\]

controls the asymptotic growth of Betti numbers.

**Theorem 3.2 (Asymptotic homological growth).**  
Suppose \(M_K\) is diagonalizable over \(\mathbb{C}\) with eigenvalues \(\mu_1,\dots,\mu_s\). Then for each degree \(m\),

\[
\beta_m(n)
=
\sum_{i=1}^s c_{m,i}\mu_i^n
+
\beta_m^{\mathrm{part}}(n),
\]

where \(\beta_m^{\mathrm{part}}\) is a particular solution determined by \(L\). If \(\rho(M_K)>1\), some Betti numbers grow exponentially. If \(\rho(M_K)=1\) and the largest Jordan block has size \(r\), growth is at most polynomial of degree \(r-1\). If \(\rho(M_K)<1\), the homogeneous part decays and the recursion approaches a fixed point determined by \((I-M_K)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\lambda}\), when it exists.

This motivates the following definition.

**Definition 3.1 (Homological entropy).**  
For a linear Betti recursion with matrix \(M_K\), define

\[
h_{\mathrm{hom}}(\mathcal{R})=\log \rho(M_K).
\]

The quantity \(h_{\mathrm{hom}}\) measures the exponential rate of topological complexity growth under the recursion.

## 4. Persistent Recursive Homology

### 4.1 Filtered kernel recursion

Let \(K,L,C_n\) be filtered chain complexes. The filtered tensor product is defined by

\[
F_t(K\otimes C_n)
=
\sum_{a+b\leq t} F_a K\otimes F_b C_n.
\]

The shift \(L[1]\) may also carry a filtration shift \(\tau_L\), so that

\[
F_t(L[1])=F_{t-\tau_L}L[1].
\]

The persistent recursion is

\[
C_{n+1}=(K\otimes C_n)\oplus L[1].
\]

Taking persistent homology gives a recursion on persistence modules.

Let

\[
V_n^q=H_q(C_n)
\]

be the \(q\)-th persistent homology module at step \(n\), and let

\[
K^p=H_p(K),\qquad L^q=H_q(L)
\]

be the persistent homology modules of the kernel and innovation complexes.

### 4.2 Persistent Künneth recursion

Over a field and under q-tame finite-type assumptions, the persistent Künneth theorem gives

\[
\boxed{
V_{n+1}^m
\cong
\bigoplus_{p+q=m}
\left(K^p * V_n^q\right)
\oplus
L^{m-1}[\tau_L],
}
\]

where \(*\) denotes convolution of persistence modules and \([\tau_L]\) denotes translation of the filtration parameter by \(\tau_L\).

At the level of persistence diagrams,

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{Dgm}_{n+1}^m
=
\bigoplus_{p+q=m}
\operatorname{Dgm}_K^p * \operatorname{Dgm}_n^q
\oplus
\operatorname{Dgm}_L^{m-1}[\tau_L].
}
\]

This is the fundamental recursive law of persistent RHD.

### 4.3 Barcode convolution

For interval modules, the convolution may be described explicitly. Represent an interval by its birth and length:

\[
I=(b,\ell),\qquad \ell=d-b,
\]

where \(d\) may be \(\infty\). Define a semigroup operation

\[
(b,\ell)\star(u,m)=(b+u,\min(\ell,m)).
\]

The degree adds:

\[
q_{\mathrm{new}}=q_K+q_C.
\]

Thus a kernel interval

\[
(q_K,b_K,\ell_K)
\]

and a state interval

\[
(q_n,b_n,\ell_n)
\]

produce a new interval

\[
(q_K+q_n,\; b_K+b_n,\; \min(\ell_K,\ell_n)).
\]

Equivalently, in birth-death coordinates,

\[
[b_K+b_n,\; b_K+b_n+\min(\ell_K,\ell_n)).
\]

If the resulting interval has nonpositive length, it is discarded.

This operation is called **barcode convolution**. It provides a direct recursive update of persistence diagrams without reconstructing the full chain complex at each step.

### 4.4 Diagram measures

Let \(\mu_n\) be the persistence diagram of \(\mathbf{H}_n\), regarded as a locally finite measure on

\[
\mathbb{Z}\times \mathbb{R}\times [0,\infty].
\]

Let \(\mu_K\) and \(\mu_L\) be the diagram measures of \(K\) and \(L\). Then the persistent recursion may be written as

\[
\boxed{
\mu_{n+1}
=
\mu_K \star \mu_n
+
S_{\tau_L}\mu_L,
}
\]

where \(\star\) denotes pushforward of the product measure under the semigroup operation above, and \(S_{\tau_L}\) shifts filtration parameters by \(\tau_L\).

This measure-theoretic formulation is useful for stability analysis, statistical modeling, and machine learning.

### 4.5 Recursive persistence landscapes and surfaces

Given persistence diagrams \(\operatorname{Dgm}_n\), one may form persistence landscapes

\[
\Lambda_n=\Lambda(\operatorname{Dgm}_n).
\]

The recursion induces a nonlinear map on landscapes:

\[
\Lambda_{n+1}=\mathcal{R}_\Lambda(\Lambda_n).
\]

In practice, \(\mathcal{R}_\Lambda\) is computed by diagram convolution followed by landscape transformation. This yields vector-valued time series

\[
\Lambda_0,\Lambda_1,\Lambda_2,\dots
\]

suitable for numerical analysis.

## 5. Stability, sensitivity, and topological chaos

### 5.1 Stable recursive functors

Let \(d_I\) denote interleaving distance on filtered chain complexes or persistence modules.

**Definition 5.1.** A recursive operator \(\Phi\) is \((A,\epsilon)\)-stable if

\[
d_I(\Phi(C),\Phi(D))
\leq
A\,d_I(C,D)+\epsilon
\]

for all admissible \(C,D\).

The case \(\epsilon=0\) and \(A=1\) is nonexpansive recursion. The case \(A<1\) is contractive. The case \(A>1\) allows amplification of perturbations.

### 5.2 Recursive stability theorem

**Theorem 5.1 (Recursive stability bound).**  
Let \(C_n=\Phi^n(C_0)\) and \(D_n=\Phi^n(D_0)\). If \(\Phi\) is \((A,\epsilon)\)-stable, then

\[
d_I(C_n,D_n)
\leq
A^n d_I(C_0,D_0)
+
\epsilon\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}A^j.
\]

In particular:

1. If \(A=1\),
   \[
   d_I(C_n,D_n)\leq d_I(C_0,D_0)+n\epsilon.
   \]

2. If \(A<1\),
   \[
   d_I(C_n,D_n)
   \leq
   A^n d_I(C_0,D_0)
   +
   \frac{\epsilon(1-A^n)}{1-A}.
   \]

3. If \(A>1\),
   \[
   d_I(C_n,D_n)
   \leq
   A^n d_I(C_0,D_0)
   +
   \epsilon\frac{A^n-1}{A-1}.
   \]

**Proof.**  
By induction. The case \(n=0\) is immediate. If the bound holds for \(n\), then

\[
\begin{aligned}
d_I(C_{n+1},D_{n+1})
&=
d_I(\Phi(C_n),\Phi(D_n)) \\
&\leq
A d_I(C_n,D_n)+\epsilon \\
&\leq
A\left(
A^n d_I(C_0,D_0)
+
\epsilon\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}A^j
\right)
+\epsilon \\
&=
A^{n+1}d_I(C_0,D_0)
+
\epsilon\sum_{j=0}^{n}A^j.
\end{aligned}
\]

This completes the induction. \(\square\)

Since bottleneck distance is bounded by interleaving distance, the same bounds apply to persistence diagrams.

### 5.3 Recursive Lyapunov exponents

For two initial states \(C_0,D_0\), define the **recursive Lyapunov exponent**

\[
\lambda_{\mathrm{RHD}}(C_0,D_0)
=
\limsup_{n\to\infty}
\frac{1}{n}
\log
\frac{d_I(C_n,D_n)}{d_I(C_0,D_0)}.
\]

If

\[
\lambda_{\mathrm{RHD}}<0,
\]

the recursion is topologically contractive near the trajectory. If

\[
\lambda_{\mathrm{RHD}}>0,
\]

small perturbations are exponentially amplified. This provides a rigorous notion of **topological chaos** in recursive homology dynamics.

For linear Betti recursions, perturbations satisfy

\[
\delta\boldsymbol{\beta}(n+1)=M_K\delta\boldsymbol{\beta}(n),
\]

so the asymptotic amplification rate is governed by \(\rho(M_K)\). Thus

\[
\lambda_{\mathrm{Betti}}=\log \rho(M_K)=h_{\mathrm{hom}}.
\]

### 5.4 Fixed points and periodic topological phases

A homological fixed point satisfies

\[
\mathbf{H}\cong \mathcal{R}(\mathbf{H}).
\]

For Betti recursions,

\[
\boldsymbol{\beta}=M_K\boldsymbol{\beta}+\boldsymbol{\lambda},
\]

or

\[
(I-M_K)\boldsymbol{\beta}=\boldsymbol{\lambda}.
\]

If \(I-M_K\) is invertible and the solution is nonnegative and integral or realizable, it defines a stable topological phase.

A periodic topological phase of period \(p\) satisfies

\[
\mathbf{H}_{n+p}\cong \mathbf{H}_n.
\]

At the Betti level,

\[
\boldsymbol{\beta}(n+p)=\boldsymbol{\beta}(n).
\]

Such phases correspond to cycles in the space of topological states.

## 6. Illustrative models

### 6.1 Loop amplification by a circle kernel

Let \(K=S^1\), so

\[
\kappa_0=1,\qquad \kappa_1=1,
\]

and let \(L=0\). Then

\[
K(z)=1+z.
\]

If \(C_0\) has Betti polynomial \(B_0(z)\), then

\[
B_n(z)=(1+z)^n B_0(z).
\]

If \(C_0=S^1\), then

\[
B_0(z)=1+z,
\]

and hence

\[
B_n(z)=(1+z)^{n+1}.
\]

Therefore

\[
\beta_m(n)=\binom{n+1}{m}.
\]

In particular,

\[
\beta_0(n)=1,\qquad
\beta_1(n)=n+1,\qquad
\beta_2(n)=\binom{n+1}{2}.
\]

The recursion generates higher-dimensional cycles from repeated interaction with the circular kernel.

### 6.2 Torus kernel

Let \(K=T^2\). Then

\[
H_0(K)\cong k,\quad
H_1(K)\cong k^2,\quad
H_2(K)\cong k,
\]

so

\[
K(z)=1+2z+z^2=(1+z)^2.
\]

With \(L=0\),

\[
B_n(z)=(1+z)^{2n}B_0(z).
\]

The Betti distribution spreads polynomially rather than exponentially. The homological entropy is zero because the leading spectral behavior is polynomial, although topological complexity across degrees increases substantially.

### 6.3 Branching kernel and exponential growth

Let \(K\) have

\[
\kappa_0=2,\qquad \kappa_q=0\text{ for }q>0.
\]

Then

\[
K(z)=2,
\]

and

\[
\beta_m(n+1)=2\beta_m(n)+\lambda_{m-1}.
\]

If \(L=0\),

\[
\beta_m(n)=2^n\beta_m(0).
\]

This models topological branching in which each existing feature duplicates independently.

### 6.4 Torsion cascade

Work over \(R=\mathbb{Z}\). Let \(K\) be a Moore complex with

\[
H_1(K)\cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z},
\]

and let \(C_0\) have a free zeroth homology class. The Tor term

\[
\operatorname{Tor}_1^{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{Z})=0
\]

does not immediately generate torsion from a free module, but if subsequent steps produce finite cyclic summands, repeated tensoring can generate nontrivial Tor contributions. More generally, if

\[
H_p(K)\cong \mathbb{Z}/a,
\qquad
H_q(C_n)\cong \mathbb{Z}/b,
\]

then

\[
\operatorname{Tor}_1^{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}/a,\mathbb{Z}/b)
\cong
\mathbb{Z}/\gcd(a,b).
\]

Thus recursive dynamics can transport torsion between degrees and produce nontrivial finite-order topological features.

## 7. Applications

### 7.1 Persistent topology

Persistent homology traditionally studies a one-parameter family \(X_t\). RHD adds a second, recursive axis:

\[
(n,t)\in \mathbb{N}\times \mathbb{R}.
\]

The resulting object is a recursive persistence module

\[
V^m(n,t)=H_m(C_n^t).
\]

When natural maps \(C_n\to C_{n+1}\) exist, this becomes a two-parameter persistence module. When no natural maps exist, one may still study the sequence of diagrams

\[
\operatorname{Dgm}_n^m.
\]

RHD provides tools to distinguish:

1. persistent features stable under recursion;
2. transient features created and destroyed by the recursive operator;
3. topological phase transitions where Betti numbers or diagrams change qualitative behavior;
4. recursive noise, defined as features with short lifespan under repeated application of \(\mathcal{R}\).

A feature is **recursively stable** if its corresponding interval persists under repeated barcode convolution. In the interval semigroup model, an interval \((b,\ell)\) is recursively stable under kernel \(\mu_K\) if repeated convolution does not reduce its length below a threshold.

This yields a natural definition of **recursive persistence entropy**:

\[
S_n
=
-\sum_i p_{n,i}\log p_{n,i},
\]

where \(p_{n,i}\) is the normalized lifespan mass of the \(i\)-th interval in \(\operatorname{Dgm}_n\). The sequence \(S_n\) measures the redistribution of topological complexity under recursion.

### 7.2 Biological evolution

In evolutionary and developmental modeling, one may represent a phenotype, regulatory network, or morphospace at generation \(g\) as a filtered simplicial complex \(X_g\). A coarse-grained evolutionary recursion has the form

\[
X_{g+1}=S\bigl(D(M(G_g))\bigr),
\]

where:

1. \(G_g\) is a genotype representation;
2. \(M\) is a mutation or variation operator;
3. \(D\) is a developmental map from genotype to phenotype;
4. \(S\) is a selection or environmental filtering operator.

Passing to chain complexes gives

\[
C_{g+1}=\Phi_E(C_g),
\]

where \(\Phi_E\) is an effective evolutionary recursion.

Homology classes can represent biologically meaningful structures:

1. \(\beta_0\): connected phenotypic components or isolated developmental attractors;
2. \(\beta_1\): regulatory feedback loops, cyclic interactions, or tunnels in morphospace;
3. \(\beta_2\): enclosed cavities, compartments, or constrained regions of phenotypic space.

The recursive law

\[
\mathbf{H}_{g+1}=\mathcal{R}_E(\mathbf{H}_g)
\]

models how topological complexity is inherited, amplified, or suppressed across generations.

A **topological innovation event** occurs when a new persistent class appears:

\[
\operatorname{Dgm}_{g+1}\setminus \operatorname{Dgm}_g\neq \varnothing.
\]

A **topological extinction event** occurs when a persistent class dies under recursion.

One may define a homological fitness functional

\[
\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{H})
=
\sum_q w_q \beta_q
-
\gamma\, C(\mathbf{H}),
\]

where \(w_q\) are fitness weights and \(C(\mathbf{H})\) is a cost or complexity penalty. Selection then biases the choice of recursive kernel \(K\) or innovation term \(L\). In this way, RHD provides a mathematical language for evolvability, robustness, and topological novelty.

### 7.3 Materials science

Material microstructures evolve under processing: annealing, additive manufacturing, sintering, fracture, corrosion, phase separation, and grain growth. Let \(\Omega_n\) denote the material domain or phase geometry after processing step \(n\). A material recursion is

\[
\Omega_{n+1}=T(\Omega_n),
\]

or, at the chain level,

\[
C_{n+1}=\Phi_T(C_n).
\]

Homological degrees have direct physical interpretations:

1. \(\beta_0\): connected components of a phase;
2. \(\beta_1\): tunnels, conducting loops, or dislocation cycles;
3. \(\beta_2\): pores, voids, or enclosed inclusions.

For a conducting phase, the disappearance of \(\beta_0\) components and the formation of percolating paths can be tracked by persistent \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\). In porous media, \(\beta_2\) tracks isolated voids, while \(\beta_1\) tracks connected channel networks.

There is also a continuum formulation. Let \(\Omega_n\) be a Riemannian domain. By Hodge theory,

\[
H^q_{\mathrm{dR}}(\Omega_n)
\cong
\mathcal{H}^q(\Omega_n),
\]

where \(\mathcal{H}^q(\Omega_n)\) is the space of harmonic \(q\)-forms:

\[
\mathcal{H}^q(\Omega_n)
=
\{\omega\in \Omega^q(\Omega_n): d\omega=0,\; d^*\omega=0\}.
\]

The dimension of \(\mathcal{H}^q\) is \(\beta_q\). Physical fields often have cohomological degrees of freedom. For example, in transport problems,

\[
\nabla_i J^i=0,
\qquad
J^i=\sigma^{ij}E_j,
\]

where \(\sigma^{ij}\) is a conductivity tensor. Nontrivial harmonic one-forms represent independent circulation or flux channels. If \(\{\gamma_a\}_{a=1}^{\beta_1}\) is a basis of one-cycles, the periods

\[
P_a=\oint_{\gamma_a} E_i\,dx^i
\]

encode topologically protected field data. Recursive evolution of \(H_1(\Omega_n)\) changes the cycle basis and hence the allowed topological degrees of freedom.

Thus RHD links microstructural topology to effective physical tensors. In schematic form,

\[
\sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}^{ij}(n)
=
G^{ij}(n)
+
T^{ij}_{\mathrm{top}}(n),
\]

where \(G^{ij}\) is a geometric contribution and \(T^{ij}_{\mathrm{top}}\) is constrained by the cohomological state \(\mathbf{H}_n\).

### 7.4 Shape analysis

In shape analysis, an object may be represented as a filtered simplicial complex, cubical complex, or Vietoris–Rips complex built from a point cloud. A shape recursion may arise from:

1. deformation;
2. curvature flow;
3. erosion and dilation;
4. subdivision;
5. growth models;
6. learned transformations in machine learning pipelines.

Let \(X_n\) be the shape at step \(n\). The recursive homology sequence

\[
\mathbf{H}_n=H_*(X_n)
\]

provides a topological signature of the shape trajectory.

A recursive shape distance may be defined by

\[
d_{\mathrm{RHD}}(X,Y)
=
\sum_{n=0}^N w_n\,
d_B\left(
\operatorname{Dgm}(X_n),
\operatorname{Dgm}(Y_n)
\right),
\]

where \(X_n=\Phi^n(X)\), \(Y_n=\Phi^n(Y)\), and \(w_n\) are weights. If \(\Phi\) is stable, this distance is robust to perturbations.

RHD also distinguishes stable shape features from unstable ones. A topological feature that survives many recursive steps is more intrinsic than one that disappears immediately. This is especially useful in noisy shape data, where ordinary persistent homology may treat noise and geometry symmetrically. Recursive filtering provides an additional criterion: **survival under recursion**.

## 8. Computational framework

### 8.1 Direct recursive computation

A direct algorithm computes the chain recursion explicitly.

**Algorithm 1: Recursive homology computation**

Input:

- initial filtered complex \(C_0\);
- kernel \(K\);
- innovation complex \(L\);
- number of steps \(N\).

For \(n=0,\dots,N-1\):

1. Form
   \[
   C_{n+1}=(K\otimes C_n)\oplus L[1].
   \]
2. Reduce \(C_{n+1}\) using algebraic collapses or persistence reduction.
3. Compute persistent homology of \(C_{n+1}\).
4. Store \(\operatorname{Dgm}_{n+1}\) and Betti numbers \(\beta_m(n+1)\).

Output:

\[
\{\operatorname{Dgm}_n,\beta_m(n)\}_{n=0}^N.
\]

The direct method can be expensive because the size of \(K\otimes C_n\) grows multiplicatively. It is therefore useful when the recursion has exploitable structure.

### 8.2 Betti-only recursion

If only Betti numbers are required, and the kernel recursion applies, one may use the matrix recursion

\[
\boldsymbol{\beta}(n+1)=M_K\boldsymbol{\beta}(n)+\boldsymbol{\lambda}.
\]

This reduces the computation to linear algebra. The cost is dominated by matrix-vector multiplication:

\[
O(D^2)
\]

per step for degree truncation \(D\), or \(O(D\log D)\) using polynomial multiplication if the recursion is convolutional.

### 8.3 Barcode convolution recursion

If persistent diagrams are available and the kernel diagram \(\operatorname{Dgm}_K\) is fixed, one may update diagrams by convolution:

\[
\operatorname{Dgm}_{n+1}
=
\operatorname{Dgm}_K * \operatorname{Dgm}_n
\oplus
\operatorname{Dgm}_L[\tau_L].
\]

Naively, this costs

\[
O(|\operatorname{Dgm}_K|\cdot |\operatorname{Dgm}_n|)
\]

per step. Approximations can be made using:

1. persistence landscapes;
2. persistence images;
3. kernel embeddings;
4. truncation of short intervals;
5. stochastic sampling of diagram pairs.

### 8.4 Inverse recursive homology

Given observed diagrams

\[
\operatorname{Dgm}_0,\operatorname{Dgm}_1,\dots,\operatorname{Dgm}_N,
\]

one may attempt to infer a recursive kernel \(K\) and innovation term \(L\). The inverse problem is

\[
\min_{K,L}
\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}
d_B\left(
\operatorname{Dgm}_{n+1},
\operatorname{Dgm}_K * \operatorname{Dgm}_n
\oplus
\operatorname{Dgm}_L[\tau_L]
\right).
\]

This is a topological deconvolution problem. In the Betti-only case, it becomes a linear inverse problem:

\[
\min_{M_K,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}
\left\|
\boldsymbol{\beta}(n+1)-M_K\boldsymbol{\beta}(n)-\boldsymbol{\lambda}
\right\|^2.
\]

Regularization is necessary because the inverse problem is typically ill-posed.

## 9. Nonlinear and feedback recursions

The linear kernel recursion is analytically tractable, but many applications require feedback. A nonlinear RHD system may be written as

\[
C_{n+1}=\Phi_{H_n}(C_n),
\]

where the operator depends on the current homological state.

At the Betti level,

\[
\boldsymbol{\beta}(n+1)
=
M_{K(\boldsymbol{\beta}(n))}\boldsymbol{\beta}(n)
+
\boldsymbol{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{\beta}(n)).
\]

This is a nonlinear discrete dynamical system on Betti vectors. It can model:

1. saturation effects;
2. biological selection penalizing excessive complexity;
3. material failure triggered by topological defects;
4. shape regularization suppressing small handles.

A simple nonlinear example is

\[
\beta_0(n+1)
=
\frac{a\beta_0(n)}{1+b\beta_0(n)},
\]

which introduces logistic saturation. Higher-degree couplings can be modeled by polynomial maps

\[
\beta_m(n+1)
=
\sum_{p+q=m}\kappa_p(\boldsymbol{\beta}(n))\beta_q(n)
+
\lambda_{m-1}(\boldsymbol{\beta}(n)).
\]

The categorical formulation remains

\[
C_{n+1}=\Phi_n(C_n),
\]

but \(\Phi_n\) is now state-dependent.

## 10. Derived-category formulation

The most general formulation of RHD uses the derived category \(D(R)\). A recursive system is an endofunctor

\[
\Phi:D(R)\to D(R),
\]

with

\[
C_{n+1}=\Phi(C_n).
\]

Homology is a functor

\[
H_*:D(R)\to \mathbf{GrMod}_R.
\]

The observable dynamics is

\[
\mathbf{H}_n=H_*(C_n).
\]

If \(\Phi\) descends to the Grothendieck group \(K_0(D(R))\), it induces a linear map

\[
[\Phi]:K_0(D(R))\to K_0(D(R)).
\]

For kernel recursion,

\[
[\Phi_{K,L}]([C])=[K]\cdot [C]+[L[1]].
\]

The Betti recursion is the image of this categorical recursion under the rank character

\[
\chi:K_0(D(R))\to \mathbb{Z}[[z]],
\]

sending a complex to its graded rank polynomial.

This perspective clarifies that RHD is fundamentally a dynamical theory of derived objects, with homology as a measurable projection.

## 11. Limitations and open problems

Several mathematical issues remain central.

1. **Non-Markovianity.**  
   Homology alone does not determine chain homotopy type. Many recursions cannot be reduced to \(\mathbf{H}_{n+1}=\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{H}_n)\) without enrichment.

2. **Realizability.**  
   Not every algebraic recursion corresponds to a natural topological operation on spaces. Determining which operators \(\mathcal{R}\) are topologically realizable is an open problem.

3. **Nonlinear stability.**  
   Stability theory for linear kernel recursions is relatively straightforward. Nonlinear feedback recursions require a more delicate analysis.

4. **Inverse problems.**  
   Inferring recursive operators from observed homological time series is ill-posed and requires regularization, priors, or statistical models.

5. **Computational scalability.**  
   Direct tensor recursions grow rapidly. Efficient implicit representations, compression schemes, and approximate convolutions are needed.

6. **Higher structure.**  
   Cup products, Massey products, Steenrod operations, and \(A_\infty\)-structures may be necessary for full recursive fidelity.

These problems define a research program rather than a completed theory.

---

## 12. Conclusion

Recursive Homology Dynamics proposes a shift in perspective: homology is not merely a static invariant of a space but a dynamical variable evolving under recursion. The formal law

\[
\mathbf{H}_{n+1}=\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{H}_n)
\]

becomes mathematically precise when lifted to chain complexes, persistence modules, or derived categories.

The kernel recursion

\[
C_{n+1}=(K\otimes C_n)\oplus L[1]
\]

provides a tractable model with explicit homological consequences. It yields closed formulas for Betti-number evolution, barcode convolution for persistent diagrams, stability bounds under interleaving distance, and dynamical invariants such as homological entropy and recursive Lyapunov exponents.

The framework applies naturally to persistent topology, evolutionary biology, materials science, and shape analysis. In each domain, recursion captures the fact that topology is not merely observed once but produced, transformed, selected, and inherited through iterative processes.

RHD therefore offers a unifying algebraic-topological language for evolving structure.
