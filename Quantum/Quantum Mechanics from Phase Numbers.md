## Abstract

This paper gives a constructive reconstruction of nonrelativistic quantum mechanics in which the primitive scalar object is a **phase number**, rather than an imaginary number. A phase number is an element of the unit circle group generated by an orthogonal quarter-turn operator \(J\) on a real Hilbert space. The operator satisfies \(J^2=-I\), but no symbol \(i\) is introduced as a primitive scalar. The conventional complex field is recovered as the two-dimensional real scalar algebra \(\mathbb C_J=\{aI+bJ:a,b\in\mathbb R\}\), while its unit-norm elements \(u_\theta=\cos\theta\,I+\sin\theta\,J\) are the phase numbers. This construction separates the globally defined phase factor from any local angle used to parameterize it.

Starting from a real Hilbert space \((\mathcal H_\mathbb R,g)\) equipped with an orthogonal complex structure \(J\), the paper derives the phase-valued inner product, ray space, Born transition probability, self-adjoint observables, and the form of continuous reversible dynamics. A norm-preserving, phase-covariant one-parameter evolution has generator \(-JH/\hbar\), with \(H\) self-adjoint; hence the Schrödinger equation is obtained as \(J\hbar\partial_t\psi=H\psi\). In position representation this produces the familiar free-particle, potential, canonical-commutator, uncertainty, and harmonic-oscillator structures without writing a primitive imaginary unit. The polar decomposition \(\psi=\sqrt\rho\,u_{S/\hbar}\) yields the continuity equation and quantum Hamilton–Jacobi equation directly in terms of an amplitude and an intrinsic phase field.

The same calculus gives local gauge covariance, Aharonov–Bohm transport, tensor-product composition, spin-\(\tfrac12\) rotations, path-integral phase weights, and Berry holonomy. The central result is an equivalence theorem: phase-number quantum mechanics is exactly ordinary complex quantum mechanics expressed on the underlying real Hilbert space together with its selected complex structure. The construction is therefore not a modification of empirical quantum theory. Its contribution is analytical: it identifies \(U(1)\)-valued phase as the primary global object, treats \(J\) as the intrinsic quarter-turn that generates it, and makes branch-independent phase transport and topology explicit from the start.

> **Main theorem.** Given a real Hilbert space with an orthogonal complex structure \(J\), the phase-number formalism reconstructs a complex Hilbert space and all standard nonrelativistic quantum kinematics and dynamics. Conversely, every complex Hilbert space determines such a real Hilbert space and \(J\). The two presentations are unitarily equivalent.

## 1. Introduction and statement of the problem

Quantum mechanics is conventionally presented over the complex field. The standard notation is compact, but it conflates three logically distinct ingredients: the two-dimensional real algebra generated by a quarter-turn; the unit-circle factors that compose quantum phases; and local real angles chosen to coordinatize those factors. This conflation is usually harmless in elementary calculations. It becomes structurally costly, however, when one studies global phase, gauge transition functions, nodal singularities, topological winding, Berry transport, or phase-sensitive numerical calculations. In all of these settings the physical object is a unitary factor, while an angle is at most a local logarithmic coordinate.

The supplied *Phase Numbers* manuscript establishes this distinction at the level of \(U(1)\), covering maps, line bundles, holonomy, and the Berry phase. The present paper carries that structure into the full architecture of nonrelativistic quantum mechanics. The goal is not to remove the geometric content commonly compressed into the imaginary unit. Rather, it is to make that content explicit and intrinsic. The real-linear operator \(J\) is the quarter-turn on every phase plane; its exponentials are phase numbers; and the usual complex scalar notation is a faithful shorthand that may be recovered after the construction is complete.

This approach is compatible with the established fact that quantum theories can be formulated over real, complex, or quaternionic Hilbert spaces under broad axiomatic hypotheses, while physical symmetry requirements can select a complex structure in an underlying real representation.[1] The present construction begins after that selection: it treats the selected complex structure as a physical-geometric datum and develops quantum theory in its associated phase-number algebra. The projective formulation is equally natural, because physical pure states are rays and transition probabilities are invariant under unit phase multiplication.[2]

A purely mathematical group cannot, by itself, derive the empirical content of quantum mechanics. The derivation below therefore states its physical postulates openly. The postulates are: a real Hilbert space of amplitudes with a distinguished phase-plane structure; ray equivalence under unit phase factors; the Born transition law; and continuous reversible evolution preserving that structure. From these postulates the usual quantum formalism follows. This is a **constructive reconstruction**, not a claim that experimental dynamics can be inferred from circle topology alone.

Sections 6–10 derive coordinate quantum mechanics, local gauge transport, composition and spin, path amplitudes, and geometric phase. ## 2. Phase-number scalar algebra

### 2.1 The intrinsic quarter-turn

Let \(\mathcal H_\mathbb R\) be a real Hilbert space with real inner product \(g(\cdot,\cdot)\). A **phase structure** on \(\mathcal H_\mathbb R\) is a bounded real-linear operator

\[
J:\mathcal H_\mathbb R\to\mathcal H_\mathbb R
\]

such that

\[
J^2=-I,
\qquad
J^*=-J,
\qquad
J^*J=I.
\tag{2.1}
\]

The first relation states that applying \(J\) twice reverses a vector. The second and third state that \(J\) is skew-adjoint and orthogonal. Equivalently,

\[
g(J\phi,J\psi)=g(\phi,\psi),
\qquad
g(J\phi,\psi)=-g(\phi,J\psi).
\tag{2.2}
\]

Thus every nonzero vector \(\psi\) spans, together with \(J\psi\), an oriented Euclidean phase plane. The operator \(J\) is not a numerical angle. It is the coordinate-free operation of a quarter turn in that plane.

Define the real two-dimensional algebra

\[
\mathbb C_J\equiv \{aI+bJ\;|\;a,b\in\mathbb R\}.
\tag{2.3}
\]

Addition and composition are inherited from real-linear operators. Because \(J^2=-I\),

\[
(aI+bJ)(cI+dJ)=(ac-bd)I+(ad+bc)J.
\tag{2.4}
\]

The involution, real part, \(J\)-coefficient, and norm are

\[
(aI+bJ)^\sharp=aI-bJ,
\qquad
\operatorname{Re}_J(aI+bJ)=a,
\qquad
\operatorname{Im}_J(aI+bJ)=b,
\tag{2.5}
\]

\[
N_J(aI+bJ)\equiv(aI+bJ)^\sharp(aI+bJ)=a^2+b^2.
\tag{2.6}
\]

The map \(aI+bJ\mapsto a+ib\) is an algebra isomorphism, but it is not taken as a definition. The phase-number construction therefore has exactly the algebraic capacity of the complex field while making the generating geometry visible.

### 2.2 Phase numbers and their global character

A **phase number** is a unit-norm element of \(\mathbb C_J\):

\[
\mathsf P_J\equiv\{u\in\mathbb C_J:N_J(u)=1\}.
\tag{2.7}
\]

For every real \(\theta\), the exponential series converges to

\[
\operatorname{Ph}_J(\theta)
\equiv e^{\theta J}
=\cos\theta\,I+\sin\theta\,J.
\tag{2.8}
\]

It follows directly from (2.4) that

\[
\operatorname{Ph}_J(\alpha)\operatorname{Ph}_J(\beta)
=\operatorname{Ph}_J(\alpha+\beta),
\qquad
\operatorname{Ph}_J(\theta)^\sharp=
\operatorname{Ph}_J(-\theta).
\tag{2.9}
\]

The exponential map is surjective onto \(\mathsf P_J\) and has kernel \(2\pi\mathbb Z\). Thus \(\mathsf P_J\cong\mathbb R/(2\pi\mathbb Z)\cong U(1)\). The phase number \(u\) is globally well defined; a parameter \(\theta\) satisfying \(u=e^{\theta J}\) is defined only modulo \(2\pi\). This reproduces the covering-space distinction developed in the source manuscript without relying on an imaginary scalar.[3]

For any smooth phase-number field \(u:X\to\mathsf P_J\), the Maurer–Cartan form is

\[
\alpha_u\equiv -J\,u^{-1}du.
\tag{2.10}
\]

Here multiplication by \(-J\) maps the algebra-valued one-form \(u^{-1}du\), which is proportional to \(J\), to a real one-form. Locally, if \(u=e^{J\theta}\), then \(\alpha_u=d\theta\). Globally \(\alpha_u\) is closed but need not be exact. For a closed loop \(C\),

\[
\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint_C\alpha_u\in\mathbb Z.
\tag{2.11}
\]

This is the winding number. It will reappear as the topological charge of nodal phase fields, the integral content of gauge transport, and the obstruction to choosing a global phase lift.

### 2.3 Coordinate realization

For a finite-dimensional system with \(N\) phase degrees of freedom, one may choose the real coordinate realization

\[
\psi=\begin{pmatrix}q\\p\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb R^{2N},
\qquad
J=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&-I_N\\
I_N&0
\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{2.12}
\]

Then a phase number acts as a planar rotation in every \((q_k,p_k)\) plane:

\[
u_\theta=
\begin{pmatrix}
\cos\theta\,I_N&-\sin\theta\,I_N\\
\sin\theta\,I_N&\cos\theta\,I_N
\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{2.13}
\]

This display is useful computationally, but it is not fundamental. The invariant data are \(g\) and \(J\), not a particular splitting into coordinate components.

## 3. Kinematic postulates and phase-valued Hilbert geometry

### 3.1 Postulates

The reconstruction uses the following postulates.

**Postulate I (amplitude geometry).** The amplitudes of an isolated system form a real Hilbert space \((\mathcal H_\mathbb R,g)\) equipped with an orthogonal phase structure \(J\).

**Postulate II (ray equivalence).** Nonzero amplitudes \(\psi\) and \(u\psi\), where \(u\in\mathsf P_J\), represent the same pure physical state.

**Postulate III (transition probability).** The probability for a normalized ray \([\psi]\) to pass a normalized rank-one filter \([\phi]\) is the squared phase norm of their phase-valued overlap.

**Postulate IV (reversible dynamics).** Isolated time evolution is a strongly continuous one-parameter group of real-linear maps preserving both \(g\) and \(J\).

The content of the usual complex-Hilbert-space formulation is concentrated in the compatibility of the real metric \(g\) and the phase operator \(J\). Their combination also defines a symplectic form,

\[
\Omega(\phi,\psi)\equiv g(J\phi,\psi),
\tag{3.1}
\]

which is bilinear, antisymmetric, and nondegenerate. Quantum phase space is consequently Kähler in finite dimensions: metric, symplectic form, and phase structure are mutually compatible.

### 3.2 Phase-valued inner product

Define the \(\mathbb C_J\)-valued inner product

\[
\langle\phi,\psi\rangle_J
\equiv g(\phi,\psi)I+g(J\phi,\psi)J.
\tag{3.2}
\]

The construction has the expected conjugate symmetry:

\[
\langle\phi,\psi\rangle_J^\sharp
=\langle\psi,\phi\rangle_J.
\tag{3.3}
\]

It is phase-antilinear in the first argument and phase-linear in the second. Explicitly, for \(a,b\in\mathbb C_J\),

\[
\langle a\phi,b\psi\rangle_J
=a^\sharp\langle\phi,\psi\rangle_Jb.
\tag{3.4}
\]

Moreover,

\[
\langle\psi,\psi\rangle_J=g(\psi,\psi)I\ge0,
\tag{3.5}
\]

with equality only for \(\psi=0\). Therefore \(\mathcal H_J\equiv(\mathcal H_\mathbb R,J,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_J)\) is a Hilbert space over the phase scalar algebra \(\mathbb C_J\).

**Proposition 3.1.** Equation (3.2) is the unique phase-valued inner product whose real part is \(g\), whose phase multiplication is generated by \(J\), and which is linear in the second argument.

**Proof.** Let \(h\) be such an inner product. Since \(\operatorname{Re}_J h(\phi,\psi)=g(\phi,\psi)\), write \(h=g+B J\) for some real bilinear form \(B\). Phase-linearity gives \(h(\phi,J\psi)=h(\phi,\psi)J\). Equating real parts yields \(g(\phi,J\psi)=-B(\phi,\psi)\). By skew-adjointness of \(J\), \(-g(\phi,J\psi)=g(J\phi,\psi)\). Hence \(B(\phi,\psi)=g(J\phi,\psi)\), giving (3.2). \(\square\)

### 3.3 Rays, projectors, and global phase

The physical pure-state space is the phase projective space

\[
\mathbb P_J\mathcal H
\equiv(\mathcal H_\mathbb R\setminus\{0\})/\mathsf P_J.
\tag{3.6}
\]

A ray may equivalently be represented by its rank-one projector

\[
P_\psi\phi
\equiv
\psi\,\frac{\langle\psi,\phi\rangle_J}
{\langle\psi,\psi\rangle_J},
\qquad \psi\ne0.
\tag{3.7}
\]

Under \(\psi\mapsto u\psi\), the numerator and denominator acquire compensating phase factors, so \(P_{u\psi}=P_\psi\). The density-operator description thus arises as a phase-number invariant before any coordinate angle is introduced.

The quotient in (3.6) is not optional bookkeeping. It is the geometric statement that global phase has no observable effect for an isolated pure state. Wigner’s theorem formalizes the complementary statement: transformations of rays that preserve transition probabilities lift to linear or antilinear Hilbert-space isometries.[2] Continuous time evolution belongs to the phase-linear branch, while antilinear transformations are associated with discrete symmetries such as time reversal.

## 4. Probability, interference, and observables

### 4.1 Born transition probability

For nonzero \(\phi\) and \(\psi\), define

\[
\mathsf p(\phi\mid\psi)
=
\frac{N_J\!\left(\langle\phi,\psi\rangle_J\right)}
{g(\phi,\phi)g(\psi,\psi)}.
\tag{4.1}
\]

Using (3.2) and (2.6), this becomes

\[
\mathsf p(\phi\mid\psi)
=
\frac{g(\phi,\psi)^2+g(J\phi,\psi)^2}
{g(\phi,\phi)g(\psi,\psi)}.
\tag{4.2}
\]

The right-hand side lies in \([0,1]\) by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality for \(\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_J\). It is unchanged when either argument is multiplied by any phase number. This is the Born rule in phase-number form.

A useful point is that (4.2) exposes the two real quadratures of a quantum overlap. A real inner product alone would retain only \(g(\phi,\psi)\) and would lose the orthogonal quadrature \(g(J\phi,\psi)\). The phase structure is precisely what combines the quadratures into a rotation-invariant probability.

### 4.2 Interference as a relative phase-number effect

Let two scalar amplitudes be \(a=R_1u_1\) and \(b=R_2u_2\), with \(R_1,R_2\ge0\) and \(u_1,u_2\in\mathsf P_J\). Then

\[
\begin{aligned}
N_J(a+b)
&=(R_1u_1+R_2u_2)^\sharp(R_1u_1+R_2u_2)\\
&=R_1^2+R_2^2+2R_1R_2\operatorname{Re}_J(u_1^\sharp u_2).
\end{aligned}
\tag{4.3}
\]

The quantity \(u_1^\sharp u_2\) is the **relative phase number**. Multiplication of both paths by the same phase number \(w\) leaves it unchanged:

\[
(wu_1)^\sharp(wu_2)=u_1^\sharp u_2.
\tag{4.4}
\]

Interference therefore measures a relative point on the phase circle, not either path’s absolute phase angle. Equation (4.3) is the direct phase-number form of the two-slit cross term.

### 4.3 Observables and spectral projectors

An observable is a densely defined real-linear operator \(A\) satisfying

\[
A^*=A,
\qquad [A,J]=0.
\tag{4.5}
\]

The first condition makes expectation values real; the second makes the observable compatible with phase equivalence. For a normalized state \(\psi\),

\[
\langle A\rangle_\psi=g(\psi,A\psi).
\tag{4.6}
\]

Since \(A\) commutes with \(J\), it is linear over \(\mathbb C_J\). The spectral theorem applies in exactly the usual way after identifying \(\mathbb C_J\) with \(\mathbb C\): a discrete observable has a projector decomposition \(A=\sum_a a\Pi_a\), and the probability of outcome \(a\) is

\[
\mathsf p(a\mid\psi)=g(\psi,\Pi_a\psi)
\tag{4.7}
\]

for normalized \(\psi\). The familiar Hilbert-space measurement calculus is consequently preserved without alteration.

## 5. Derivation of Schrödinger dynamics

### 5.1 Continuous reversible evolution

Let \(U_t\) be the time-evolution family of an isolated system. Postulate IV supplies

\[
U_{t+s}=U_tU_s,
\qquad U_0=I,
\qquad g(U_t\phi,U_t\psi)=g(\phi,\psi),
\qquad U_tJ=JU_t.
\tag{5.1}
\]

For a differentiable finite-dimensional evolution, define \(G=\dot U_t|_{t=0}\). Differentiation of the norm-preservation relation gives

\[
g(G\phi,\psi)+g(\phi,G\psi)=0,
\qquad\text{hence}\qquad G^*=-G.
\tag{5.2}
\]

Differentiation of \([U_t,J]=0\) yields \([G,J]=0\). Now define

\[
H\equiv\hbar JG.
\tag{5.3}
\]

This operator is self-adjoint:

\[
H^*=(\hbar JG)^*=\hbar G^*J^*=\hbar(-G)(-J)=\hbar GJ=\hbar JG=H.
\tag{5.4}
\]

Conversely, \(G=-JH/\hbar\). The state equation is therefore

\[
\boxed{\;J\hbar\frac{d\psi}{dt}=H\psi.\;}
\tag{5.5}
\]

This is the Schrödinger equation in phase-number form. The standard complex notation is recovered only after mapping \(J\) to the conventional imaginary scalar. For strongly continuous infinite-dimensional groups, the same result is the phase-number translation of Stone’s theorem: the generator of a strongly continuous unitary group is self-adjoint after multiplication by the phase generator.[4]

The solution of the time-independent problem is

\[
U_t=\exp\!\left(-\frac{t}{\hbar}JH\right).
\tag{5.6}
\]

For an energy eigenvector \(H\psi_E=E\psi_E\),

\[
\psi_E(t)=\operatorname{Ph}_J\!\left(-\frac{Et}{\hbar}\right)\psi_E(0).
\tag{5.7}
\]

Energy is thus the angular velocity, in units of \(\hbar\), of an amplitude’s phase-number transport.

### 5.2 Conservation laws and the Heisenberg equation

Norm conservation follows directly from (5.5):

\[
\frac{d}{dt}g(\psi,\psi)
=2g\!\left(\psi,-\frac{J}{\hbar}H\psi\right)=0,
\tag{5.8}
\]

because \(JH\) is skew-adjoint. If \(H\) is time independent, then the energy expectation is also conserved:

\[
\frac{d}{dt}g(\psi,H\psi)=0.
\tag{5.9}
\]

For a time-independent observable \(A\), the Heisenberg-picture operator \(A_H(t)=U_t^{-1}AU_t\) obeys

\[
\frac{dA_H}{dt}
=\frac{J}{\hbar}[H,A_H].
\tag{5.10}
\]

No primitive imaginary scalar occurs. The phase generator \(J\) carries the antisymmetric direction required to turn a self-adjoint energy observable into a norm-preserving flow.

### 5.3 Hamiltonian geometry

The Schrödinger flow has a direct real symplectic interpretation. Let

\[
\mathcal E(\psi)=\frac12g(\psi,H\psi).
\tag{5.11}
\]

Its differential is \(d\mathcal E_\psi(\delta\psi)=g(H\psi,\delta\psi)\). The Hamiltonian vector field \(X_{\mathcal E}\) determined by

\[
\Omega(X_{\mathcal E},\delta\psi)=\frac{1}{\hbar}d\mathcal E_\psi(\delta\psi)
\tag{5.12}
\]

is \(X_{\mathcal E}=-JH\psi/\hbar\). Thus the Schrödinger equation is Hamilton’s equation on the real symplectic space \((\mathcal H_\mathbb R,\Omega)\), while the Hilbert norm supplies its compatible metric. This is the intrinsic reason the phase generator appears in quantum time evolution.

## 6. Coordinate quantum mechanics and the quantum Hamilton–Jacobi system

### 6.1 Position and momentum

Let the configuration space be \(\mathbb R^d\), and let wave amplitudes be square-integrable \(\mathbb C_J\)-valued functions. Position acts by multiplication,

\[
(X_j\psi)(x)=x_j\psi(x),
\tag{6.1}
\]

while the momentum operator is

\[
P_j=-J\hbar\partial_j.
\tag{6.2}
\]

A direct calculation on a suitable common domain gives

\[
[X_j,P_k]=J\hbar\delta_{jk}I.
\tag{6.3}
\]

The canonical commutator is therefore a phase-plane statement: position translation is generated by a quarter-turn-scaled derivative. Its standard appearance with a scalar \(i\) is a coordinate encoding of \(J\).

For a scalar potential \(V(x)\), the nonrelativistic Hamiltonian is

\[
H=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2+V(x),
\tag{6.4}
\]

and (5.5) becomes

\[
J\hbar\partial_t\psi
=\left(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2+V\right)\psi.
\tag{6.5}
\]

Plane-wave eigenfunctions have the form

\[
\psi_{p,E}(x,t)=R\,\operatorname{Ph}_J\!\left(\frac{p\cdot x-Et}{\hbar}\right),
\qquad E=\frac{|p|^2}{2m},
\tag{6.6}
\]

where the displayed phase number, rather than the chosen real action coordinate, is the globally meaningful factor.

### 6.2 Uncertainty relation

For self-adjoint \(A\) and \(B\), write \(\Delta A=A-\langle A\rangle_\psi I\), and similarly for \(B\), in a normalized state. Cauchy–Schwarz applied to \(\Delta A\psi\) and \(\Delta B\psi\) gives the Robertson bound

\[
(\Delta A)^2(\Delta B)^2
\ge \frac14 N_J\!\left(\langle\psi,[A,B]\psi\rangle_J\right).
\tag{6.7}
\]

For \(A=X_j\) and \(B=P_j\), equation (6.3) gives

\[
\Delta X_j\,\Delta P_j\ge\frac{\hbar}{2}.
\tag{6.8}
\]

The phase number \(J\) in the commutator has unit phase norm and hence disappears from the numerical lower bound. This explains why an intrinsically phase-geometric commutator produces a real experimental uncertainty scale.

### 6.3 Polar amplitude and phase field

Away from nodes, every scalar wave amplitude has a unique factorization

\[
\psi(x,t)=R(x,t)u(x,t),
\qquad R\ge0,
\qquad u(x,t)\in\mathsf P_J.
\tag{6.9}
\]

On a simply connected nodal-free region, choose a local real action lift \(S\) such that

\[
u(x,t)=\operatorname{Ph}_J\!\left(\frac{S(x,t)}{\hbar}\right).
\tag{6.10}
\]

The phase number, not \(S\), is primary. The identity

\[
u^{-1}\partial_\mu u=\frac{J}{\hbar}\partial_\mu S
\tag{6.11}
\]

is local; its exponentiated content remains globally meaningful even when \(S\) has nontrivial winding.

Substitution of \(\psi=Ru\) into (6.5), followed by separation into components parallel to \(I\) and \(J\), gives two real equations. With \(\rho=R^2\), the phase component yields

\[
\partial_t\rho+
\nabla\!\cdot\!\left(\rho\frac{\nabla S}{m}\right)=0,
\tag{6.12}
\]

while the amplitude component yields

\[
\partial_tS+\frac{|\nabla S|^2}{2m}+V+Q=0,
\qquad
Q=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\nabla^2\sqrt\rho}{\sqrt\rho}.
\tag{6.13}
\]

Equation (6.12) is the continuity equation, and (6.13) is the quantum Hamilton–Jacobi equation. The quantum potential \(Q\) is not an additional postulate; it is the curvature correction produced when the Laplacian acts on the amplitude modulus. The two equations reconstruct (6.5) wherever \(\rho>0\).

At a nodal set \(\rho=0\), the phase number is undefined. This is a physical and mathematical feature, not a notation failure. A loop around a nodal defect can possess nonzero winding,

\[
\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint_C -J\,u^{-1}du\in\mathbb Z,
\tag{6.14}
\]

which records globally stable circulation even though no global action angle exists.

### 6.4 Harmonic oscillator

For one spatial dimension and \(V(x)=m\omega^2x^2/2\), define

\[
a=\sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}}X+
\frac{J}{\sqrt{2m\hbar\omega}}P,
\qquad
a^\sharp=\sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}}X-
\frac{J}{\sqrt{2m\hbar\omega}}P.
\tag{6.15}
\]

Then \([a,a^\sharp]=I\) and

\[
H=\hbar\omega\left(a^\sharp a+\frac12I\right).
\tag{6.16}
\]

The oscillator spectrum follows by the standard ladder argument: \(E_n=\hbar\omega(n+\tfrac12)\). The phase-number notation preserves all operator identities while displaying that the second quadrature is generated by \(J\), not by an otherwise unexplained scalar symbol.

## 7. Local phase symmetry and electromagnetic coupling

### 7.1 Gauge transformations as local phase-number multiplication

Let \(\chi(x,t)\) be a real gauge function and define a local phase transformation

\[
\psi'(x,t)=\operatorname{Ph}_J\!\left(\frac{q\chi(x,t)}{\hbar}\right)\psi(x,t).
\tag{7.1}
\]

For spatial vector potential \(\mathbf A\), set

\[
\mathbf D\equiv\nabla-\frac{Jq}{\hbar}\mathbf A.
\tag{7.2}
\]

If

\[
\mathbf A'=\mathbf A+\nabla\chi,
\tag{7.3}
\]

then direct differentiation proves the covariance identity

\[
\mathbf D'\psi'
=\operatorname{Ph}_J\!\left(\frac{q\chi}{\hbar}\right)\mathbf D\psi.
\tag{7.4}
\]

The gauge-covariant kinetic momentum is

\[
\boldsymbol\Pi=-J\hbar\mathbf D=-J\hbar\nabla-q\mathbf A.
\tag{7.5}
\]

The minimally coupled Schrödinger equation is

\[
J\hbar\partial_t\psi=
\left[\frac{\boldsymbol\Pi^2}{2m}+q\varphi+V\right]\psi.
\tag{7.6}
\]

It is invariant under (7.1) provided \(\varphi'=\varphi-\partial_t\chi\). The complete gauge transformation is therefore a change of local phase frame, expressed entirely as multiplication by phase numbers.

### 7.2 Curvature and Aharonov–Bohm transport

Write the electromagnetic connection one-form as \(A=A_\mu dx^\mu\) and curvature as

\[
F=dA=\frac12F_{\mu\nu}dx^\mu\wedge dx^\nu,
\qquad
F_{\mu\nu}=\partial_\mu A_\nu-\partial_\nu A_\mu.
\tag{7.7}
\]

A charged amplitude transported around a closed spatial loop \(C\) accumulates the gauge phase number

\[
U_{\mathrm{AB}}(C)=
\operatorname{Ph}_J\!\left(\frac{q}{\hbar}\oint_C\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf x\right).
\tag{7.8}
\]

When \(C=\partial\Sigma\) in a gauge chart, Stokes’ theorem gives

\[
U_{\mathrm{AB}}(C)=
\operatorname{Ph}_J\!\left(\frac{q}{\hbar}\int_\Sigma\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf S\right).
\tag{7.9}
\]

The Aharonov–Bohm effect is precisely the observation that this holonomy can alter interference even when the magnetic field vanishes along the two particle paths.[5] The invariant object is the phase number in (7.8); the loop integral itself is an angle representative defined modulo \(2\pi\).

### 7.3 Flux quantization and phase lifting

If a condensate or order parameter is single valued around \(C\), its phase number must return to itself. Thus its local action lift satisfies

\[
\oint_C dS=2\pi n\hbar,
\qquad n\in\mathbb Z.
\tag{7.10}
\]

With gauge coupling, the gauge-invariant circulation is

\[
\oint_C(\nabla S-q\mathbf A)\cdot d\mathbf x.
\tag{7.11}
\]

This exhibits the topological origin of flux quantization in a form that does not mistake the lift \(S\) for the global physical phase field.

## 8. Composite systems, spin, and finite-dimensional quantum information

### 8.1 Tensor composition

A composite system must have one common phase generator, rather than two independently acting copies of \(J\). Given phase Hilbert spaces \((\mathcal H_A,J_A)\) and \((\mathcal H_B,J_B)\), begin with the real tensor product and impose the balancing relation

\[
(J_A\psi_A)\otimes\psi_B
\sim
\psi_A\otimes(J_B\psi_B).
\tag{8.1}
\]

The quotient defines \(\mathcal H_A\otimes_{\mathbb C_J}\mathcal H_B\), and the common phase generator is

\[
J_{AB}(\psi_A\otimes\psi_B)
=(J_A\psi_A)\otimes\psi_B
=\psi_A\otimes(J_B\psi_B).
\tag{8.2}
\]

This is the real-geometric construction of the usual complex tensor product. It prevents an unphysical doubling of phase degrees of freedom.

For a noninteracting pair,

\[
H_{AB}=H_A\otimes I_B+I_A\otimes H_B,
\tag{8.3}
\]

and the joint evolution obeys

\[
J_{AB}\hbar\partial_t\Psi=H_{AB}\Psi.
\tag{8.4}
\]

Entanglement is the failure of \(\Psi\) to factorize in this balanced tensor product. It is therefore retained exactly, not approximated, by the phase-number presentation.

### 8.2 Qubits and relative phase

Choose an orthonormal phase basis \(\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}\). A normalized qubit is

\[
|\psi\rangle=a|0\rangle+b|1\rangle,
\qquad a,b\in\mathbb C_J,
\qquad N_J(a)+N_J(b)=1.
\tag{8.5}
\]

A global phase \(u\) sends \((a,b)\mapsto(ua,ub)\) and leaves the ray unchanged. Only one relative phase number is physically relevant. For the equal-weight state,

\[
|\psi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(|0\rangle+u_\varphi|1\rangle\right),
\tag{8.6}
\]

all operational phase dependence is contained in \(u_\varphi\in\mathsf P_J\).

The usual phase gate is

\[
P(\varphi)=
\begin{pmatrix}
I&0\\
0&u_\varphi
\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{8.7}
\]

The discrete roots \(u_{2\pi k/N}\) form finite phase alphabets underlying the discrete Fourier transform. The distinction between a modular phase number and an unwrapped angle is as important in quantum algorithms as it is in continuum interference.

### 8.3 Spin one-half and rotations

On a two-component phase module, define

\[
\sigma_x=
\begin{pmatrix}0&I\\I&0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\sigma_y=
\begin{pmatrix}0&-J\\J&0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\sigma_z=
\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\0&-I\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{8.8}
\]

These matrices obey

\[
\sigma_i\sigma_j=\delta_{ij}I+J\epsilon_{ijk}\sigma_k,
\qquad
[\sigma_i,\sigma_j]=2J\epsilon_{ijk}\sigma_k.
\tag{8.9}
\]

Thus the spin algebra does not require a primitive imaginary scalar; it requires an intrinsic phase generator. A rotation by \(\vartheta\) about \(\widehat{\mathbf n}\) is

\[
R(\widehat{\mathbf n},\vartheta)
=
\exp\!\left[-\frac{J\vartheta}{2}\widehat{\mathbf n}\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\right]
=
\cos\frac\vartheta2\,I-
J\sin\frac\vartheta2\,\widehat{\mathbf n}\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma.
\tag{8.10}
\]

The relation \(R(\widehat{\mathbf n},2\pi)=-I\) is a central phase number. It acts trivially on rays but nontrivially on spinors, expressing the double cover of spatial rotations by the spin group.

## 9. Path amplitudes and the composition law

### 9.1 Short-time kernel

The propagator is the phase-number-valued kernel of \(U_t\). For a particle in one dimension with Lagrangian \(L=m\dot x^2/2-V(x)\), a time-sliced short-time kernel can be written as

\[
K_\varepsilon(x_{j+1},x_j)
=
\left(\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar\varepsilon}\right)^{1/2}
\operatorname{Ph}_J\!\left(-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)
\operatorname{Ph}_J\!\left[
\frac{\varepsilon}{\hbar}L\!\left(\frac{x_{j+1}+x_j}{2},\frac{x_{j+1}-x_j}{\varepsilon}\right)
\right].
\tag{9.1}
\]

The first phase factor is the Gaussian normalization phase; the second is the action phase number. The conventional kernel is recovered by replacing \(J\) with the usual imaginary scalar.

### 9.2 Functional integral

Iterating the composition law and taking the formal continuum limit gives

\[
K(x_f,t_f;x_i,t_i)
=\mathcal N\int_{x_i}^{x_f}\mathcal D x\;
\operatorname{Ph}_J\!\left(\frac{S[x]}{\hbar}\right),
\qquad
S[x]=\int_{t_i}^{t_f}L(x,\dot x,t)dt.
\tag{9.2}
\]

The essential algebraic fact is multiplicativity:

\[
\operatorname{Ph}_J(S_1/\hbar)
\operatorname{Ph}_J(S_2/\hbar)
=
\operatorname{Ph}_J((S_1+S_2)/\hbar).
\tag{9.3}
\]

Accordingly, the path-integral composition law is a direct expression of phase-number multiplication. In the stationary-phase regime, nearby paths with rapidly varying phase cancel in pairs, while the stationary-action paths contribute coherently. This is the familiar semiclassical mechanism expressed without treating the phase angle as a globally primary object.[6]

### 9.3 Gauge-invariant action phase

For a charged particle, the action includes \(q\int(\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf x-\varphi dt)\). Around a closed loop, the path phase contains the gauge holonomy (7.8). A gauge transformation changes the local action lift by an endpoint term; the full phase number changes only by phase factors assigned to the endpoint frames. Closed-loop interference is therefore gauge invariant by construction.

## 10. Adiabatic transport and Berry holonomy

### 10.1 Eigenstate line bundle

Let \(H(R)\) be a smoothly parameterized Hamiltonian with an isolated normalized eigenstate \(n(R)\):

\[
H(R)n(R)=E_n(R)n(R),
\qquad g(n,n)=1.
\tag{10.1}
\]

The collection of one-dimensional eigenspaces over parameter space forms a phase line bundle. A local rephasing is

\[
n(R)\mapsto n'(R)=u_{\chi(R)}n(R).
\tag{10.2}
\]

Define the real Berry connection one-form

\[
\mathcal A_n=-g(Jn,dn).
\tag{10.3}
\]

Normalization implies \(g(n,dn)=0\), so the phase-valued overlap is

\[
\langle n,dn\rangle_J=J\,g(Jn,dn)=-J\mathcal A_n.
\tag{10.4}
\]

Under (10.2), direct substitution yields

\[
\mathcal A_n' =\mathcal A_n-d\chi.
\tag{10.5}
\]

Thus the connection is local-frame dependent, as it must be; its curvature

\[
\mathcal F_n=d\mathcal A_n
=\frac12F^{(n)}_{\mu\nu}dR^\mu\wedge dR^\nu
\tag{10.6}
\]

is frame invariant.

### 10.2 Adiabatic phase number

Use the adiabatic ansatz

\[
\psi(t)=u_{\alpha_n(t)}
\operatorname{Ph}_J\!\left(-\frac{1}{\hbar}\int_0^tE_n(t')dt'\right)n(R(t)).
\tag{10.7}
\]

Inserting it into (5.5) and projecting along the eigenline gives

\[
\dot\alpha_n(t)=\mathcal A_n(\dot R).
\tag{10.8}
\]

For a closed parameter curve \(C\), the geometric phase number is therefore

\[
U_{\mathrm{geom}}(C)=
\operatorname{Ph}_J\!\left(\oint_C\mathcal A_n\right).
\tag{10.9}
\]

When the loop bounds a surface \(\Sigma\) in one gauge chart,

\[
U_{\mathrm{geom}}(C)=
\operatorname{Ph}_J\!\left(\int_\Sigma\mathcal F_n\right).
\tag{10.10}
\]

Berry derived the additional adiabatic phase factor and Simon identified it as Hermitian-line-bundle holonomy.[7] [8] Equations (10.3)–(10.10) make the same result intrinsic: the observable is a phase number, whereas the Berry angle is a local representative.

### 10.3 Spin-\(\tfrac12\) solid-angle law

For

\[
H=-\frac{\hbar\omega}{2}\widehat{\mathbf n}\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma,
\qquad
\widehat{\mathbf n}=(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\sin\theta\sin\phi,\cos\theta),
\tag{10.11}
\]

choose the positive eigenvector on the north chart,

\[
n_+(\theta,\phi)=
\begin{pmatrix}
\cos(\theta/2)\\
u_\phi\sin(\theta/2)
\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{10.12}
\]

Equation (10.3) yields

\[
\mathcal A_+=-
\sin^2\!\left(\frac\theta2\right)d\phi
=-\frac{1-\cos\theta}{2}d\phi,
\tag{10.13}
\]

and

\[
\mathcal F_+=-\frac12\sin\theta\,d\theta\wedge d\phi.
\tag{10.14}
\]

For a loop enclosing oriented solid angle \(\Omega(C)\),

\[
U_{\mathrm{geom}}(C)=
\operatorname{Ph}_J\!\left(-\frac{\Omega(C)}{2}\right).
\tag{10.15}
\]

The result is globally well defined even though the local eigenvector and the connection (10.13) require gauge patches. This is the model example of why phase numbers, rather than unqualified phase angles, are the correct global observables.

## 11. Equivalence theorem, scope, and analytical consequences

### 11.1 Exact equivalence with conventional complex quantum mechanics

**Theorem 11.1 (equivalence).** Let \((\mathcal H_\mathbb R,g,J)\) be a real Hilbert space with orthogonal complex structure. Define scalar multiplication by \(a+ib\) through

\[
(a+ib)\cdot\psi\equiv(aI+bJ)\psi,
\tag{11.1}
\]

and define the complex inner product by identifying \(\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_J\) with the usual complex scalar algebra. Then \(\mathcal H_\mathbb R\) becomes a complex Hilbert space \(\mathcal H\). Conversely, every complex Hilbert space determines \((\mathcal H_\mathbb R,g,J)\) by restriction of scalars, with \(J\) equal to multiplication by the standard phase generator. The constructions are inverse up to canonical unitary isomorphism.

**Proof.** Equations (2.3)–(2.6) show that \(\mathbb C_J\) is a field isomorphic to \(\mathbb C\). Equations (3.2)–(3.5) supply a positive-definite \(\mathbb C_J\)-Hilbert inner product. The completeness of the real norm is unchanged by the scalar identification. Conversely, the real part of a complex Hilbert inner product produces \(g\), and multiplication by the conventional phase generator produces an orthogonal \(J\) satisfying (2.1). The two maps preserve scalar multiplication, inner products, observables, projectors, and unitary evolution. \(\square\)

This theorem settles the formal status of the construction. It is not a competing probabilistic theory and does not predict deviations from standard nonrelativistic quantum mechanics. It is an intrinsic real-geometric presentation in which the phase group and its generator are explicit. The selection of a complex structure is physically substantive; suppressing its notation does not remove it. Analyses of real-Hilbert-space formulations likewise emphasize that an appropriate complex structure recovers the standard quantum theory.[1]

### 11.2 What the phase-number formulation clarifies

The principal analytical gain is a clean hierarchy of objects. The phase number is global; an angle is a local lift. The phase generator \(J\) is intrinsic; a coordinate symbol for it is conventional. Quantum states are rays; amplitudes are local representatives. Gauge potentials are local connection data; holonomies are global observable phase numbers. The table summarizes the translation.

| Standard expression | Phase-number expression | Invariant content |
| --- | --- | --- |
| \(i\) | \(J\) | Orthogonal quarter-turn |
| \(a+ib\) | \(aI+bJ\) | Two-dimensional phase scalar |
| \(e^{i\theta}\) | \(\operatorname{Ph}_J(\theta)\) | Unit phase number |
| \(i\hbar\partial_t\psi=H\psi\) | \(J\hbar\partial_t\psi=H\psi\) | Hamiltonian phase rotation |
| \(-i\hbar\nabla\) | \(-J\hbar\nabla\) | Translation generator |
| \(e^{iS/\hbar}\) | \(\operatorname{Ph}_J(S/\hbar)\) | Action phase number |
| \(e^{i\oint\mathcal A}\) | \(\operatorname{Ph}_J(\oint\mathcal A)\) | Gauge/Berry holonomy |
| \(\psi\sim e^{i\chi}\psi\) | \(\psi\sim u\psi\) | Projective pure state |

Several common ambiguities become impossible or visibly incomplete in this language. One cannot say that a global phase angle exists on a winding configuration, because the global object is stated first as a phase-number field. One cannot confuse a local gauge potential with an observable phase, because only exponentiated holonomy is frame independent. One cannot accidentally average phases across a branch cut, because addition is performed in the two-dimensional phase algebra before a coordinate angle is selected.

### 11.3 Boundary of the construction

The present paper treats nonrelativistic quantum mechanics and its abelian \(U(1)\) phase structure. Quantum field theory, relativistic covariance, open-system dynamics, and non-abelian Berry transport require additional machinery. Degenerate eigenspaces, for example, lead to a \(U(r)\)-valued connection and path-ordered holonomy. The scalar phase group remains the rank-one case, where all local phase factors commute and topological information is already nontrivial.

The framework also does not eliminate the need for a complex structure. A real Hilbert space lacking a selected \(J\) does not carry the full phase interference geometry encoded in (4.2), nor does it supply the phase-covariant generator in (5.5). The precise conclusion is stronger and cleaner: quantum mechanics can be written without a primitive imaginary **symbol**, but not without the oriented phase-plane structure that the symbol conventionally represents.

## 12. Conclusion

A complete phase-number reconstruction of nonrelativistic quantum mechanics has been given. The starting datum is a real Hilbert space with an orthogonal quarter-turn \(J\). From \(J\) one obtains the scalar algebra \(\mathbb C_J\), the unit phase group \(\mathsf P_J\), phase-valued inner products, ray space, Born probabilities, and self-adjoint observables. Continuous reversible phase-covariant evolution necessarily has the Schrödinger form \(J\hbar\partial_t\psi=H\psi\), with self-adjoint \(H\). Position, momentum, uncertainty, polar wave mechanics, gauge coupling, path amplitudes, tensor products, spin, and Berry holonomy then follow in a single phase-first language.

The conceptual consequence is not that quantum mechanics is secretly real in a way that discards complex structure. It is that the complex structure is more transparently understood as a distinguished physical-geometric operation. Its unit exponentials are phase numbers. The conventional scalar notation is a useful coordinate compression, but the compact group-valued phase factor is the global object that composes under interference, glues local gauge frames, measures circulation, and records holonomy.

> **Phase-first principle.** In quantum mechanics, the fundamental object is the unit phase factor generated by \(J\). A real phase angle is a local coordinate for that factor; a primitive imaginary symbol is a notation for its infinitesimal generator.

## Appendix A. Derivation of the phase-valued inner-product identities

For completeness, let \(a=a_0I+a_1J\) and \(b=b_0I+b_1J\). Orthogonality of \(J\) gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
\langle a\phi,b\psi\rangle_J
&=g(a\phi,b\psi)I+g(Ja\phi,b\psi)J\\
&=a^\sharp\big[g(\phi,\psi)I+g(J\phi,\psi)J\big]b\\
&=a^\sharp\langle\phi,\psi\rangle_Jb.
\end{aligned}
\tag{A.1}
\]

For conjugate symmetry, use symmetry of \(g\) and skew-adjointness of \(J\):

\[
\begin{aligned}
\langle\phi,\psi\rangle_J^\sharp
&=g(\phi,\psi)I-g(J\phi,\psi)J\\
&=g(\psi,\phi)I+g(J\psi,\phi)J\\
&=\langle\psi,\phi\rangle_J.
\end{aligned}
\tag{A.2}
\]

Finally, \(g(J\psi,\psi)=0\) because \(g(J\psi,\psi)=-g(\psi,J\psi)\). Hence \(\langle\psi,\psi\rangle_J=g(\psi,\psi)I\), establishing positivity.

## Appendix B. Polar decomposition of the Schrödinger equation

Set \(\psi=Ru\), \(u=\operatorname{Ph}_J(S/\hbar)\), and write \(\partial_\mu u=(J/\hbar)(\partial_\mu S)u\). Then

\[
\partial_t\psi
=u\left(\partial_tR+\frac{J}{\hbar}R\partial_tS\right),
\tag{B.1}
\]

and

\[
\nabla^2\psi=u\left[
\nabla^2R-\frac{R}{\hbar^2}|\nabla S|^2
+\frac{J}{\hbar}\left(2\nabla R\cdot\nabla S+R\nabla^2S\right)
\right].
\tag{B.2}
\]

Substitution into (6.5) gives, after cancellation of the common phase number \(u\),

\[
-R\partial_tS+J\hbar\partial_tR
=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2R
+\frac{R}{2m}|\nabla S|^2+VR
-\frac{J\hbar}{2m}\left(2\nabla R\cdot\nabla S+R\nabla^2S\right).
\tag{B.3}
\]

Equating the \(I\) and \(J\) components yields (6.12) and (6.13).

## Appendix C. Gauge covariance calculation

Let \(u_\chi=\operatorname{Ph}_J(q\chi/\hbar)\). Then

\[
\nabla(u_\chi\psi)
=u_\chi\left(\nabla\psi+\frac{Jq}{\hbar}(\nabla\chi)\psi\right).
\tag{C.1}
\]

Therefore, with \(\mathbf A'=\mathbf A+\nabla\chi\),

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf D'\psi'
&=\left(\nabla-\frac{Jq}{\hbar}\mathbf A'\right)(u_\chi\psi)\\
&=u_\chi\left[\nabla\psi+\frac{Jq}{\hbar}(\nabla\chi)\psi
-\frac{Jq}{\hbar}(\mathbf A+\nabla\chi)\psi\right]\\
&=u_\chi\mathbf D\psi.
\end{aligned}
\tag{C.2}
\]

This proves (7.4).

## Appendix D. Sign convention for the Berry connection

The local Berry connection in this manuscript is \(\mathcal A_n=-g(Jn,dn)\). Under the identification \(J\leftrightarrow i\), equation (10.4) gives \(i\langle n|dn\rangle\leftrightarrow\mathcal A_n\). The choice is consistent with the holonomy \(\operatorname{Ph}_J(\oint\mathcal A_n)\). Authors using \(-i\langle n|dn\rangle\) instead use the opposite orientation convention for the connection; the exponentiated holonomy is unchanged when all signs are used consistently.
