Abstract

The equivalence principle is ordinarily formulated within general relativity as a statement concerning the universal response of sufficiently small freely falling bodies to gravity. In Universal Phase Theory (UPT), however, geometry itself is not primitive. The metric is instead hypothesized to emerge from the organization, stability, susceptibility, and transport structure of an underlying universal phase field.

This changes the foundational question.

Rather than postulating that all matter universally couples to a pre-existing gravitational metric, UPT asks whether sufficiently localized phase excitations are forced to propagate according to one and the same emergent response geometry,

[
g^\Phi_{\mu\nu},
]

independently of their internal phase-sector structure.

The central proposed implication is

[
\boxed{
\text{universal phase propagation}
;\Longrightarrow;
\text{phase equivalence principle}
}
]

where the phase equivalence principle is the conditional statement that all sufficiently localized stable excitations, in the geometric-optics or point-particle limit, follow geodesics of the same emergent metric:

[
\boxed{
\frac{D^2x^\mu}{d\tau^2}=0.
}
]

The crucial discriminator is sector universality. If two phase sectors (a) and (b) reconstruct distinct effective metrics,

[
g_{\mu\nu}^{(a)}\neq g_{\mu\nu}^{(b)},
]

then UPT predicts a violation of universal free propagation. Such a violation would be experimentally accessible through differential trajectories, differential redshift, differential lensing, species-dependent propagation cones, or composition-dependent free fall.

This paper develops the mathematical structure of the proposed Phase Equivalence Principle. It distinguishes metric emergence from metric universality, geodesic motion from gravitational dynamics, local inertial behavior from global curvature, and universal propagation from merely similar dispersion relations. It derives necessary and sufficient conditions for a common emergent metric, identifies the principal-symbol criterion for universality, formulates phase-sector violation tensors, develops weak-field and tidal limits, and establishes a hierarchy of experimental tests.

The result is not a claim that UPT has already derived the equivalence principle. Rather, the equivalence principle becomes a sharply defined structural gate:

[
\boxed{
\mathcal E_\Phi:
\quad
g_{\mu\nu}^{(a)}

g_{\mu\nu}^{(b)}

g^\Phi_{\mu\nu}
\quad
\forall a,b
\text{ in the universal low-energy sector}.
}
]

If this condition follows from the microscopic phase theory, equivalence becomes a derived property of phase organization. If it does not, UPT predicts or permits equivalence-principle violation.
