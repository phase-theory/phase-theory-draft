Abstract

Modern physics commonly identifies light with electromagnetic radiation: propagating excitations of the electromagnetic field whose quantum is the photon. The electromagnetic spectrum, however, is only one known spectrum of propagating physical phenomena. Gravitational waves provide an experimentally established non-electromagnetic radiation channel, while theoretical physics permits additional fields, hidden gauge sectors, dark photons, scalar fields, massive vector fields, axion-like excitations, and other weakly coupled degrees of freedom.

This paper develops a general framework called Beyond the Electromagnetic Spectrum (BES). The central proposition is that the electromagnetic spectrum should not be regarded as synonymous with the total spectrum of propagating physical excitations. Instead, it should be regarded as one experimentally accessible sector of a more general radiative state space.

We define generalized radiation as an information-bearing propagating excitation of a physical field or geometric degree of freedom. Under this definition, ordinary photons occupy the electromagnetic sector, gravitational waves occupy a tensor-geometric sector, and hypothetical dark photons or other weakly coupled fields occupy additional sectors.

The framework introduces a generalized spectral object

[
\mathfrak S

\bigcup_a \mathfrak S_a,
]

where each (\mathfrak S_a) corresponds to a distinct physical field sector. Each sector possesses its own dispersion relation, polarization structure, coupling structure, conservation laws, propagation characteristics, and measurement channels.

A crucial distinction is made between frequency space and physical-sector space. A phenomenon can possess a perfectly ordinary frequency while remaining invisible to electromagnetic detectors because it belongs to a different field sector. Consequently, extending the electromagnetic spectrum to higher or lower frequencies does not constitute a theory beyond electromagnetism.

The paper develops a mathematical language for generalized spectra, defines observability and darkness in terms of coupling operators, establishes a taxonomy of non-electromagnetic radiation, and proposes experimental strategies for discovering previously inaccessible sectors. Particular attention is given to hidden photons, scalar radiation, gravitational radiation, phase excitations, topological modes, and cross-sector conversion.

The resulting framework does not assert the existence of presently unconfirmed forms of “dark light.” Instead, it establishes a falsifiable theoretical architecture within which such phenomena can be precisely defined, searched for, constrained, or rejected.

The central hypothesis is:

[
\boxed{
\text{The electromagnetic spectrum may be a sector of the radiative spectrum, not the radiative spectrum itself.}
}
]

PART I — THE SPECTRAL QUESTION

1. Introduction

Humanity historically encountered light through vision.

The visible spectrum therefore acquired an epistemological privilege: it became the intuitive meaning of “light.”

Physics subsequently demonstrated that visible light is only a narrow interval within electromagnetic radiation. Radio waves, microwaves, infrared radiation, ultraviolet radiation, X-rays, and gamma rays are all electromagnetic phenomena despite being inaccessible to ordinary human vision.

This expansion produced a profound lesson:

[
\boxed{
\text{Invisible does not mean nonexistent.}
}
]

Instrumentation repeatedly extended the accessible electromagnetic domain.

But a second conceptual limitation remains.

The electromagnetic spectrum is still fundamentally a spectrum of one field.

The electromagnetic field is described by

[
A_\mu,
]

with field strength

[
F_{\mu\nu}

\partial_\mu A_\nu-\partial_\nu A_\mu.
]

Its propagating quantum is the photon.

The natural question is therefore not:

How much farther can we extend the electromagnetic spectrum?

It is:

Are there propagating physical excitations that are not electromagnetic excitations at all?

The answer is already known to be yes in at least one experimentally established sense.

Gravitational waves are not electromagnetic waves. LIGO explicitly distinguishes gravitational radiation from electromagnetic radiation and detects it through spacetime strain rather than electromagnetic absorption. (LIGO Lab | Caltech)

Thus physics has already crossed the conceptual boundary beyond the electromagnetic spectrum.

The next question is whether additional sectors exist.
