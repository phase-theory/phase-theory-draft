# The Variational Genesis of Gravity

## Deriving Effective Geometry and Einstein-Like Dynamics from More Primitive Structures

Research white paper · Mathematical foundations · Effective-field derivation · Classical recovery · Falsifiable corrections

## Abstract

The Variational Genesis of Gravity (VGG) investigates whether the metric field and its gravitational dynamics can emerge as effective structures selected by a more primitive variational problem. Rather than postulating the Einstein–Hilbert action as fundamental, the framework begins with microscopic or pregeometric degrees of freedom, defines a restricted space of admissible configurations, and asks whether a coarse-grained or constrained description induces an effective metric, an effective gravitational action, and field equations that reproduce general relativity in a controlled regime.

The central mathematical problem is one of variational reduction. Given primitive variables \\(q\\) and an admissibility functional \\(\mathcal C[q]\\), one seeks a map from admissible configurations to an effective geometric field \\(g\_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{eff}}\\), together with a reduced functional \\(\Gamma[g,\psi]\\) whose stationary points determine the macroscopic dynamics. The Einstein equations must then arise from the structure of this reduction rather than from the insertion of the Einstein–Hilbert term at the microscopic level.

This paper develops a general variational architecture, distinguishes constrained minimization from dynamical emergence, and derives the conditions under which the low-energy effective action takes the form

\\[ \Gamma[g,\psi] = \int d^4x\\,\sqrt{-g} \left[ \frac{M\_{\mathrm{eff}}^2}{2}R -\Lambda\_{\mathrm{eff}} +\mathcal L\_{\mathrm{matter}} +\mathcal L\_{\mathrm{corr}} \right]. \\]

The appearance of the Einstein–Hilbert term is treated as a result to be established, not as a premise. A concrete conditional construction uses a primitive symmetric tensor density whose nondegenerate phase defines an effective metric, with the Einstein–Hilbert term generated by a curvature-sensitive coarse-grained functional. This construction demonstrates how the desired variational reduction can be formulated, while also exposing the crucial unresolved requirement: the microscopic dynamics must independently generate the curvature term with the correct sign and coefficient.

The paper then derives the low-energy field equations, identifies the conditions for recovering general relativity, and develops a testable correction through a curvature-squared effective operator. In four dimensions, a generic \\(R^2\\) correction introduces an additional scalar gravitational degree of freedom unless its coefficient is treated perturbatively or the theory is otherwise constrained. The resulting framework therefore includes explicit stability, effective-field-theory, conservation, and observational consistency requirements.

The principal conclusion is deliberately limited. A variational route from primitive structure to Einstein-like dynamics is mathematically definable, and its effective predictions can be tested. But recovery of general relativity does not establish a successful quantum-gravity theory. Such a theory must additionally demonstrate a consistent microscopic dynamics, controlled ultraviolet behavior, the correct quantum degrees of freedom, compatibility with established observations, and a nontrivial prediction beyond classical general relativity.

Keywords: emergent gravity; variational principles; pregeometric dynamics; effective metric; Einstein–Hilbert action; constrained variation; coarse graining; curvature-squared gravity; effective field theory; quantum gravity.

## 1. The foundational problem

### 1.1 The conventional gravitational action

In general relativity, the metric \\(g\_{\mu\nu}\\) is a dynamical field. In units \\(c=\hbar=1\\), the conventional action is

\\[ S[g,\psi] = \int\_{\mathcal M}d^4x\\,\sqrt{-g} \left[ \frac{R}{16\pi G} +\mathcal L\_{\mathrm{matter}}(g,\psi) \right], \\]

where \\(g=\det(g\_{\mu\nu})\\), \\(R\\) is the Ricci scalar, \\(G\\) is Newton's gravitational constant, and \\(\psi\\) collectively denotes matter fields.

Writing

\\[ M\_{\mathrm{Pl}}^2=\frac{1}{8\pi G}, \\]

the gravitational term becomes

\\[ S\_{\mathrm{EH}} = \frac{M\_{\mathrm{Pl}}^2}{2} \int\_{\mathcal M}d^4x\\,\sqrt{-g}\\,R. \\]

Varying the total action with respect to \\(g^{\mu\nu}\\), with suitable boundary conditions or the appropriate gravitational boundary term, yields

\\[ G\_{\mu\nu}+\Lambda g\_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi G\\,T\_{\mu\nu}, \\]

if a cosmological constant is included.

This variational derivation is established. It does not, however, explain why the metric is the correct macroscopic variable, why its action contains the Einstein–Hilbert term, or why the coefficient is the observed gravitational coupling.

### 1.2 The inverse foundational question

VGG reverses the usual order of construction.

Instead of starting with a metric and assuming its gravitational action, begin with a more primitive configuration space \\(\mathcal Q\\) and ask whether an effective metric can be reconstructed from admissible microscopic states.

The proposed chain is

\\[ \boxed{ \text{Primitive degrees of freedom} \longrightarrow \text{admissibility} \longrightarrow \text{effective geometry} \longrightarrow \text{reduced action} \longrightarrow \text{gravitational dynamics}. } \\]

Each arrow represents a separate mathematical obligation.

The primitive variables need not initially possess spacetime indices. They might be relational variables, discrete adjacency data, tensor fields on an auxiliary domain, collective order parameters, or quantum degrees of freedom. Their physical interpretation depends on the model.

The emergence of a metric requires more than assigning four indices to a tensor. One must establish nondegeneracy, signature, coordinate or relational transformation properties, and a meaningful notion of causal propagation.

Likewise, the emergence of the Einstein–Hilbert action requires more than observing that the scalar curvature is an available invariant. The coefficient and sign of the curvature term must follow from the primitive dynamics or a controlled matching procedure.

### 1.3 The central hypothesis

The central VGG hypothesis is:

> A restricted variational problem over primitive degrees of freedom may possess a macroscopic regime in which the admissible collective configurations are describable by an effective metric and their reduced dynamics are governed, to leading derivative order, by the Einstein–Hilbert action.

This is a research hypothesis, not an established result about nature.

Its strongest form would demonstrate all of the following:

1. The metric is constructed from primitive variables rather than introduced as a fundamental field.
2. The primitive variational problem determines the reduced gravitational functional.
3. The leading reduced equations reproduce general relativity.
4. Corrections outside the recovery regime are derived and parameter-controlled.
5. The resulting microscopic theory is consistent with quantum mechanics and known gravitational observations.

The first four objectives can be investigated within classical or effective models. The fifth is the substantially harder quantum-gravity problem.

## 2. Formal definition of the variational problem

### 2.1 Primitive configuration space

Let \\(\mathcal Q\\) be a space of primitive configurations \\(q\\). The symbol \\(q\\) may represent a finite collection of fields, a discrete structure, a relational network, or a microscopic state. The definition of \\(\mathcal Q\\) is part of the theory and cannot be left entirely unspecified in a completed model.

Let \\(\lambda\\) denote a set of microscopic couplings and boundary data. The primitive dynamics are specified by a functional

\\[ S\_{\mathrm{micro}}[q;\lambda]. \\]

A general VGG construction introduces an admissibility condition

\\[ \mathcal C_A[q]=0, \qquad A\in\mathcal A, \\]

where \\(\mathcal A\\) indexes the constraints. These may encode consistency, symmetry, nondegeneracy, conservation, or restrictions on allowed configurations.

The primitive stationary configurations satisfy

\\[ \delta S\_{\mathrm{micro}}[q;\lambda] + \sum_A\int d\mu\\, \Lambda^A\\,\delta\mathcal C_A[q] =0, \\]

together with

\\[ \mathcal C_A[q]=0. \\]

Here \\(\Lambda^A\\) are Lagrange multipliers and \\(d\mu\\) is the relevant measure on the primitive domain. If the admissibility conditions are inequalities, gauge constraints, or nonlocal restrictions, the corresponding variational formulation must be adapted.

This formalism does not imply that the physical universe is selected by global minimization. Stationary points may be saddles, local minima, metastable states, or dynamically selected solutions. The actual selection mechanism must be specified.

### 2.2 Three distinct meanings of emergence

The word emergence can refer to three different mathematical procedures.

Constrained reduction. The primitive configuration is restricted to a submanifold or constraint surface. The effective variables parameterize this restricted space.

Coarse graining. Microscopic variables are integrated out or averaged over, producing an effective functional for collective variables.

Dynamical attraction. Evolution drives a broad class of initial configurations toward a macroscopic sector that admits an effective geometric description.

These procedures are not equivalent. A constrained reduction can produce a useful metric without explaining why the universe occupies the relevant constraint surface. Coarse graining can generate new effective interactions but requires a specified measure and microscopic dynamics. Dynamical attraction requires an evolution law and a stability analysis.

A complete VGG theory must identify which mechanism is responsible for each claimed result.

### 2.3 The effective metric map

Suppose a map

\\[ \mathfrak G:\mathcal Q\_{\mathrm{adm}}\rightarrow \operatorname{Met}(\mathcal M) \\]

assigns to an admissible primitive configuration an effective metric,

\\[ q\longmapsto g\_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{eff}}[q]. \\]

Here \\(\operatorname{Met}(\mathcal M)\\) denotes the appropriate class of nondegenerate metric fields on an emergent manifold \\(\mathcal M\\).

For the construction to be meaningful, the map must specify:

- how the effective manifold or continuum limit is identified;
- how coordinate transformations arise or act;
- how the metric signature is fixed;
- under what conditions \\(g\_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{eff}}\\) is nondegenerate;
- how causal cones and proper distances are recovered;
- whether multiple primitive configurations map to the same effective geometry.

The final issue is important. The map may be many-to-one. Different microscopic states could yield the same metric but differ in higher-order effective couplings or quantum correlations. In that case, the metric is not a complete description of the primitive state.

### 2.4 Induced dynamics on the metric sector

Let \\(\mathcal Q_g\subseteq\mathcal Q\_{\mathrm{adm}}\\) denote a sector whose configurations admit an effective metric description. The reduced action can be defined schematically by

\\[ \Gamma[g,\psi] = \operatorname\*{Ext}\_{q:\\,\mathfrak G[q]=g} \left\\{ S\_{\mathrm{micro}}[q;\lambda] + S\_{\mathrm{source}}[q,\psi] \right\\}, \\]

where \\(\operatorname\*{Ext}\\) denotes an appropriate stationary reduction over microscopic configurations compatible with \\(g\\). If the microscopic variables are quantum fields, the corresponding effective action is more generally obtained by a functional integral or Legendre transform, not merely by classical extremization.

The effective gravitational equations are then

\\[ \frac{\delta\Gamma[g,\psi]}{\delta g^{\mu\nu}}=0, \\]

along with the matter equations

\\[ \frac{\delta\Gamma[g,\psi]}{\delta\psi}=0. \\]

The foundational challenge is now sharply stated:

\\[ \boxed{ \text{Derive }\Gamma[g,\psi]\text{ from }S\_{\mathrm{micro}}, \text{ rather than stipulating its curvature terms.} } \\]

The remainder of the paper develops the consequences and requirements of this program.

## 3. Constructing an effective metric from primitive variables

A useful theory must eventually give an explicit construction. This section presents a minimal illustrative architecture and then identifies what remains unproved.

### 3.1 A primitive symmetric tensor density

Consider a primitive symmetric tensor density \\(Q^{\mu\nu}\\) of weight \\(+1\\). Assume that the primitive configuration space contains a sector in which \\(Q^{\mu\nu}\\) is nondegenerate and has Lorentzian signature.

In four dimensions, define

\\[ Q^{\mu\nu}=\sqrt{-g}\\,g^{\mu\nu}. \\]

This relation can be inverted in the nondegenerate sector. Taking determinants,

\\[ \det Q^{\mu\nu} = \det(\sqrt{-g}\\,g^{\mu\nu}) = (-g)^2\det(g^{\mu\nu}) = -g. \\]

Thus,

\\[ g=-\det Q, \\]

and

\\[ \boxed{ g^{\mu\nu} = \frac{Q^{\mu\nu}}{\sqrt{-\det Q}}. } \\]

This equation provides a metric reconstruction from a primitive tensor density, provided the determinant and signature conditions are satisfied.

It does not yet derive gravity. The tensor density has been chosen to contain exactly the information needed to reconstruct a metric. The unresolved question is why this variable and its Lorentzian nondegenerate sector arise from the primitive theory.

This is an example of an explicit effective-variable map, not a derivation of spacetime from a fully pregeometric substrate.

### 3.2 The admissible sector

The admissible set may be defined by

\\[ \mathcal Q\_{\mathrm{adm}} = \left\\{ Q^{\mu\nu}: \det Q\neq0,\\; \operatorname{signature}(g)=(-,+,+,+) \right\\}. \\]

If the primitive dynamics allow \\(\det Q=0\\), the metric reconstruction becomes singular at the boundary of this sector. A complete theory must specify whether such configurations are dynamically excluded, approached in a controlled limit, or physically meaningful as nonmetric phases.

One must not assume that the variational dynamics automatically preserve nondegeneracy. This is a dynamical property to be established by solving or bounding the equations of motion.

### 3.3 Why the metric map is insufficient

The reconstruction equation determines \\(g\_{\mu\nu}\\) algebraically from \\(Q^{\mu\nu}\\). It does not determine:

- the Einstein–Hilbert coefficient;
- the cosmological constant;
- the matter coupling;
- the connection or its relation to the metric;
- the number of propagating gravitational degrees of freedom;
- the quantum measure over primitive configurations.

A metric can emerge kinematically while its dynamics remain non-Einsteinian. VGG must therefore treat metric emergence and gravitational-action emergence as separate theorems or derivations.

## 4. The variational genesis of the curvature action

### 4.1 The derivative expansion

Assume that the reduced dynamics admit a local derivative expansion at energies well below a microscopic scale \\(M\\). Diffeomorphism invariance of the effective description permits a sequence of scalar densities constructed from the metric, curvature, matter fields, and covariant derivatives.

A generic four-dimensional effective action has the schematic form

\\[ \begin{aligned} \Gamma[g,\psi] =\int d^4x\sqrt{-g}\bigg[ &-\Lambda\_{\mathrm{eff}} +\frac{M\_{\mathrm{eff}}^2}{2}R\\\ &+\alpha R^2 +\beta R\_{\mu\nu}R^{\mu\nu} +\gamma R\_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}R^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\\\ &+\mathcal L\_{\mathrm{matter}} +\mathcal L\_{\mathrm{higher}} \bigg]. \end{aligned} \\]

The coefficients are effective couplings determined by the primitive model, matching conditions, and renormalization prescription.

The existence of this expansion is conditional on locality at the scales under consideration, the relevant symmetries, and the availability of a suitable effective description. Nonlocal terms can arise when massless degrees of freedom are integrated out or when the microscopic theory does not admit a local low-energy expansion of the assumed form.

The critical distinction is:

\\[ \text{Symmetry allows }R \quad\not\Rightarrow\quad \text{dynamics generate }R. \\]

Diffeomorphism invariance constrains the form of a local effective action, but it does not by itself prove that the Einstein–Hilbert term appears with a positive coefficient, that the coefficient is finite, or that the metric sector is dynamically selected.

### 4.2 The coefficient of curvature

The leading curvature coefficient is written as

\\[ \frac{M\_{\mathrm{eff}}^2}{2} = \frac{1}{16\pi G\_{\mathrm{eff}}}. \\]

The desired recovery of general relativity requires

\\[ M\_{\mathrm{eff}}^2>0 \\]

in the standard sign convention for a healthy massless spin-2 sector.

In VGG, \\(M\_{\mathrm{eff}}\\) should be determined by the primitive dynamics or by matching to an independently specified microscopic model. Setting

\\[ M\_{\mathrm{eff}}^2=M\_{\mathrm{Pl}}^2 \\]

by hand reproduces the observed normalization but does not explain it.

A successful microscopic derivation would calculate the coefficient in terms of primitive couplings and demonstrate that the observed gravitational scale follows within the model's parameter domain.

### 4.3 Curvature as a generated term

A controlled route to curvature generation could proceed through a coarse-grained functional,

\\[ \Gamma\_\mu[g,\psi] = \mathcal R\_\mu \left[S\_{\mathrm{micro}},\mathcal D\right], \\]

where \\(\mathcal R\_\mu\\) denotes a specified reduction or renormalization procedure at scale \\(\mu\\), and \\(\mathcal D\\) denotes the microscopic measure, constraints, and field content.

The desired result is

\\[ \Gamma\_\mu = \int d^4x\sqrt{-g} \left[ -\Lambda\_\mu+\frac{M\_\mu^2}{2}R+\cdots \right]. \\]

To qualify as a derivation, this calculation must exhibit the mechanism producing the \\(R\\) term. Possible mechanisms in broader research include quantum effective actions, induced-gravity constructions, collective modes, and continuum limits of discrete structures. These approaches are not interchangeable and do not share a universal proof of Einstein gravity.

The present framework does not claim to have solved the microscopic calculation. It identifies the calculation that a complete VGG model must perform.

### 4.4 Boundary terms and variational well-posedness

For a spacetime region with a boundary, the Einstein–Hilbert action alone generally does not yield a well-posed metric variation under fixed induced boundary metric. A suitable boundary term, conventionally the Gibbons–Hawking–York term, must be included, together with any necessary corner contributions.

Thus, an emergent Einstein–Hilbert sector requires more than generating a bulk scalar \\(R\\). The effective reduction must also account for boundary data and the variational principle appropriate to the class of configurations under consideration.

If the primitive theory has no fundamental spacetime boundary in the same sense, the boundary structure of the effective theory must itself be explained.

## 5. The effective field equations

### 5.1 Leading-order recovery of Einstein gravity

Suppose the primitive variational problem has been reduced to an effective action of the form

\\[ \Gamma[g,\psi] = \int d^4x\sqrt{-g} \left[ \frac{M\_{\mathrm{eff}}^2}{2}R -\Lambda\_{\mathrm{eff}} +\mathcal L\_{\mathrm{matter}} \right]. \\]

Define the stress-energy tensor by

\\[ T\_{\mu\nu} = -\frac{2}{\sqrt{-g}} \frac{\delta S\_{\mathrm{matter}}} {\delta g^{\mu\nu}}. \\]

Variation with respect to \\(g^{\mu\nu}\\), with the appropriate boundary treatment, gives

\\[ M\_{\mathrm{eff}}^2G\_{\mu\nu} +\Lambda\_{\mathrm{eff}}g\_{\mu\nu} = T\_{\mu\nu}. \\]

Equivalently,

\\[ \boxed{ G\_{\mu\nu}+\Lambda\_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(G)}g\_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi G\_{\mathrm{eff}}T\_{\mu\nu}, } \\]

where

\\[ 8\pi G\_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{1}{M\_{\mathrm{eff}}^2}, \qquad \Lambda\_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(G)} =\frac{\Lambda\_{\mathrm{eff}}}{M\_{\mathrm{eff}}^2}. \\]

This is the Einstein equation with an effective gravitational coupling and cosmological constant.

The result is exact for the assumed effective action. In VGG, however, it is only a conditional recovery theorem: the difficult step is establishing that the primitive theory produces this action with the required coefficients and no unsuppressed additional terms.

### 5.2 Conservation as a consistency condition

Diffeomorphism invariance implies the Noether identity associated with coordinate transformations. For the Einstein–Hilbert sector, the contracted Bianchi identity gives

\\[ \nabla^\mu G\_{\mu\nu}=0. \\]

If the effective cosmological constant is constant, the field equations imply

\\[ \nabla^\mu T\_{\mu\nu}=0. \\]

A VGG construction must recover the appropriate conservation law from its effective symmetries and matter couplings. If the primitive theory permits energy exchange between sectors, the effective matter stress tensor may not be separately conserved; the total effective source must then satisfy the corresponding identity.

This is a structural consistency condition, not an optional observational refinement.

### 5.3 Controlled recovery as an asymptotic statement

Let \\(E\\) denote a characteristic physical energy or inverse length scale in the effective regime, and let \\(M\\) be the microscopic cutoff. A low-energy recovery claim should specify a parameter such as

\\[ \epsilon=\frac{E}{M}\ll1. \\]

One seeks equations of the form

\\[ \mathcal E\_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{VGG}} = M\_{\mathrm{eff}}^2G\_{\mu\nu} +\Lambda\_{\mathrm{eff}}g\_{\mu\nu} -T\_{\mu\nu} +\mathcal O(\epsilon^p)=0, \\]

where the exponent \\(p>0\\), the normalization of the error, and the domain of validity are derived from the model.

The notation \\(\mathcal O(\epsilon^p)\\) is not enough by itself. A rigorous recovery result should specify whether the error is controlled pointwise, in a suitable norm, for perturbations about a background, or in an effective-action expansion.

It should also determine whether the limit is stable under quantum corrections and whether the effective degrees of freedom remain healthy as \\(\epsilon\to0\\).

## 6. A specified testable correction: curvature-squared dynamics

A useful theory must predict something outside its general-relativistic recovery regime. The first controlled example is a curvature-squared correction.

### 6.1 Corrected effective action

Consider

\\[ \boxed{ \Gamma[g,\psi] = \int d^4x\sqrt{-g} \left[ \frac{M\_{\mathrm{eff}}^2}{2}R -\Lambda\_{\mathrm{eff}} +\alpha R^2 +\mathcal L\_{\mathrm{matter}} \right]. } \\]

The coefficient \\(\alpha\\) is dimensionless in four spacetime dimensions when \\(\hbar=c=1\\).

This action is a standard effective-gravity ansatz. It is not claimed here to have been derived from a particular microscopic theory. In VGG, the research task is to calculate \\(\alpha\\) from the primitive dynamics rather than leave it as a freely adjustable coefficient.

The \\(R^2\\) term is chosen because its variation is explicit and it produces a well-defined correction to the Einstein equations.

### 6.2 Variation of the curvature-squared term

The metric variation is

\\[ \frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}} \frac{\delta}{\delta g^{\mu\nu}} \int d^4x\sqrt{-g}\\,R^2 = H\_{\mu\nu}^{(R^2)}, \\]

where

\\[ \begin{aligned} H\_{\mu\nu}^{(R^2)} ={}& 2RR\_{\mu\nu} -\frac12g\_{\mu\nu}R^2\\\ &+2\left( g\_{\mu\nu}\Box -\nabla\_\mu\nabla\_\nu \right)R. \end{aligned} \\]

Here \\(\Box=g^{\rho\sigma}\nabla\_\rho\nabla\_\sigma\\). The corrected field equations are therefore

\\[ \boxed{ M\_{\mathrm{eff}}^2G\_{\mu\nu} +\Lambda\_{\mathrm{eff}}g\_{\mu\nu} +2\alpha H\_{\mu\nu}^{(R^2)} = T\_{\mu\nu}. } \\]

When \\(\alpha\to0\\), the equations reduce to the leading-order Einstein equations.

The correction contains terms with derivatives of the Ricci scalar, so the equations are generally fourth order in the metric when written without additional variables. This higher-derivative form has physical consequences and cannot be ignored.

### 6.3 Trace equation and the additional scalar mode

In four dimensions, the trace of the curvature-squared tensor is

\\[ g^{\mu\nu}H\_{\mu\nu}^{(R^2)} = 6\Box R. \\]

Taking the trace of the corrected field equations gives

\\[ -M\_{\mathrm{eff}}^2R +4\Lambda\_{\mathrm{eff}} +12\alpha\Box R = T, \\]

where \\(T=g^{\mu\nu}T\_{\mu\nu}\\).

For constant \\(\Lambda\_{\mathrm{eff}}\\), this can be rearranged as

\\[ \left(\Box-m_0^2\right)R = -\frac{T-4\Lambda\_{\mathrm{eff}}} {12\alpha}, \\]

with

\\[ \boxed{ m_0^2=\frac{M\_{\mathrm{eff}}^2}{12\alpha}. } \\]

This expression applies to the trace sector of the specified \\(R+\alpha R^2\\) theory. The usual healthy scalaron interpretation requires \\(\alpha>0\\) and \\(M\_{\mathrm{eff}}^2>0\\), subject to the standard assumptions about the background and sign conventions.

The correction introduces an additional scalar gravitational degree of freedom. This is not a negligible bookkeeping detail: it changes the dynamical content of the theory.

### 6.4 Effective-field-theory interpretation

There are two different ways to use the curvature-squared action.

Perturbative effective theory. The \\(R^2\\) term is treated as a small correction at energies well below the scale associated with the higher-curvature operators. The equations are solved perturbatively, and solutions that arise only from treating the truncated higher-derivative equations nonperturbatively may lie outside the effective theory's validity.

Nonperturbative \\(f(R)\\) theory. The \\(R^2\\) term is retained as an exact part of the action. The scalar degree of freedom is then included as a genuine propagating field, and its stability and phenomenology must be analyzed explicitly.

These approaches are not equivalent. A VGG model must state which one is intended.

### 6.5 The correction scale

Define

\\[ \ell\_\alpha^2=\frac{12\alpha}{M\_{\mathrm{eff}}^2} =\frac{1}{m_0^2}. \\]

The dimension of \\(\ell\_\alpha\\) is length. It sets the characteristic scale of the additional scalar response in this model.

For a process with characteristic inverse length \\(E\\), the dimensionless ratio

\\[ \delta\_\alpha=E^2\ell\_\alpha^2 \\]

measures the relative importance of derivative corrections at the level of power counting, provided the background curvature and source scales are also within the effective regime.

For \\(\delta\_\alpha\ll1\\), a perturbative treatment may be justified. When the ratio becomes order unity, the correction cannot generally be neglected. This estimate does not substitute for solving the full equations or verifying the theory's cutoff.

### 6.6 What is actually predicted?

The action fixes the form of the correction, but a numerical prediction requires a value of \\(\alpha\\), or equivalently \\(m_0\\), derived from the microscopic theory or constrained by independent observations.

The model then predicts departures from general relativity in settings where the scalar curvature responds dynamically, subject to the source, background, and screening behavior of the theory. Possible tests include:

- deviations in weak-field gravitational potentials;
- changes in the growth of cosmological perturbations;
- modified background expansion if the curvature correction is dynamically important;
- scalar gravitational responses in strong-field environments, where the model's approximations remain valid.

A particular signal cannot be asserted without solving the relevant background and perturbation equations. The correction scale supplies a parameter-controlled test program, not an automatic detection claim.

## 7. Cosmological and weak-field consequences

### 7.1 Homogeneous cosmology

For a spatially flat FLRW metric,

\\[ ds^2=-dt^2+a^2(t)d\mathbf x^2, \\]

the Ricci scalar is

\\[ R=6\left(2H^2+\dot H\right). \\]

The \\(R^2\\) term modifies the Friedmann equations through \\(H\\), \\(\dot H\\), and higher time derivatives. The trace equation becomes a dynamical equation for \\(R(t)\\), coupled to the matter trace.

In a model with constant vacuum energy and a radiation-dominated source, the matter trace vanishes, \\(T=0\\). This does not force \\(R\\) to vanish in the corrected theory, because the curvature sector has its own dynamics. Whether a nonzero scalar curvature develops depends on the initial conditions and the complete coupled equations.

For a specified matter content and initial data, the model can be integrated numerically and compared with the expansion history inferred from supernovae, baryon acoustic oscillations, and other cosmological probes.

### 7.2 Linear perturbations

At the perturbative level, the additional scalar mode modifies the relation between matter perturbations and metric potentials. In a suitable regime, the scalaron may be represented explicitly as an auxiliary field, converting the \\(R^2\\) theory into an equivalent scalar–tensor description.

The resulting predictions depend on the scalaron mass, the cosmological background, and the coupling to matter. In particular, the growth rate of matter perturbations and the relation between lensing and dynamical potentials may differ from the general-relativistic prediction.

The model must be tested against the full set of relevant observables rather than relying on a single expansion-history fit. Degeneracies with dark energy, neutrino properties, and other cosmological parameters must be included in the analysis.

### 7.3 Weak-field behavior

In a weak-field, approximately static regime, the additional scalar degree of freedom can mediate a finite-range response whose characteristic range is of order \\(m_0^{-1}\\), under the usual assumptions of the linearized theory.

The exact potential and its prefactor depend on the normalization, source, background, and boundary conditions. It would therefore be premature to state a universal Yukawa correction with a fixed amplitude for every VGG realization.

The correct procedure is to linearize the derived field equations about the relevant background, identify the propagating modes, solve the source problem, and compare the result with laboratory, Solar System, and astrophysical constraints.

## 8. The variational reduction theorem VGG would need

The preceding calculations show how an effective gravitational action produces Einstein-like equations and corrections. They do not yet show that the action emerges from primitive degrees of freedom.

A central theorem for a complete VGG model would formalize this missing step.

### 8.1 Conditional effective-action theorem

Theorem 8.1 — Conditional emergence of Einstein-like dynamics.

Let \\(S\_{\mathrm{micro}}[q;\lambda]\\) be a primitive action on a configuration space \\(\mathcal Q\\), and suppose the following conditions hold in a specified regime:

1. An admissible sector \\(\mathcal Q\_{\mathrm{adm}}\\) admits a well-defined continuum description on a four-dimensional manifold \\(\mathcal M\\).
2. A map \\(\mathfrak G:\mathcal Q\_{\mathrm{adm}}\to\operatorname{Met}(\mathcal M)\\) defines a nondegenerate Lorentzian metric.
3. A controlled reduction procedure produces a local, diffeomorphism-invariant effective action \\(\Gamma[g,\psi]\\).
4. The derivative expansion of \\(\Gamma\\) is valid for characteristic energies \\(E\ll M\\).
5.

# The leading terms in the reduced action are \\[ \Gamma[g,\psi]

\int d^4x\sqrt{-g}\
\left[\
\frac{M\_{\mathrm{eff}}^2}{2}R\
-\Lambda\_{\mathrm{eff}}\
+\mathcal L\_{\mathrm{matter}}\
\right]\
+\Delta\Gamma,\
\\]\
with \\(M\_{\mathrm{eff}}^2>0\\) and \\(\Delta\Gamma\\) suppressed by the stated effective expansion.

Then the leading effective metric equations reproduce Einstein's equations with gravitational coupling \\(8\pi G\_{\mathrm{eff}}=M\_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-2}\\) and the corresponding effective cosmological constant, up to corrections induced by \\(\Delta\Gamma\\).

Proof. By Conditions 3–5, the leading local effective action is the Einstein–Hilbert action with the stated normalization, cosmological term, and matter action. Variation with respect to \\(g^{\mu\nu}\\), using the assumed variational boundary conditions, gives the Einstein tensor equation. Contributions from \\(\Delta\Gamma\\) yield the subleading corrections. The positivity condition on \\(M\_{\mathrm{eff}}^2\\) fixes the sign of the leading tensor kinetic term under the adopted convention. \\(\square\\)

### 8.2 The theorem's limitation

The theorem is conditional because its key physical content lies in the hypotheses. In particular, Condition 5 already specifies the presence of the Einstein–Hilbert term in the effective action.

The theorem proves that if a controlled reduction generates that action, then its leading equations are Einstein-like. It does not prove that any given primitive theory generates it.

The foundational VGG result must be stronger:

\\[ S\_{\mathrm{micro}}[q;\lambda] \overset{\text{derived reduction}}{\longrightarrow} \Gamma[g,\psi] \quad\text{with}\quad M\_{\mathrm{eff}}^2>0, \\]

where the reduction calculates the curvature coefficient and the suppressed corrections from the primitive model without assuming the desired effective action.

This distinction is essential to avoid circular reasoning.

## 9. Degrees of freedom, stability, and consistency

Recovering the background Einstein equations is insufficient if the reduced theory contains unstable modes, negative-energy excitations, or inconsistent constraints.

### 9.1 Tensor-mode stability

A viable low-energy gravitational sector should reproduce the two propagating tensor polarizations of general relativity in the appropriate regime. The sign of the tensor kinetic term must be healthy, and the tensor propagation speed must agree with observational constraints in the regime where those constraints apply.

For an effective metric theory, these properties should be derived from the quadratic action for perturbations about the relevant background. The positivity of \\(M\_{\mathrm{eff}}^2\\) is a necessary sign condition for the conventional Einstein–Hilbert tensor sector, but it is not a complete stability theorem for the full model.

### 9.2 Additional degrees of freedom

Primitive theories may contain collective modes that do not disappear in the continuum limit. If an additional scalar, vector, or tensor mode remains light, the low-energy theory may differ significantly from general relativity.

A successful reduction must establish one of the following:

- the additional modes are absent by a constraint or gauge symmetry;
- they acquire masses above the effective cutoff and decouple in the low-energy regime;
- they remain in the theory and generate explicitly calculated corrections consistent with observations.

The second possibility requires care. Heavy fields can leave residual higher-dimension operators even when they are not dynamically excited at low energies.

### 9.3 Ghosts and higher derivatives

Higher-derivative field equations can signal additional propagating degrees of freedom. Whether these represent physical instabilities depends on the action, the constraints, and the energy regime.

A truncated higher-curvature effective action must not be interpreted as a fundamental theory at arbitrarily high energy. Conversely, a theory retaining higher-curvature terms nonperturbatively must analyze the complete propagating spectrum rather than assuming that the Einstein limit guarantees stability.

For the \\(R+\alpha R^2\\) model with \\(\alpha>0\\), the additional scalar can be healthy under the standard conditions. A generic collection of curvature-squared terms need not share this property.

### 9.4 Constraint algebra

If the primitive model uses constraints to define the admissible geometric sector, the constraints must be preserved by the dynamics. In a Hamiltonian formulation, their Poisson-bracket or quantum commutator structure must close appropriately.

An effective metric description that reproduces the background equations but fails to reproduce the appropriate constraint structure may not possess the correct physical degrees of freedom.

This is especially important for quantum gravity, where consistency of the constraints is tied to the treatment of gauge redundancy and spacetime diffeomorphisms.

### 9.5 Conservation and matter coupling

A viable effective theory must explain how matter couples to the emergent metric. If all low-energy matter fields couple universally to the same metric, the theory can recover the usual metric interpretation of free fall and local experiments, subject to the remaining field equations.

If different matter sectors couple to different effective metrics, composition-dependent forces or violations of the equivalence principle may arise. Such effects can be tested, but they must be derived from the primitive model rather than introduced as unexplained phenomenological freedom.

## 10. What would constitute a genuine derivation of gravity?

A complete VGG theory must pass a sequence of increasingly demanding tests.

### 10.1 Geometric derivation

The metric must arise from a specified primitive structure. The derivation must establish nondegeneracy, Lorentzian signature, transformation properties, and a consistent causal structure over the claimed regime.

### 10.2 Dynamical derivation

The primitive action and its reduction must produce the curvature term with a calculable coefficient. The derivation must explain the sign of the tensor kinetic term and determine the cosmological term or explain why it remains an independent parameter.

### 10.3 Matter derivation

The theory must identify how matter fields and their stress-energy tensor arise or couple to the emergent geometry. Universal coupling, if claimed, must follow from a symmetry, constraint, or dynamical result.

### 10.4 Controlled limit

The theory must identify a parameter regime in which the effective equations converge to general relativity. It must state the scale hierarchy, the terms neglected, and the conditions under which the expansion is uniform or otherwise controlled.

### 10.5 Distinguishable correction

The theory must derive at least one correction whose functional form and coefficient are fixed or independently constrained by the microscopic model. A generic list of allowed higher-curvature terms is not a prediction.

### 10.6 Quantum consistency

If the theory is claimed to be a quantum theory of gravity, it must define the quantum state space or path-integral measure, explain how constraints are imposed, identify the physical observables, and demonstrate a consistent treatment of quantum fluctuations.

The classical recovery of general relativity is a necessary benchmark, but it does not establish ultraviolet completeness, unitarity, a resolution of singularities, or a unique quantum description of spacetime.

## 11. Comparison with established approaches

VGG occupies a broad conceptual territory that includes induced gravity, emergent geometry, effective field theory, and several pregeometric approaches. It must be compared with these approaches before novelty can be claimed.

### 11.1 Induced gravity

In induced-gravity constructions, gravitational dynamics arise from quantum effects of other fields or from a more fundamental nonmetric sector. Under suitable conditions, the effective action can contain curvature terms.

The central VGG question is whether the primitive model calculates the coefficient of the curvature term and the remaining effective operators. The presence of an induced Einstein–Hilbert term in an existing construction would not by itself establish a new result; the microscopic mechanism and its predictions must be compared directly.

### 11.2 Sakharov-type reasoning

Sakharov's induced-gravity proposal is a historically important example of treating gravity as an effective phenomenon associated with quantum fields in a background geometry. It motivates the idea that gravitational dynamics may be induced rather than fundamental.

However, such a proposal does not automatically solve the problem of deriving the metric itself, controlling the cosmological constant, or constructing a complete ultraviolet theory. VGG must distinguish the emergence of an effective curvature action on an assumed geometric background from the more ambitious emergence of geometry and dynamics together.

### 11.3 Effective field theory of gravity

Low-energy effective field theory organizes gravitational interactions as a derivative expansion, with the Einstein–Hilbert action leading and higher-curvature terms providing corrections. This framework is a standard tool even when general relativity is not regarded as fundamental at arbitrarily high energies.

VGG can use this framework to define its recovery regime. Its additional task is to derive the effective coefficients from primitive dynamics and, ideally, explain why the low-energy geometric variables are the correct ones.

### 11.4 Metric-affine and first-order formulations

In metric-affine formulations, the metric and connection can be varied independently. In first-order formulations, curvature and torsion may play distinct roles, depending on the chosen action and constraints.

These formulations show that the Einstein equations can arise from different variational architectures. They do not, by themselves, establish that gravity emerges from nongeometric degrees of freedom.

A VGG model that begins with independent primitive connection variables must explicitly derive the relation between its effective connection and the Levi-Civita connection, or calculate any residual torsion or nonmetricity.

### 11.5 Discrete and relational approaches

Discrete structures, networks, and relational models can provide candidate microscopic descriptions from which continuum geometry might emerge. Their central challenges include the continuum limit, dimensionality, causal structure, and the recovery of local gravitational dynamics.

VGG provides a common variational language for posing these questions, but it does not resolve them merely by introducing an admissible configuration space.

## 12. The cosmological constant and the normalization problem

Two parameters are especially important in any emergence theory: the gravitational coupling and the cosmological constant.

### 12.1 The effective gravitational scale

The reduced coefficient

\\[ M\_{\mathrm{eff}}^2=\frac{1}{8\pi G\_{\mathrm{eff}}} \\]

sets the normalization of the leading gravitational response. A microscopic derivation should relate this scale to primitive couplings, a cutoff, an order parameter, or another independently defined quantity.

Dimensional analysis may restrict the possible dependence, but dimensional analysis alone does not determine the coefficient or its sign.

### 12.2 The vacuum-energy problem

The effective cosmological term \\(\Lambda\_{\mathrm{eff}}\\) receives contributions from the primitive sector and, in a quantum theory, potentially from vacuum fluctuations and matching across energy scales.

A variational emergence framework does not automatically solve the cosmological constant problem. It must show why the effective vacuum curvature is small relative to the microscopic scales or identify a dynamical mechanism that produces the observed value without unacceptable fine tuning.

If the cosmological term remains an independent parameter, that is not necessarily fatal to the effective theory, but it limits the explanatory claim.

### 12.3 Radiative stability

Even if the primitive classical reduction produces a small cosmological term, quantum corrections may shift it. A quantum-gravity claim therefore requires an account of how the relevant effective parameters change under coarse graining and renormalization.

The required analysis depends on the microscopic theory. There is no general theorem that variational emergence makes the cosmological constant naturally small.

## 13. Quantum gravity: the boundary of the present construction

### 13.1 Why classical recovery is not enough

A theory may reproduce the Einstein equations in a low-energy limit and still fail as a quantum theory.

For example, the primitive dynamics may fail to define a consistent quantum measure, the constraint algebra may not close, additional modes may violate unitarity, or the theory may lose predictive control at high energy.

Conversely, a theory can be mathematically consistent at the quantum level while failing to reproduce the observed classical gravitational regime. Both requirements must be met.

The correct logical structure is

\\[ \text{consistent microscopic theory} \quad\Longrightarrow?\quad \text{controlled low-energy gravity}, \\]

with the implication to be demonstrated for a specific model. Recovering the right-hand side does not prove the existence of the left-hand side.

### 13.2 Quantum definition of the reduced action

If the primitive degrees of freedom are quantum variables, a formal starting point is

\\[ Z[J] = \int\_{\mathcal Q\_{\mathrm{adm}}} \mathcal Dq\\, \exp\left[ iS\_{\mathrm{micro}}[q] +iS_J[q] \right], \\]

where the measure, gauge treatment, constraints, and source coupling must be defined. The corresponding effective action is obtained through a generating functional and Legendre transform, where this construction is well-defined.

The central challenges are not removed by writing this expression. The theory must specify the integration domain, the measure, the treatment of redundant configurations, and the prescription for extracting physical observables.

A Euclidean version may be useful for some calculations, but analytic continuation to Lorentzian dynamics requires justification. A well-defined Euclidean functional does not automatically guarantee a unitary Lorentzian theory.

### 13.3 Ultraviolet behavior

The low-energy derivative expansion is expected to fail when the characteristic energy approaches the cutoff. A complete quantum-gravity theory must explain what replaces that expansion, whether the theory has a consistent ultraviolet completion or fixed point, and how its low-energy couplings are matched.

VGG does not establish such behavior in the absence of a specified microscopic model. The emergence framework is compatible with multiple ultraviolet possibilities and cannot select among them without additional dynamics.

### 13.4 Singularities and high-curvature regimes

A curvature correction may alter a classical singularity, but the presence of an \\(R^2\\) term does not guarantee singularity resolution. That conclusion requires analysis of the complete field equations, admissible initial conditions, and the regime in which the effective action remains valid.

If a proposed resolution occurs only at curvatures above the cutoff, the truncated effective theory cannot establish it reliably.

## 14. Falsifiability and validation

A serious VGG program must identify outcomes that would count against its central claims.

### 14.1 Mathematical failure criteria

The proposed construction fails in its stated form if:

1. The metric map is undefined or degenerate throughout the claimed physical regime.
2. The admissible sector is not preserved by the primitive dynamics.
3. The reduction fails to produce a local or otherwise controlled effective action where one is claimed.
4. The Einstein–Hilbert coefficient has the wrong sign or cannot be calculated from the primitive parameters.
5. Additional unsuppressed modes prevent the claimed general-relativistic limit.
6. The effective equations violate the required conservation identities or possess inconsistent constraints.

Each failure may motivate a revised model, but the original claim must not be treated as established.

### 14.2 Empirical failure criteria

The curvature-squared example can be tested through the derived scalar response and its impact on gravitational or cosmological observables.

A particular model is disfavored or excluded if its independently derived parameters imply deviations that conflict with reliable measurements. It is also disfavored if the model predicts a detectable correction that is not observed at the required sensitivity.

The absence of a deviation constrains the parameter range; it does not exclude all possible emergent-gravity theories.

### 14.3 Validation protocol

A credible program should follow a reproducible sequence:

1. Define the primitive action and its admissible configuration space.
2. Derive the metric map and the reduced effective action.
3. Calculate \\(M\_{\mathrm{eff}}\\), \\(\Lambda\_{\mathrm{eff}}\\), and the leading correction coefficients.
4. Derive the propagating degrees of freedom and verify their stability.
5. Obtain the background and linearized equations.
6. Calculate a concrete observable with numerical error control.
7. Compare against general relativity and established alternative models.
8. Test the prediction against independent data and state the exclusion conditions.

The key criterion is predictive closure: the model must not tune the same parameters independently to each observable it claims to explain.

## 15. The decisive research problem: deriving curvature without assuming it

The entire program reduces to a precise mathematical obstacle.

Suppose the primitive variables \\(q\\) define an effective metric through a map \\(\mathfrak G[q]\\). It remains to prove that their dynamics generate a reduced functional containing

\\[ \int d^4x\sqrt{-g}\\,R \\]

with a positive coefficient, together with a controlled hierarchy of additional operators.

There are several possible routes, but each carries a different burden of proof.

### 15.1 Route A — Coarse-grained generation

Specify a microscopic action and measure, integrate out the appropriate microscopic degrees of freedom, and calculate the curvature terms in the effective action.

The central result would be a calculation of the form

\\[ S\_{\mathrm{micro}} \longrightarrow \Gamma\_\mu[g,\psi] = \int d^4x\sqrt{-g} \left[ -\Lambda\_\mu +\frac{M\_\mu^2}{2}R +\sum_i c_i(\mu)\mathcal O_i \right]. \\]

The derivation must determine the coefficient \\(M\_\mu^2\\), not simply identify \\(R\\) as an allowed invariant.

### 15.2 Route B — Collective geometric variables

Demonstrate that a collective mode of the primitive system transforms as an effective metric and that its long-wavelength dynamics generate a massless spin-2 excitation with the correct interactions.

This route must address the consistency of the spin-2 sector, its coupling to matter, and the conditions under which the nonlinear interactions reproduce general relativity. The appearance of a tensor order parameter alone is not sufficient.

### 15.3 Route C — Continuum limit of a discrete variational system

Define a discrete action over relational or combinatorial structures and prove that a suitable continuum limit exists. Then show that the leading continuum functional is the Einstein–Hilbert action.

The continuum limit must recover more than the correct dimensionality and a metric-like variable. It must also recover the appropriate causal structure, local degrees of freedom, constraint relations, and gravitational interactions.

### 15.4 Route D — Restricted variational geometry

Identify a mathematically characterized class of admissible configurations for which the induced metric and connection satisfy conditions that force the leading reduced equations into the Einstein class.

This route would require a genuine sufficiency theorem. The restrictions must arise from the primitive theory and must not simply encode the Einstein equations in disguised form.

These routes are possible research strategies, not interchangeable derivations. The decisive advance would be a worked microscopic model that completes one of them.

## 16. Research objectives and deliverables

The next stage of VGG should be organized around a small number of sharply defined results.

| Objective                  | Required result                                                              | Acceptance criterion                                                     |
| -------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Primitive specification    | A complete action, configuration space, constraints, and physical parameters | No essential dynamics left unspecified                                   |
| Metric emergence           | An explicit map from primitive variables to a nondegenerate effective metric | Signature, covariance, and admissible domain established                 |
| Curvature generation       | A derivation of the Einstein–Hilbert term                                    | Coefficient and sign calculated rather than assumed                      |
| General-relativistic limit | A controlled approximation to Einstein's equations                           | Error terms and scale hierarchy quantified                               |
| Testable correction        | At least one independently constrained higher-order coefficient              | A measurable prediction follows from the model                           |
| Dynamical consistency      | Complete mode and constraint analysis                                        | No unaccounted-for pathological degrees of freedom in the claimed regime |
| Quantum completion         | A consistent microscopic quantum definition                                  | Physical observables and ultraviolet behavior addressed                  |

The first five deliverables establish a serious effective theory of emergent gravity. They do not, on their own, establish a complete quantum theory of gravity.

## 17. Final conclusions

The Variational Genesis of Gravity asks whether spacetime geometry and gravitational dynamics can be obtained from a more primitive variational system rather than introduced through the Einstein–Hilbert action.

The framework developed here establishes a disciplined route to investigating that possibility:

\\[ \boxed{ q \longrightarrow \mathcal Q\_{\mathrm{adm}} \longrightarrow g\_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{eff}} \longrightarrow \Gamma[g,\psi] \longrightarrow \mathcal E\_{\mu\nu}=0. } \\]

An illustrative primitive tensor density demonstrates how an effective metric can be reconstructed algebraically. A conditional variational theorem shows that, if the reduced action contains the Einstein–Hilbert term with a positive coefficient and controlled corrections, its leading equations reproduce general relativity. A curvature-squared effective action provides an explicit correction whose additional scalar mode and characteristic mass can be derived and tested.

These results must be interpreted according to their logical status. The metric reconstruction is a kinematic construction. The recovery theorem is conditional on the effective action. The curvature-squared model is a testable effective theory, but its coefficient has not been derived from a specified microscopic system here.

The foundational task therefore remains open: derive the effective metric and Einstein–Hilbert term from a concrete primitive dynamics, without assuming either as an input.

A successful derivation would explain not only why Einstein's equations emerge, but also the range over which they apply, the coefficients governing their corrections, and the additional degrees of freedom that appear outside that range.

Even that achievement would constitute an effective theory of emergent gravity rather than automatically a complete theory of quantum gravity. Quantum consistency, ultraviolet control, and independent empirical predictions remain separate requirements.

The strongest defensible thesis of VGG is consequently a research hypothesis with a precise mathematical target:

Gravity may emerge as the leading variational dynamics of a restricted macroscopic sector of a more primitive theory. The hypothesis becomes explanatory only when the primitive theory determines the metric, the curvature action, and at least one independently testable correction.
