# Consciousness, Mathematics, and the Ontological Priority of Understanding

## Toward a Theory of Mathematical Intelligibility, Holistic Reality, and the Limits of Formal Explanation

A foundational theoretical preprint

## Abstract

The relationship between consciousness and mathematics presents a foundational problem for the philosophy of science. Mathematics is indispensable to contemporary physics, yet the capacity to formulate, interpret, and understand mathematical structures is exercised by conscious cognitive agents. This raises a question that cannot be settled merely by demonstrating the predictive success of mathematical physics: does mathematical description explain consciousness completely, or does mathematical explanation presuppose conditions of intelligibility that it cannot independently establish?

This paper develops a framework for investigating that question through five interconnected problems: the epistemic and ontological status of mathematics; the formal representation of holistic systems; the relationship between Hilbert-space quantum mechanics and physical spacetime; the explanatory limits of mathematical models of consciousness; and the possibility of a theory in which consciousness, mathematical structure, and physical reality occupy distinct but interdependent explanatory levels.

The central proposal is a principle of explanatory stratification: the mathematical representation of a phenomenon, the physical realization of the represented structure, and the conditions under which that representation becomes intelligible must be distinguished before their relationships can be assessed. These levels may be connected by rigorous mappings without being automatically reducible to one another.

A formal architecture is introduced using a space of possible experiences, a family of mathematical structures, a physical state space, and mappings between representational, operational, and interpretive domains. The framework establishes criteria for distinguishing mathematical representation from physical ontology, structural interdependence from unrestricted holism, and empirical correlation from a complete explanation of subjective experience. It further identifies what would be required to establish a stronger thesis: that consciousness is not merely the means by which mathematics is known, but a constitutive condition of mathematical reality or physical existence.

The analysis does not assume that consciousness creates the external universe, that mathematics is incapable of describing consciousness, or that quantum computation takes place in a nonphysical realm. Instead, it develops the strongest defensible form of the underlying philosophical reversal: the success of mathematical explanation does not, by itself, explain the intelligibility of mathematics or establish that every dimension of conscious experience is exhausted by formal description.

The resulting program seeks a disciplined account of the relations among experience, mathematical structure, physical realization, and scientific knowledge.

Keywords: consciousness; philosophy of mathematics; mathematical intelligibility; holism; quantum mechanics; Hilbert space; quantum computation; scientific explanation; ontology; formal systems; emergence; epistemology.

# 1. Introduction: Reversing the Direction of Explanation

Modern theoretical physics proceeds largely through mathematical formalization. Physical systems are assigned states, their evolution is represented by equations, and observations are related to predictions through mathematical rules. The extraordinary success of this method makes it tempting to regard mathematical structure as the ultimate explanatory foundation of reality.

Yet a preliminary question remains.

Mathematical equations do not interpret themselves. Symbols must be distinguished, rules must be understood, structures must be recognized, and the relationship between formal statements and physical observations must be established. These activities occur within the cognitive practices of scientific agents.

This does not invalidate mathematics. It reveals that mathematical physics involves more than equations alone.

Let the ordinary explanatory direction be represented schematically by

M⟶P⟶C,\mathcal{M}\longrightarrow\mathcal{P} \longrightarrow\mathcal{C},M⟶P⟶C,

where:

* M\mathcal{M}M denotes mathematical structure;

* P\mathcal{P}P denotes physical reality and its dynamics;

* C\mathcal{C}C denotes conscious experience.

This ordering suggests that mathematical structure determines physical behavior and that physical behavior eventually explains consciousness.

The difficulty is that the last step remains incomplete. A physical theory may correlate neural states with reports, predict cognitive performance, or model information processing without thereby settling why a physical process is accompanied by subjective experience.

A reversed investigation begins elsewhere:

C⟶Int⁡(M)⟶Rep⁡(P),\mathcal{C} \longrightarrow \operatorname{Int}(\mathcal{M}) \longrightarrow \operatorname{Rep}(\mathcal{P}),C⟶Int(M)⟶Rep(P),

where Int⁡(M)\operatorname{Int}(\mathcal{M})Int(M) denotes the intelligibility of mathematical structures and Rep⁡(P)\operatorname{Rep}(\mathcal{P})Rep(P) denotes the mathematical representation of physical reality.

This second ordering must not be mistaken for an established causal sequence. It expresses a change in the question being asked. Instead of beginning exclusively with the structure of physical law, we examine the conditions under which mathematical structures can be known, interpreted, and connected to a world.

The central thesis of this paper is therefore methodological before it is metaphysical:

A complete account of scientific explanation must distinguish the structure being described, the formalism used to describe it, the physical processes that instantiate it, and the conditions under which the description is understood.

Whether these dimensions ultimately reduce to one underlying reality remains an open problem.

## 1.1 The explanatory asymmetry

There is an asymmetry between describing an object and explaining the possibility of description.

A physical theory can assign a state xxx to a system and specify a law

dxdt=F(x,t).\frac{dx}{dt}=F(x,t).dtdx=F(x,t).

It may predict future states with remarkable accuracy. But the predictive law alone does not explain why its symbols possess meaning for an investigator, why the investigator recognizes the law as a law, or why the mathematical representation is applicable to the observed system.

Some of these questions can be addressed by cognitive science, the theory of measurement, formal semantics, and the philosophy of scientific models. The point is not that they are intrinsically unanswerable. The point is that they are not answered merely by writing down FFF.

The distinction becomes especially important when the proposed explanation attempts to explain consciousness itself using formal resources whose interpretation already depends on conscious understanding.

This is not an immediate logical contradiction. A process can participate in explaining the conditions under which that process is studied. The issue is whether the explanation is complete, noncircular, and sufficiently precise.

## 1.2 The research problem

The paper investigates four questions.

1. Can the epistemic dependence of mathematics on conscious cognition establish an ontological dependence of mathematics on consciousness?

2. Can holistic physical systems be represented mathematically without assuming that their parts possess independent, complete states?

3. What does the use of Hilbert space in quantum mechanics establish about the relationship between abstract mathematical structures and physical processes?

4. What additional conditions would be required for a mathematical theory of consciousness to explain subjective experience rather than merely correlate with it?

The answers require separating claims that are often grouped together under broad declarations about consciousness and reality.


# 2. Definitions and Foundational Distinctions

A rigorous theory must begin by specifying what its central terms mean. In particular, consciousness, mathematics, physical reality, and intelligibility cannot be treated as interchangeable expressions for a single unexplained primitive.

## 2.1 Consciousness

Let C\mathcal{C}C denote the domain of conscious episodes. An individual episode is written

c∈C.c\in\mathcal{C}.c∈C.

The term consciousness refers here to subjective experience: there is something it is like for a subject to undergo an episode. This definition does not presuppose a particular neural mechanism, metaphysical substance, or theory of phenomenal content.

We distinguish:

* Phenomenal consciousness: the presence of subjective experience.

* Access consciousness: information being available for reasoning, report, and control of behavior.

* Reflective consciousness: the capacity to make experience itself an object of attention.

* Mathematical cognition: the cognitive activities through which mathematical structures are discovered, constructed, manipulated, and understood.

These categories may overlap, but their equivalence cannot be assumed. A system's ability to perform mathematical operations would not, by itself, establish that it possesses phenomenal consciousness. Conversely, the existence of experience does not imply that every conscious episode involves explicit mathematical reasoning.

## 2.2 Mathematics

Let M\mathfrak{M}M denote a class of mathematical structures. A structure may be represented by

S=(D,R,O),\mathsf{S}=(D,\mathcal{R},\mathcal{O}),S=(D,R,O),

where:

* DDD is a domain of objects;

* R\mathcal{R}R is a collection of relations;

* O\mathcal{O}O is a collection of operations.

Examples include groups, manifolds, vector spaces, Hilbert spaces, probability spaces, and operator algebras.

A mathematical theory also includes rules governing valid constructions and inferences. A structure and a formal theory describing it are related but distinct: a formal system may describe several models, while different formal systems may characterize the same structure.

We therefore distinguish three senses of mathematics.

Formal mathematics is the system of symbols, axioms, inference rules, and derivations.

Structural mathematics concerns the abstract relations and operations characterized by mathematical theories.

Practiced mathematics consists of the activities by which mathematical agents formulate definitions, prove theorems, develop concepts, and interpret results.

The first two can be investigated independently of any particular human mathematician. The third necessarily involves actual practices and their cognitive, social, and material conditions.

Whether structural mathematics exists independently of all possible minds is a further metaphysical question.

## 2.3 Physical reality

Let P\mathcal{P}P denote the domain of physical systems, states, processes, and interactions.

A physical theory need not identify physical reality with a single mathematical structure. Instead, it typically provides a model

μ:Ptarget⟶M,\mu:\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{target}} \longrightarrow \mathfrak{M},μ:Ptarget⟶M,

where Ptarget\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{target}}Ptarget is the portion of reality addressed by the theory and μ\muμ maps that portion into a mathematical representation.

In practice, this mapping may be indirect. Experiments produce records; calibration procedures connect records to physical quantities; theoretical assumptions connect those quantities to a model.

The representation is therefore part of a larger inferential chain rather than a transparent identity between reality and equations.

## 2.4 Intelligibility

Let I\mathcal{I}I denote the domain of intelligibility: the conditions under which a mathematical expression or model can be interpreted as expressing a proposition, representing a system, or supporting an inference.

For a mathematical object m∈Mm\in\mathfrak{M}m∈M, intelligibility is not simply the object's existence. It concerns a relation between the object, an interpretive practice, and a context of application.

We may write

Int(m;c,κ),\mathsf{Int}(m;c,\kappa),Int(m;c,κ),

where ccc is a cognitive or conscious episode and κ\kappaκ is an interpretive context comprising conventions, definitions, inferential rules, and relevant background knowledge.

This notation does not assert that mathematical truths depend on individual opinion. It records the difference between a proposition being true in a specified mathematical structure and a subject understanding that proposition.

A theorem may hold whether or not anyone knows it. Its being understood by a particular subject is a separate event.

## 2.5 Four relations that must not be conflated

| Relation       | Meaning                                                             | What it does not establish                                   |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Representation | A mathematical object models a target system                        | That the target is literally a mathematical object           |
| Realization    | A physical system implements a specified operation                  | That every mathematical structure is physically realizable   |
| Interpretation | An agent understands the significance of a formal expression        | That the expression's truth depends on that agent            |
| Constitution   | One domain is claimed to be fundamental to the existence of another | That the claimed dependence follows from successful modeling |

The last relation is the most demanding. An ontological theory must provide arguments for constitution, not merely demonstrate representation or interpretation.

# 3. The Epistemic Priority of Consciousness

The first proposition of the proposed framework concerns knowledge rather than the ultimate composition of reality.

## 3.1 Mathematical knowledge as an activity

Consider a mathematical theorem

Γ⊢φ,\Gamma\vdash\varphi,Γ⊢φ,

where Γ\GammaΓ is a set of assumptions and φ\varphiφ is a conclusion derivable from them.

The formal relation expresses derivability under specified rules. A mathematician's recognition that the derivation is valid is a distinct epistemic event.

Let

D(Γ,φ)\mathsf{D}(\Gamma,\varphi)D(Γ,φ)

denote the formal derivation and

Ka(Γ⊢φ)\mathsf{K}_a(\Gamma\vdash\varphi)Ka(Γ⊢φ)

denote agent aaa's knowledge that the conclusion follows from the assumptions.

The existence of a derivation does not guarantee that a particular agent has discovered it, understands it, or can verify it. Conversely, an agent's belief that a derivation is valid does not guarantee that it is valid.

Thus,

D(Γ,φ)≢Ka(Γ⊢φ).\mathsf{D}(\Gamma,\varphi) \not\equiv \mathsf{K}_a(\Gamma\vdash\varphi).D(Γ,φ)≡Ka(Γ⊢φ).

This distinction is elementary but consequential. Formal validity and conscious apprehension of validity are different properties.

## 3.2 The epistemic dependence proposition

Proposition 1 — Epistemic dependence of practiced mathematics.

For any mathematical practice ppp that involves conscious mathematical understanding, the occurrence of that practice presupposes cognitive capacities sufficient for the relevant acts of understanding.

Argument. A practice involving the conscious interpretation of definitions, the apprehension of inferential relations, and the recognition of proof steps requires the corresponding cognitive activities. Remove those activities from the specified practice and it no longer occurs as that same conscious practice. Therefore, conscious mathematical understanding is a condition of the occurrence of practiced mathematics in this sense. □\square□

This proposition is deliberately limited. It does not establish that every formal derivation requires a conscious agent to execute it, nor does it establish that mathematical structures cannot exist independently of minds.

It establishes a relation between consciousness and mathematical knowledge.

## 3.3 Why epistemic dependence does not establish ontological dependence

Suppose an agent discovers a previously unknown theorem. The discovery is a conscious event, but the theorem may be true in the relevant structure independently of the discovery.

The distinction can be formalized as follows:

Truth(φ,S)≠Knowledgea(φ).\mathsf{Truth}(\varphi,\mathsf{S}) \neq \mathsf{Knowledge}_a(\varphi).Truth(φ,S)=Knowledgea(φ).

The left-hand side concerns whether φ\varphiφ holds in structure S\mathsf{S}S. The right-hand side concerns whether agent aaa knows it.

The two may be related through proof, interpretation, and evidence, but they are not identical.

Three broad philosophies of mathematics interpret this distinction differently.

Mathematical realism holds, in one or another form, that mathematical truths or structures are not constituted by individual acts of thought.

Formalism emphasizes formal systems, symbol manipulation, and derivability, though formalist positions differ in how they interpret mathematical practice and truth.

Constructivist and intuitionist approaches place greater emphasis on constructions, proofs, and the conditions under which mathematical objects can be meaningfully asserted to exist.

None of these positions can be eliminated merely by observing that human beings learn mathematics through conscious activity.

## 3.4 The stronger thesis and its additional burden

The stronger claim that consciousness creates mathematics can mean at least three different things:

H1:Conscious agents create mathematical notations and theories,H2:Mathematical structures depend ontologically on consciousness,H3:Consciousness constitutes the physical reality represented by mathematics.\begin{aligned} H_1 &: \text{Conscious agents create mathematical notations and theories},\\ H_2 &: \text{Mathematical structures depend ontologically on consciousness},\\ H_3 &: \text{Consciousness constitutes the physical reality represented by mathematics}. \end{aligned}H1H2H3:Conscious agents create mathematical notations and theories,:Mathematical structures depend ontologically on consciousness,:Consciousness constitutes the physical reality represented by mathematics.

The first is compatible with many philosophical positions. The second requires an account of mathematical existence. The third additionally requires a theory of physical constitution.

Evidence for H1H_1H1 is not sufficient evidence for H2H_2H2, and evidence for H2H_2H2 would not automatically establish H3H_3H3.

A defensible research program must identify which hypothesis it investigates and what observations or arguments could discriminate it from alternatives.

# 4. Can a Formal System Explain the Conditions of Its Own Intelligibility?

The next problem concerns explanatory circularity.

## 4.1 Formal derivation and semantic interpretation

A formal language LLL may be characterized by a vocabulary, formation rules, axioms, and inference rules. A derivation is a finite or otherwise specified sequence of expressions satisfying those rules.

A semantic interpretation assigns meanings or models to expressions. In a model S\mathsf{S}S, a statement φ\varphiφ may be true or false:

S⊨φ.\mathsf{S}\models\varphi.S⊨φ.

Syntax and semantics are connected, but they are not identical. A string can be manipulated according to formal rules without its physical inscription possessing an intrinsic interpretation for the machine that manipulates it.

For human mathematics, understanding often involves both formal competence and semantic grasp. For machine computation, the relation between formal processing and meaning depends on the system and the account of semantics being adopted.

This gives rise to a foundational question: can a theory of consciousness explain the very capacities through which that theory is formulated and understood?

## 4.2 Circularity is not automatically vicious

Consider a scientific theory of vision that is developed by scientists who themselves rely on vision. This dependence does not invalidate the theory. Independent instruments, calibration, cross-checks, and predictive tests can constrain the resulting account.

Similarly, a mathematical theory of cognition is not refuted merely because mathematical reasoning is one of the capacities it models.

A vicious explanatory circle arises only under stronger conditions: for example, when the proposed explanation presupposes precisely the fact it claims to explain, without providing an independent mechanism, derivation, or reduction.

We must distinguish:

1. Methodological self-reference: a theory is developed using the capacity it studies.

2. Epistemic dependence: knowledge of the theory requires the capacity being studied.

3. Explanatory circularity: the theory's explanation of that capacity presupposes the same capacity in the relevant explanatory sense.

4. Logical inconsistency: the theory entails a contradiction.

These are distinct conditions. The first two do not entail the third, and the third does not automatically entail the fourth.

## 4.3 The explanatory closure question

Let TTT be a theory intended to explain consciousness, and let CCC denote the phenomenon of conscious experience.

A complete explanatory account would need to specify what counts as explaining CCC. For example, it might seek to derive:

* the functional capacities associated with consciousness;

* the neural and physical conditions under which conscious episodes occur;

* the structure and contents of reported experience;

* the existence of subjective experience itself.

A theory may succeed at one task while leaving another unresolved.

Let the explanatory target be partitioned into components,

C=(Cfunc,Cneural,Ccontent,Cphen),C=(C_{\mathrm{func}},C_{\mathrm{neural}}, C_{\mathrm{content}},C_{\mathrm{phen}}),C=(Cfunc,Cneural,Ccontent,Cphen),

where the final component denotes phenomenal consciousness.

This partition is analytical rather than a claim that the components are ontologically separable.

A theory that accurately predicts CfuncC_{\mathrm{func}}Cfunc and CneuralC_{\mathrm{neural}}Cneural has achieved genuine explanatory progress. Whether it also explains CphenC_{\mathrm{phen}}Cphen depends on the adequacy of the bridge principles connecting physical or functional descriptions to subjective experience.

The unresolved issue is not whether mathematics can describe anything about consciousness. It is whether a mathematical description, supplemented by an adequate physical and conceptual theory, can account for every feature of consciousness included in the explanatory target.

## 4.4 The incompleteness of a description is not the impossibility of explanation

A map is not identical to the territory. A model is not identical to the modeled system. These observations establish representational distinctions, not an absolute limit on mathematical explanation.

To infer that no mathematical theory could explain consciousness, one would need to establish that a relevant feature of consciousness is inherently inaccessible to every admissible mathematical model, rather than simply absent from existing models.

That is a substantially stronger proposition.

Gödel's incompleteness theorems do not, by themselves, establish that consciousness transcends mathematics. Their conclusions concern formal systems satisfying specific mathematical conditions. Applying them to consciousness requires additional premises about the relation between human understanding, formal provability, and the nature of conscious thought.

The appropriate conclusion is therefore conditional: the possibility of complete mathematical explanation remains open, but its success cannot be inferred solely from the successes of mathematical physics elsewhere.

# 5. Holism: From Independent Objects to Relational Constitution

The claim that reality is holistic can be formulated more precisely by distinguishing several kinds of dependence.

## 5.1 Weak, structural, and strong holism

Weak holism states that interactions between parts materially affect the behavior of the whole.

Structural holism states that some properties of a whole are determined by relational structures that cannot be recovered from isolated descriptions of the parts alone.

Strong ontological holism states that the whole is ontologically prior to, or constitutive of, the parts.

These claims differ in strength. Weak holism is common in classical physics, chemistry, biology, and engineering. Structural holism is mathematically expressible in network theory, many-body physics, and relational models. Strong ontological holism requires an additional metaphysical argument.

The word inside can refer to different things in this debate: intrinsic properties, first-person experience, internal state, or ontological constitution. These meanings must not be conflated.

## 5.2 Composite systems and nonseparability

Let a composite system consist of two subsystems AAA and BBB. Its Hilbert space is

HAB=HA⊗HB.\mathcal{H}_{AB} = \mathcal{H}_A\otimes\mathcal{H}_B.HAB=HA⊗HB.

A pure product state has the form

∣ψAB⟩=∣ψA⟩⊗∣ψB⟩.|\psi_{AB}\rangle = |\psi_A\rangle\otimes|\psi_B\rangle.∣ψAB⟩=∣ψA⟩⊗∣ψB⟩.

But not every state in the composite Hilbert space has this form. An entangled state cannot be represented as a single product of subsystem state vectors.

For example,

∣Ψ⟩=12(∣0⟩A∣0⟩B+∣1⟩A∣1⟩B).|\Psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left( |0\rangle_A|0\rangle_B+ |1\rangle_A|1\rangle_B \right).∣Ψ⟩=21(∣0⟩A∣0⟩B+∣1⟩A∣1⟩B).

The joint state contains correlations that are not represented by assigning independent pure states to AAA and BBB.

The reduced state of subsystem AAA is

ρA=Tr⁡B(∣Ψ⟩⟨Ψ∣)=12∣0⟩⟨0∣+12∣1⟩⟨1∣.\rho_A = \operatorname{Tr}_B \left(|\Psi\rangle\langle\Psi|\right) = \frac{1}{2}|0\rangle\langle0| + \frac{1}{2}|1\rangle\langle1|.ρA=TrB(∣Ψ⟩⟨Ψ∣)=21∣0⟩⟨0∣+21∣1⟩⟨1∣.

The same holds for BBB. Neither reduced state captures the complete joint-state structure.

This is a mathematically precise sense in which a description of the whole cannot always be reconstructed from descriptions of the parts considered separately.

It is not evidence that the two systems are literally connected by a hidden conscious interior. It is evidence that quantum-state descriptions permit nonseparable structures.

## 5.3 Holism as a failure of unrestricted factorization

Let ΩAB\Omega_{AB}ΩAB denote the admissible states of a composite system. A separability assumption would require that the relevant global state be recoverable from subsystem states through a suitable product construction.

Schematically,

ΩAB=?ΩA×ΩB.\Omega_{AB} \stackrel{?}{=} \Omega_A\times\Omega_B.ΩAB=?ΩA×ΩB.

For quantum systems, this relation does not generally hold as a description of all joint states. The tensor-product state space contains entangled states that cannot be reduced to a single product-state assignment.

More generally, a holistic system may possess a global constraint

G(xA,xB,RAB)=0,\mathcal{G}(x_A,x_B,R_{AB})=0,G(xA,xB,RAB)=0,

where RABR_{AB}RAB represents relational information not exhausted by independent specifications of xAx_AxA and xBx_BxB.

This formulation is useful across many domains. In a mechanical system, RABR_{AB}RAB may encode interaction constraints. In a network, it may encode topology. In quantum theory, the relevant global structure is represented by the joint state and its correlations.

The formalism establishes that relational dependence can be mathematically essential. It does not determine whether relations are metaphysically more fundamental than relata.

## 5.4 Internal relations and external description

A relation may be described externally by an observer while remaining constitutive of the system being modeled. For example, a crystal lattice has a relational structure that constrains the behavior of its constituent atoms. The atoms and their interactions can be studied from an external perspective, but the resulting explanation must include the relational organization.

Thus, the contrast between inside and outside is not always a contrast between two physical locations.

We can distinguish:

external description≠external constitution.\text{external description} \neq \text{external constitution}.external description=external constitution.

An external description can represent internal dependencies. Conversely, a first-person experience of inwardness does not by itself identify an additional physical dimension.

The central scientific question is which relational structures are necessary for the phenomena under study, how they can be measured or inferred, and whether competing models preserve the same explanatory relationships.

## 5.5 The limit of the holism argument

The claim that everything is connected to everything else is stronger than the claim that some systems exhibit irreducible relational properties.

To establish unrestricted universal connectedness, one would need to define the relevant relation and demonstrate that it links every pair of elements in the intended domain. Different relations produce different notions of connectedness. Gravitational influence, causal dependence, quantum entanglement, information transfer, and shared membership in a global state are not interchangeable.

A disciplined holistic theory should therefore specify:

1. the domain of entities or events;

2. the relation under consideration;

3. the criteria for direct and indirect connection;

4. whether the connection is causal, informational, structural, or ontological;

5. what empirical or mathematical result would distinguish the theory from a nonholistic alternative.

Without these conditions, holism remains an intuition rather than a sufficiently defined scientific hypothesis.


# 6. Hilbert Space and the Ontology of Quantum Computation

Quantum computing provides a particularly revealing case because it forces a distinction between physical operations and the mathematical spaces used to represent them.

The central mistake to avoid is identifying the space in which a state is represented with the physical location in which the corresponding process occurs.

## 6.1 The mathematical structure of a quantum computer

A quantum system is represented by a state in a Hilbert space H\mathcal{H}H. For a pure state,

∣ψ⟩∈H,⟨ψ∣ψ⟩=1.|\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{H}, \qquad \langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1.∣ψ⟩∈H,⟨ψ∣ψ⟩=1.

An observable is represented, in the standard formulation, by an appropriate self-adjoint operator. A closed-system transformation is represented by a unitary operator UUU:

U†U=UU†=I.U^\dagger U=UU^\dagger=I.U†U=UU†=I.

The transformation of a state is

∣ψ′⟩=U∣ψ⟩.|\psi'\rangle=U|\psi\rangle.∣ψ′⟩=U∣ψ⟩.

For a system of nnn idealized two-level quantum subsystems,

Hn=(C2)⊗n,dim⁡Hn=2n.\mathcal{H}_n = (\mathbb{C}^2)^{\otimes n}, \qquad \dim\mathcal{H}_n=2^n.Hn=(C2)⊗n,dimHn=2n.

The exponential growth of the dimension is one of the central structural features of quantum computation. It does not mean that 2n2^n2n independently accessible classical values are automatically available for reading. The constraints imposed by quantum measurement, entanglement, noise, and the structure of admissible operations remain decisive.

A quantum algorithm is specified through an initial-state preparation, a sequence of transformations, and a measurement procedure. Its computational advantage depends on how these elements work together for a particular task.

## 6.2 Does the computation occur in Hilbert space?

The phrase computation in Hilbert space can mean two different things.

In the mathematical sense, the system's state and its transformations are represented in Hilbert space.

In the physical sense, a quantum processor undergoes physical changes that implement those transformations. A superconducting processor, for example, uses controlled electromagnetic interactions in a physical device. Other architectures employ trapped ions, photons, or alternative quantum systems.

The Hilbert-space representation is not itself a claim that the hardware occupies an additional spatial region.

A useful distinction is

H⏟state representation≠X⏟physical configuration space,\underbrace{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{state representation}} \qquad\neq\qquad \underbrace{\mathcal{X}}_{\text{physical configuration space}},state representationH=physical configuration spaceX,

where X\mathcal{X}X denotes an appropriate space of physical configurations or states in a physical model.

The two spaces may be related by a mathematical map, but they need not be identical.

The distinction also applies to classical physics. A system's state may be represented by a point in phase space, even though phase space is not ordinarily interpreted as an additional physical room through which the apparatus travels.

## 6.3 The state-space realization problem

The important question is not whether an abstract vector literally moves through an abstract space. It is how the physical system realizes the state transitions represented by the vector and the operators.

Let x∈Xx\in\mathcal{X}x∈X represent a physical configuration and let μ(x)∈H\mu(x)\in\mathcal{H}μ(x)∈H represent its quantum state. For an idealized transformation, we may write

μ ⁣(FU(x))=Uμ(x),\mu\!\left(F_U(x)\right) = U\mu(x),μ(FU(x))=Uμ(x),

where FUF_UFU denotes the physical process implementing UUU.

This equation is schematic. A complete implementation theory must account for the physical control parameters, preparation procedures, interactions with the environment, and measurement records.

The equation expresses a compatibility requirement: the mathematical state transformation and the physical implementation must agree under the representation map.

It does not require the physical system to move through Hilbert space as though Hilbert space were a second laboratory.

## 6.4 Quantum mechanics as a representation of physical operations

A deeper formulation considers a family of physical operations Ophys\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{phys}}Ophys and a mathematical representation

π:Ophys⟶O(H),\pi: \mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{phys}} \longrightarrow \mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}),π:Ophys⟶O(H),

where O(H)\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H})O(H) denotes an appropriate class of operators acting on the Hilbert space.

The representation is useful when it preserves relevant structural relations. If two physical operations compose in a particular order, their mathematical representations must reproduce the corresponding composition, subject to the assumptions of the model.

The success of quantum theory indicates that this representation works extremely well in its tested domains.

But representational success leaves open a philosophical question: does the Hilbert-space structure describe a physical reality that exists independently of the representation, or does it capture a more fundamental aspect of physical ontology?

Several interpretations of quantum mechanics answer this question differently. The mathematical formalism alone does not settle the dispute.

## 6.5 The ontology problem is real, but it is not a computational contradiction

The argument that quantum computation is incoherent because it uses an abstract Hilbert space depends on an invalid inference:

1. Hilbert space is a mathematical abstraction.

2. Quantum computation is described using Hilbert space.

3. Therefore, the computation must physically occur in an abstract mathematical realm.

The conclusion does not follow. Mathematical descriptions routinely represent physical processes without being physical locations themselves.

Nevertheless, the ontological question remains important. What physical reality corresponds to the structure represented by a quantum state? Which aspects of the state are physically real, which encode predictive relationships, and how should the formalism be interpreted?

These questions are legitimate subjects of foundational physics. They must be separated from the claim that quantum computers cannot operate because their mathematics is abstract.

## 6.6 The observer is not automatically the source of the state

The presence of an observer in the interpretation of measurement does not establish that consciousness physically creates quantum outcomes.

In quantum mechanics, measurement can be modeled through interactions between a system, an apparatus, and an environment. The role of conscious observation in the fundamental dynamics remains interpretation-dependent; it is not required as an established extra operation in standard quantum-computing protocols.

The following distinction is essential:

A measurement requires an interpretive account⇏consciousness causes the measurement outcome.\text{A measurement requires an interpretive account} \not\Rightarrow \text{consciousness causes the measurement outcome}.A measurement requires an interpretive account⇒consciousness causes the measurement outcome.

A theory that attributes a constitutive role to consciousness would need to specify that role mathematically and identify what distinguishes its predictions from those of theories in which physical interactions suffice.

## 6.7 The unresolved bridge

The quantum-computing case therefore motivates a more precise foundational problem:

> What relation connects the mathematical structure of a physical theory to the physical processes that realize its operations, and what relation connects both to the conscious understanding of the theory?

This question has three components:

# 7. Mathematical Structure and the Physical World

The effectiveness of mathematics in physics motivates a further question: why do mathematical structures describe physical regularities so successfully?

## 7.1 The modeling relation

Let D\mathcal{D}D denote a domain of empirical data, and let S\mathsf{S}S denote a mathematical structure. A scientific model includes a mapping

μ:D⟶S,\mu:\mathcal{D}\longrightarrow\mathsf{S},μ:D⟶S,

or, more commonly, a relation that connects measured quantities to mathematical variables.

A model also specifies how observations are generated or predicted. In a deterministic model,

In a probabilistic model,

The model's scientific value depends on more than the existence of a mathematical representation. Its assumptions must be coherent, its parameters must be constrained, and its predictions must be evaluated against data not merely used to construct it.

## 7.2 Why predictive success does not settle ontology

Suppose two mathematical models reproduce the same observable results within a specified domain. Their empirical equivalence in that domain does not guarantee that their ontological interpretations are equivalent.

Let T1T_1T1 and T2T_2T2 be two theories, and let O\mathcal{O}O be the set of observables relevant to an experiment. They are empirically equivalent relative to O\mathcal{O}O if

Conversely, different mathematical formulations can represent the same physical content through a change of variables, a gauge redundancy, or an appropriate isomorphism. Distinct notation does not necessarily indicate distinct physical ontology.

We must therefore distinguish empirical adequacy from ontological uniqueness.

## 7.3 Mathematical intelligibility as a second-order problem

The first-order scientific question asks:

What laws govern the observed system?\text{What laws govern the observed system?}What laws govern the observed system?

The second-order question asks:

Why is this kind of mathematical law applicable and intelligible?\text{Why is this kind of mathematical law applicable and intelligible?}Why is this kind of mathematical law applicable and intelligible?

The first question is addressed through theory construction, experimentation, and mathematical analysis. The second involves the philosophy of representation, the structure of scientific explanation, and the relation between mathematical cognition and the world.

A successful answer to the first does not automatically answer the second. But neither does the second invalidate the first.

The two questions operate at different explanatory levels.

## 7.4 The possibility of structural realism

One position consistent with this distinction is structural realism: the view that scientific theories may successfully capture structural relations in the world even when the intrinsic nature of the entities involved remains uncertain.

Under a structural-realist interpretation, the effectiveness of mathematics is not necessarily evidence that the universe is literally a mathematical object. It may indicate that mathematical structures capture stable relational features of physical phenomena.

This view does not solve every problem. It must still explain what kind of relation holds between physical reality and mathematical structure, and how the relevant structures are identified. But it provides an alternative to the assumption that either mathematics is merely a subjective invention or physical reality is nothing but an abstract mathematical object.

The possibility of structural realism illustrates why the question of mathematical intelligibility cannot be settled by a simple opposition between mind and matter.

# 8. A Formal Architecture of Consciousness, Mathematics, and Reality

The preceding analysis motivates a formal framework in which consciousness, mathematical structure, physical realization, and interpretation are represented as distinct domains connected by specified relations.

The purpose of the framework is not to declare a new metaphysical substance. It is to make the competing explanatory commitments explicit.

Define the quadruple

It does not establish that the physical system is ontologically identical to its mathematical representation.

## 8.3 Interpretive adequacy

A model may be mathematically valid and empirically successful while being interpreted incorrectly. We therefore introduce a predicate

1. internal mathematical consistency;

3. empirical accuracy;

4. robustness under appropriate changes in modeling assumptions;

5. explanatory or predictive performance relative to alternatives.

The criteria are not interchangeable. Internal consistency does not imply empirical accuracy, and empirical accuracy does not imply ontological uniqueness.

This proposition applies equally to consciousness models, quantum-state representations, and mathematical descriptions of spacetime.

## 8.5 The nonreduction principle as a research criterion

A stronger question concerns whether consciousness can be reduced to physical and mathematical descriptions.

Let CCC be a target feature of conscious experience, and let TTT be a physical-mathematical theory. A reduction would require more than a correlation between variables. It would require an account of how the relevant properties of CCC follow from, or are identified with, the resources of TTT under explicit bridge principles.

A theory cannot establish nonreducibility merely by leaving a feature unexplained. But a theory also cannot establish complete reducibility merely by predicting observable correlates of that feature.

## 8.6 What the architecture contributes

The framework separates four questions that are otherwise easily conflated:

* What is experienced?

* What mathematical structure represents the phenomenon?

* What physical process instantiates the relevant dynamics?

* What warrants the interpretation connecting the model to the phenomenon?

A complete theory might eventually unify these domains. The framework does not prohibit unification. It requires that unification be demonstrated rather than assumed.


# 9. The Consciousness–Mathematics Boundary

The central thesis can now be sharpened. The issue is not whether mathematical symbols can refer to consciousness, but whether a mathematical theory can explain the full nature of the phenomenon to which those symbols refer.

## 9.1 Three levels of explanation

Let a theory of consciousness be represented by

TC=(S,D,B),T_C=(\mathsf{S},\mathsf{D},\mathsf{B}),TC=(S,D,B),

where:

* S\mathsf{S}S is a mathematical structure;

* D\mathsf{D}D is a set of derived results;

* B\mathsf{B}B is a collection of bridge principles connecting the structure to conscious phenomena.

We distinguish three levels of explanatory success.

Level I: Correlational adequacy. The theory identifies reliable relationships between physical or computational variables and observable indicators associated with consciousness.

Level II: Mechanistic adequacy. The theory explains how the relevant physical and cognitive processes produce the functional capacities under investigation.

Level III: Phenomenal adequacy. The theory explains, or provides a defensible account of, why and how those processes are associated with subjective experience.

The third level is the most philosophically contested. Its definition also depends on what counts as an explanation of phenomenal consciousness.

A theory that satisfies Level I may be scientifically useful without satisfying Level II. A theory that satisfies Levels I and II may still leave unresolved the relationship between physical mechanism and subjective experience.

This is not an argument that Level III is impossible. It is a demand that claims of complete explanation identify the additional work needed to reach it.

## 9.2 The explanatory gap

Suppose a neuroscientific model predicts that a subject will report seeing red under a specified set of conditions. The model might describe neural activity, visual processing, attention, memory, and verbal report.

The remaining question is whether the model explains the subjective character of seeing red or only the physical and functional processes associated with that experience.

A physicalist may argue that an adequate account of the physical and functional processes is sufficient, even if the explanatory relationship initially seems unintuitive. A dualist may argue that phenomenal properties require a different ontological category. A panpsychist may propose that experience is a fundamental feature of reality. Other approaches seek to dissolve or reformulate the problem.

The present framework does not assume one of these positions.

Instead, it identifies a shared requirement: the theory must specify which features of consciousness it explains and what would count as explanatory completion.

## 9.3 Can the concept of consciousness be fully formalized?

A formal theory can define a mathematical object called CCC. But defining such an object does not establish that it captures phenomenal consciousness.

For example, let a system's functional state be

x(t)∈Rnx(t)\in\mathbb{R}^nx(t)∈Rn

and let a proposed consciousness measure be

Q(x)∈R.\mathcal{Q}(x)\in\mathbb{R}.Q(x)∈R.

The measure might quantify integration, complexity, information accessibility, recurrent processing, or some other property. Its usefulness depends on its definition and validation.

The existence of Q\mathcal{Q}Q does not establish that Q(x)\mathcal{Q}(x)Q(x) measures subjective experience rather than a correlated property.

To support that interpretation, the theory must supply a bridge principle:

Q(x)∈R⟹specified claim about experience,\mathcal{Q}(x)\in\mathcal{R} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{specified claim about experience},Q(x)∈R⟹specified claim about experience,

where R\mathcal{R}R is an explicitly defined range of values.

The bridge principle may be empirically supported, theoretically motivated, or conjectural. These statuses must be distinguished.

## 9.4 The self-reference objection

One might argue that a mathematical theory of consciousness is inherently circular because consciousness is required to understand the theory.

This argument is insufficient as stated.

A theory of digestion does not require digestion to be the subject's method of understanding the theory. A theory of vision may be tested through instruments and cross-modal evidence. A theory of mathematical cognition can use behavioral experiments, neural measurements, and formal models.

Nevertheless, conscious mathematical understanding introduces a distinctive reflexive problem: the theory's development and evaluation occur within the cognitive domain it seeks to explain.

This makes the epistemic problem unavoidable, but it does not make the scientific project self-contradictory.

The strongest defensible claim is therefore not that consciousness cannot be described mathematically. It is that a mathematical description of consciousness does not automatically explain the epistemic and phenomenal conditions under which conscious experience and mathematical understanding occur.

# 10. The Difference Between Constructing Mathematics and Discovering Mathematical Truths

The claim that mathematics is created by consciousness often combines two distinct activities: constructing mathematical representations and discovering properties that are not freely determined by the constructor.

## 10.1 Constructed notation, constrained consequences

A mathematician may choose a notation, introduce definitions, and specify axioms. Once the formal system is fixed, however, its consequences are constrained by its rules.

Suppose a theory TTT contains axioms A1,…,AnA_1,\ldots,A_nA1,…,An, and a theorem φ\varphiφ follows from them:

A1,…,An⊢Tφ.A_1,\ldots,A_n\vdash_T\varphi.A1,…,An⊢Tφ.

The mathematician's freedom to introduce the axioms does not imply freedom to choose which conclusions validly follow from them.

The act of construction and the consequences of construction must be distinguished.

This yields a modest but important principle:

Human agency in the creation of mathematical systems does not entail arbitrary control over the truths derivable within those systems.

## 10.2 The discovery–invention spectrum

Different components of mathematical practice can be understood differently.

* Notation and symbolic conventions are substantially constructed.

* Definitions specify which concepts a theory studies.

* Axioms select a formal or structural setting.

* Proofs establish consequences under the adopted rules.

* Mathematical discovery identifies relationships that may not have been anticipated by the investigator.

* Applications establish whether a mathematical structure is useful for modeling a particular domain.

Whether the objects of mathematics are ultimately invented or discovered remains a philosophical question. The distinction is not resolved by classifying every component of mathematical practice under a single label.

## 10.3 Why the distinction matters for consciousness

Suppose conscious agents create a formal system TTT. Does it follow that the structures characterized by TTT depend for their existence on those agents?

No such inference follows without an additional metaphysical premise.

The formalist may emphasize the system and its derivations. The realist may regard the system as describing an independently existing structure. The constructivist may insist that existence claims be supported by appropriate constructions.

The same mathematical result can therefore be embedded in different philosophies of mathematics.

The consciousness-first thesis becomes more precise when it asks whether conscious activity is constitutive of mathematical existence, rather than merely essential to the discovery, communication, or use of mathematics.

## 10.4 A criterion for the stronger thesis

A theory asserting that consciousness constitutes mathematical reality would need to specify:

1. what it means for a mathematical structure to exist;

2. which features of consciousness are constitutive of that existence;

3. how mathematical truth is determined under the proposed account;

4. whether mathematical structures can exist when no conscious agent represents them;

5. how the proposal differs from existing realist, formalist, and constructivist interpretations.

Without such commitments, the assertion that consciousness creates mathematics remains ambiguous between a relatively uncontroversial claim about mathematical practice and a much stronger claim about ontology.

# 11. Is Consciousness Fundamental to Physical Reality?

The possibility that consciousness is fundamental requires separate consideration. Even if consciousness is indispensable to mathematical knowledge, it does not follow that consciousness is the fundamental substance or principle of the universe.

## 11.1 Competing ontological models

Let C\mathcal{C}C denote conscious experience and P\mathcal{P}P denote physical reality. Several broad positions can be distinguished.

Physicalist approaches treat consciousness as dependent on, or ultimately accounted for by, physical processes.

Dualist approaches posit that conscious and physical properties are distinct in a way that cannot be reduced to one another.

Idealist approaches assign consciousness or mind a fundamental role in the constitution of reality.

Panpsychist approaches treat experience or proto-experiential properties as fundamental or widespread features of nature.

Neutral-monist approaches propose a more basic domain from which mental and physical descriptions emerge as distinct aspects.

These labels encompass diverse theories. None can be evaluated solely by assigning it a name; the relevant question is whether a particular formulation provides coherent explanations and discriminating consequences.

## 11.2 The danger of reversing an unjustified hierarchy

If physicalism assumes that consciousness must be explained by physical structure, a consciousness-first theory may be tempted to make the opposite assumption: physical structure must be explained by consciousness.

But reversing an assumption does not establish its negation.

The correct procedure is to identify the explanatory commitments of each theory and compare their consequences.

For example, an idealist account must explain why physical observations exhibit stable regularities, why different observers obtain compatible results under controlled conditions, and why interventions have reproducible effects. A physicalist account must explain how physical processes relate to subjective experience and whether its proposed account addresses the phenomenal dimension rather than only behavior and function.

The burden of explanation applies in both directions.

## 11.3 Consciousness as a constitutive condition

A more limited thesis avoids identifying consciousness with a universal substance. It asks whether consciousness is a constitutive condition of the world as known.

This claim has a clear epistemological interpretation: every empirical world-description available to an investigator depends on cognitive capacities, conceptual frameworks, measurement procedures, and interpretive practices.

It does not imply that the world exists only when it is observed.

We can express the distinction as

World as known≠World as it exists independently of our knowledge.\text{World as known} \neq \text{World as it exists independently of our knowledge}.World as known=World as it exists independently of our knowledge.

The two may be related, but the relation requires analysis.

A theory of scientific knowledge should explain how the first can provide reliable information about the second without assuming that our representations are identical to their targets.

## 11.4 The strongest open possibility

A deeper possibility is that consciousness is not merely an epistemic condition but an ontological feature of reality. This is a legitimate metaphysical hypothesis, but it requires more than the observation that all theories are understood by conscious agents.

A complete theory would need to specify the proposed constitutive role of consciousness, connect it to known physical phenomena, and explain how it improves on alternative accounts.

Until those tasks are performed, the claim should be treated as a research hypothesis rather than an established consequence of the foundations of mathematics.

# 12. The Problem of Measurement and the Role of the Observer

The relationship between consciousness and measurement deserves particular care because the word observer has different meanings in physics and ordinary language.

## 12.1 The operational meaning of observation

In experimental physics, an observation is ordinarily associated with an interaction that produces a record: a detector click, a photographic mark, a stored bit, or another physical outcome.

The record can subsequently be examined by a conscious observer, but the physical process that produces it need not be defined as a conscious act.

In quantum mechanics, measurement is represented through the relationship between the measured system, the apparatus, and the possible outcomes. The mathematical treatment depends on the measurement model and the interpretation adopted.

The formalism does not require that every recorded event be witnessed by a human mind at the instant it occurs.

## 12.2 The measurement problem

The quantum measurement problem concerns the relationship between quantum-state evolution, measurement interactions, and the definite outcomes experienced or recorded in experiments.

Different interpretations address this relationship differently. Some emphasize the role of environmental decoherence, some modify or supplement the dynamics, and others reinterpret the status of the quantum state or measurement outcome.

Decoherence explains important aspects of the suppression of observable interference between components of a system's reduced state. It does not, by itself, settle every aspect of the measurement problem, particularly the interpretation of individual outcomes.

The debate therefore remains significant.

However, the existence of this foundational problem does not establish that consciousness must cause the outcome. A consciousness-based account must offer a precise mechanism or interpretation and show what additional explanatory work it accomplishes.

## 12.3 A consciousness-dependent measurement hypothesis

Suppose one proposes that conscious experience plays a fundamental role in measurement. The hypothesis should be stated in a form that can be compared with alternatives.

Let MMM denote a physical measurement procedure and OOO its recorded outcome. A standard operational model specifies probabilities

P(O=o∣M,ρ),P(O=o\mid M,\rho),P(O=o∣M,ρ),

where ρ\rhoρ is the relevant quantum state.

A consciousness-dependent extension might introduce an additional variable CCC:

P(O=o∣M,ρ,C).P(O=o\mid M,\rho,C).P(O=o∣M,ρ,C).

This notation does not imply that such an extension is necessary or correct. It specifies what a proposed extension would need to address.

If CCC is claimed to have an experimentally relevant causal role, the theory must define it sufficiently to yield predictions that differ from those of the standard model. If all observable predictions remain identical, the extension may still have interpretive significance, but its additional ontological claims would not be established by those observations alone.

## 12.4 The observer's epistemic role

Even if consciousness has no special role in the physical dynamics of measurement, it remains essential to the interpretation of experimental evidence.

A detector record becomes evidence for a theory through calibration, statistical analysis, comparison with alternatives, and inferential judgment.

This role is not equivalent to physical causation. It is the role of the observer in establishing what the experiment supports.

The distinction allows us to acknowledge the importance of consciousness in scientific knowledge without prematurely assigning it a special place in quantum dynamics.

# 13. An Information-Theoretic Account: What Information Does and Does Not Explain

Information theory offers a mathematical vocabulary for describing correlations, uncertainty, communication, and the structure of physical states. It is often invoked in theories of consciousness and quantum foundations.

Its power should not be confused with unlimited explanatory scope.

## 13.3 Information is not automatically experience

Suppose a system contains a large amount of information, exhibits complex interactions, and integrates data from multiple sources. It does not follow solely from these properties that the system is conscious.

A theory may propose that particular forms of information processing are sufficient for consciousness. To establish this claim, it must specify the relevant organization and justify the connection between that organization and subjective experience.

The word information must not be used as a substitute for the phenomenon that the theory is supposed to explain.

## 13.4 The semantic problem

Physical signals can carry information in a statistical or operational sense without possessing meaning for every system through which they pass.

A receiver can transform signals according to fixed rules. Whether those transformations constitute semantic understanding depends on the account of meaning being adopted.

This distinction is central to the consciousness–mathematics problem. Mathematical expressions can be manipulated formally, but their interpretation as claims about reality requires a semantic and epistemic account.

It does not follow that only human consciousness can interpret mathematics. It follows that formal manipulation and semantic interpretation must be distinguished in any theory that invokes both.

# 14. A Principle of Explanatory Stratification

We now formulate the main methodological result of the paper.

## 14.1 Statement of the principle

Principle of Explanatory Stratification (PES).

When a theory connects conscious experience, mathematical structure, physical realization, and scientific interpretation, it must distinguish the explanatory claims made at each level and explicitly specify the relations by which conclusions at one level support claims at another.

The principle has four requirements.

1. Representational clarity: the mathematical object and the target phenomenon must be distinguished.

2. Realization clarity: the physical process that implements or instantiates the modeled behavior must be specified where relevant.

3. Interpretive clarity: the relation between formal expressions and their empirical or phenomenal meaning must be made explicit.

4. Ontological restraint: a claim that one domain constitutes another requires an argument beyond the success of a representation.

The principle is methodological. It does not establish that the four domains are ultimately independent or irreducible. It requires that their possible unification be demonstrated rather than presumed.

## 14.2 A criterion for cross-level inference

Let LiL_iLi and LjL_jLj denote two explanatory levels, and let RijR_{ij}Rij be a proposed bridge relation between them.

A claim qjq_jqj at level LjL_jLj is justified by evidence or a result qiq_iqi at level LiL_iLi only if the theory supplies a valid inference rule, bridge principle, or independently supported mapping connecting them.

Schematically,

qi∧Rij⊢qj.q_i\land R_{ij}\vdash q_j.qi∧Rij⊢qj.

Without RijR_{ij}Rij, the inference may be invalid even when qiq_iqi is correct.

For example:

* A neural correlation does not alone establish an identity between a neural state and a phenomenal state.

* A Hilbert-space representation does not alone establish that Hilbert space is a literal physical location.

* The conscious origin of mathematical notation does not alone establish that mathematical truth is created by consciousness.

* The predictive success of a mathematical model does not alone establish that reality is nothing but mathematics.

These are instances of the same structural error: transferring a conclusion across explanatory levels without an adequate bridge.

## 14.3 Corollary: mathematical success is not ontological uniqueness

Suppose a mathematical model MMM successfully predicts a set of empirical outcomes D\mathcal{D}D. Suppose another model M′M'M′ makes the same predictions over that domain.

Then the empirical success of MMM does not, by itself, establish that MMM is the unique ontologically correct description of the target.

Additional evidence might favor one model: novel predictions, better explanatory integration, fewer unsupported assumptions, or greater robustness. But these are additional criteria, not consequences of predictive success alone.

## 14.4 Corollary: epistemic dependence is not ontological creation

Suppose a mathematical theory can be known only through cognitive activities. It follows that mathematical knowledge depends on those activities in the relevant epistemic sense.

It does not follow that every mathematical structure known through those activities is constituted by them.

The conclusion requires a further premise establishing the ontological dependence of the structure itself.

## 14.5 Corollary: holistic representation is not universal metaphysical unity

Suppose a physical system cannot be adequately represented as a collection of independently specified subsystems. It follows that the relevant model must preserve the system's nonseparable structure.

It does not follow that all reality forms a single conscious entity or that every subsystem is directly connected to every other subsystem.

Such claims require independent definitions and arguments.

## 14.6 The role of the principle

The PES does not solve the consciousness problem by definition. Its contribution is to prevent several familiar philosophical conclusions from being treated as established when the corresponding bridge principles have not been supplied.

It also provides a constructive path forward: identify the domains, specify the mappings, derive the consequences, and determine which conclusions are warranted.


# 15. Objections and Replies

A foundational framework must be tested against its strongest objections. The following objections challenge the central proposal from physicalist, mathematical-realist, idealist, and methodological perspectives.

## 15.1 Objection I: Neuroscience will eventually explain consciousness

The objection states that present limitations are temporary. As neuroscience advances, a sufficiently complete theory of brain dynamics may explain all relevant features of consciousness. Introducing a separate level of intelligibility may unnecessarily complicate the problem.

Reply. The proposed framework does not deny that neuroscience may achieve a complete explanation. It requires the scope of that explanation to be specified.

If a theory derives the relevant phenomenal properties from physical and functional principles through adequate bridge principles, it would satisfy a stronger explanatory standard than a theory that merely predicts reports or identifies correlations.

The framework is therefore compatible with successful physical reduction. It rejects only the inference that such reduction has already been established by the success of physical modeling in general.

## 15.2 Objection II: Mathematics exists independently of consciousness

A mathematical realist may argue that mathematical structures are independent of human thought. Conscious mathematicians discover these structures rather than create them.

Reply. This possibility is compatible with the present analysis. The epistemic dependence of mathematical practice does not establish the ontological dependence of mathematical truth.

The framework distinguishes the existence of a structure, the formal characterization of that structure, and the conscious recognition of its properties. A realist may regard the first as independent of the third.

The burden on a consciousness-first theory is to explain why the realist interpretation is inadequate and to provide a more successful account of mathematical existence.

## 15.3 Objection III: The framework merely renames the explanatory gap

One might object that distinguishing formal, physical, and phenomenal levels does not solve the problem. It simply gives the unresolved difficulty a more elaborate vocabulary.

Reply. This objection is partly correct. The architecture is not itself a completed theory of consciousness.

Its contribution is diagnostic and methodological. It identifies where an explanation must do additional work, specifies which bridge principles are required, and prevents a result at one level from being mistaken for a result at another.

A framework that clarifies the conditions of a solution is not equivalent to the solution, but it can make subsequent research more rigorous.

## 15.4 Objection IV: All distinctions are themselves conceptual constructions

An idealist may argue that the distinction between consciousness, mathematics, and physical reality is made within consciousness. Consequently, it cannot establish an independent physical domain.

Reply. The premise that distinctions are formulated through conscious activity does not establish that their referents are constituted by that activity.

An investigator can formulate a distinction between a map and a territory without thereby creating the territory. The idealist may reject this analogy, but must then explain why the relationship between representation and represented object is fundamentally different.

The question is not whether conceptual distinctions are made by minds. It is what those distinctions warrant us in claiming about existence.

## 15.5 Objection V: If consciousness is not measurable, it is not scientific

A strict operationalist may insist that a phenomenon belongs to science only insofar as it can be connected to observable procedures and quantitative measurements.

Reply. Measurement is essential to empirical science, but measurement procedures are not identical to the entire meaning of a scientific concept.

Theoretical entities are often studied through indirect evidence, models, and inferential relationships. Consciousness research can similarly use reports, behavioral tasks, neural recordings, and experimental interventions.

The existence of first-person experience presents special methodological challenges, but it does not make every investigation of consciousness unscientific.

The requirement is operational discipline: proposed measures must be validated for the properties they are claimed to represent.

## 15.6 Objection VI: The theory is unfalsifiable

If consciousness is placed at the foundation of all intelligibility, any possible observation could be interpreted as confirming the theory because every observation is known through consciousness.

Reply. This objection identifies a genuine danger.

A purely epistemological thesis—that all empirical knowledge depends on cognitive and interpretive capacities—does not by itself yield a distinctive physical prediction. A metaphysical thesis that consciousness constitutes physical reality also need not yield unique predictions unless supplemented by a more specific model.

The framework must therefore separate two projects:

* a philosophical analysis of the conditions of knowledge;

* an empirical theory proposing a particular relationship between consciousness and physical phenomena.

The second project must identify possible observations that would count against its proposed mechanisms or distinctive predictions. A claim that makes no such distinctions may remain a philosophical position, but it should not be presented as an empirically confirmed physical theory.

# 16. A Research Program for Consciousness-First Foundations

The framework suggests a concrete program of research. The program is not a declaration that consciousness is already known to be fundamental. It is an attempt to specify how that possibility could be investigated without confusing philosophical motivation with demonstrated result.

## 16.1 Program A: Formal models of mathematical understanding

The first task is to model the cognitive activities involved in mathematical reasoning.

Let aaa denote an agent, TTT a formal theory, and ppp a proof. A cognitive model might seek to explain how the agent acquires the ability to verify the proof, identify relevant lemmas, detect errors, and transfer the result to new problems.

The key distinction is between modeling mathematical performance and explaining phenomenal experience. Success in the first domain is valuable but does not automatically settle the second.

## 16.3 Program C: The physical realization of mathematical structure

The third task is to study the relation between abstract state spaces and physical implementations.

For a quantum processor, the relevant questions include:

* Which mathematical states are physically preparable?

* Which transformations can be implemented with bounded resources?

* How do environmental interactions alter the intended transformation?

* What operational criteria establish that the mathematical model accurately describes the device?

* Which features of the formal representation remain invariant under changes of physical implementation?

A useful implementation study would compare a target transformation UUU with an experimentally reconstructed channel E\mathcal{E}E. One could quantify their difference using an appropriate operational metric, such as a channel distance or an experimentally estimated process fidelity.

This approach investigates the bridge between mathematical operations and physical dynamics without presupposing that the mathematical space is itself a physical location.

## 16.4 Program D: Testing consciousness-dependent physical hypotheses

If a theory proposes that consciousness changes physical outcomes, it must formulate a testable extension of the existing physical model.

Let H0H_0H0 be a baseline model and H1H_1H1 a consciousness-dependent alternative. Both must specify:

1. the experimental system;

2. the relevant physical and cognitive variables;

3. the procedure for measuring those variables;

4. the predicted outcome distributions;

5. the expected size of any difference;

6. the controls needed to exclude ordinary physical and statistical explanations.

The comparison should be based on preregistered predictions, adequate statistical power, independent replication, and transparent analysis.

If H1H_1H1 predicts the same distribution as H0H_0H0 under all relevant observations, the proposed extension cannot be established through those observations alone. If the models predict different outcomes, an experiment can in principle distinguish them, subject to practical limitations.

No consciousness-dependent physical effect should be claimed merely because quantum theory contains unresolved interpretive questions.

## 16.5 Program E: Comparative evaluation of ontologies

The final task is to compare candidate metaphysical accounts using explicit criteria.

These may include:

* internal coherence;

* consistency with established physical results;

* explanatory scope;

* compatibility with empirical evidence;

* parsimony;

* clarity of bridge principles;

* ability to generate novel, discriminating consequences.

No single criterion settles every metaphysical question. Nevertheless, a theory that cannot specify what it explains, how it relates to established science, or what distinguishes it from alternatives is less developed than one that can.

The objective is not to privilege physicalism or idealism in advance. It is to make their explanatory commitments comparable.

# 17. A Conditional Theorem About Explanatory Completeness

We can formulate a result that captures the logical core of the paper.

## 17.1 Definitions

Let XXX be an explanatory target, TTT a theory, and Q(X)\mathcal{Q}(X)Q(X) a specified collection of properties of XXX that the theory is intended to explain.

Let D(T)\mathcal{D}(T)D(T) denote the consequences derivable from TTT under its formal rules and stated assumptions.

A theory is formally adequate relative to Q(X)\mathcal{Q}(X)Q(X) if its formal consequences correctly represent the relevant target properties under an agreed interpretation.

A theory is explanatorily complete relative to Q(X)\mathcal{Q}(X)Q(X) if it additionally supplies adequate explanatory connections for every property included in the target collection, according to the adopted standard of explanation.

These definitions are relative to a specified target and explanatory standard. They do not presuppose that all properties of a phenomenon can be collected into a single formal theory.

## 17.2 Theorem

Theorem — Formal adequacy alone does not entail explanatory completeness.

Let TTT be a theory that is formally adequate for a specified collection of properties Q(X)\mathcal{Q}(X)Q(X). If the criteria for explanatory completeness include at least one explanatory connection not established by formal adequacy, then formal adequacy alone is insufficient to establish explanatory completeness.

Proof.

By definition, formal adequacy establishes that the theory's formal consequences correctly represent the specified properties under an interpretation. Explanatory completeness, under the stated criterion, additionally requires an explanatory connection that formal adequacy does not establish.

Consequently, the premises establish the representational condition but do not establish the additional explanatory condition. Therefore, explanatory completeness does not follow from formal adequacy alone. □\square□

## 17.3 Scope of the theorem

The theorem is logically modest. It does not establish that consciousness is nonphysical, that mathematical explanation must fail, or that a particular physical theory is incomplete.

Its conclusion follows from the distinction between representation and explanation. It applies to any scientific theory in which the criterion for explanation exceeds the criterion for accurate formal representation.

For consciousness research, the theorem implies that the predictive success of a mathematical model does not, without further argument, establish that the model explains every feature of subjective experience.

For quantum foundations, it implies that the predictive success of Hilbert-space quantum mechanics does not, without additional interpretive premises, settle every question about the ontology of quantum states.

For the philosophy of mathematics, it implies that the success of mathematical applications does not, by itself, settle whether mathematical structures are invented, discovered, or ontologically fundamental.

These applications share a common logical structure, but they remain distinct substantive problems.

# 18. What Would Count as Progress?

A foundational proposal becomes scientifically and philosophically useful when it identifies what would improve our understanding and what would force its revision.

The consciousness–mathematics framework admits several forms of progress.

## 18.1 Progress in mathematical foundations

Progress would include a more precise account of how mathematical structures, formal systems, and mathematical knowledge relate to one another.

A successful theory should distinguish the construction of a formal language from the truth of its consequences and should clarify whether its ontological claims go beyond the epistemic conditions of mathematical practice.

## 18.2 Progress in physics

Progress would include a clearer account of how mathematical state spaces represent physical processes and which elements of the representation correspond to physically distinguishable possibilities.

For quantum mechanics, this means developing the relationship between formal states, measurement procedures, physical implementation, and interpretation. It does not require treating Hilbert space as a literal spatial container.

## 18.3 Progress in consciousness research

Progress would include models that explain increasingly detailed aspects of conscious experience while clearly identifying the relationship between measured variables, functional capacities, and phenomenal properties.

A theory should be judged not merely by whether it assigns a number to consciousness, but by whether its definitions, evidence, and explanatory claims justify the interpretation of that number.

## 18.4 Progress in ontology

Progress would include a more explicit comparison between physicalist, idealist, dualist, panpsychist, neutral-monist, and structural-realist positions.

The comparison must address their respective explanatory burdens rather than allowing one position to inherit the successes of mathematics or physics without explaining the relation between its ontology and those successes.

## 18.5 A decisive methodological standard

The guiding question for every proposed advance is:

What has been established by derivation, what is supported by observation, what follows only under an additional assumption, and what remains conjectural?

A theory that maintains these distinctions is better positioned to make genuine progress than one that treats mathematical elegance as sufficient evidence for an ontological conclusion.


# 19. Limitations of the Present Framework

The framework developed here is intentionally more modest than a complete consciousness-first ontology. Its limitations are substantive and should be stated explicitly.

First, it does not provide a derivation of consciousness from a fundamental conscious field. No such field has been defined here with independently justified dynamics, and no physical evidence has been presented that would establish its existence.

Second, it does not prove that consciousness is irreducible to physical processes. The distinction between phenomenal and functional explanations identifies a question that competing theories must address; it does not establish in advance which theory will succeed.

Third, it does not derive the existence of mathematical structures from conscious experience. The formal architecture distinguishes mathematical existence from mathematical knowledge precisely because the relationship between them remains contested.

Fourth, it does not establish that the universe is globally nonseparable in every possible sense. Quantum entanglement and other relational structures demonstrate specific forms of nonseparability, but the unrestricted thesis that everything is internally connected requires additional definition and evidence.

Fifth, it does not establish that quantum computation occurs outside physical spacetime. Hilbert-space representations are central to quantum theory, but their use does not entail that physical operations take place in a separate, nonphysical realm.

Finally, the Principle of Explanatory Stratification is a methodological proposal, not a new physical law. Its value depends on whether applying it produces clearer theories, exposes invalid inferences, and helps researchers identify missing explanatory connections.

These limitations do not undermine the research program. They define its present boundary.

# 20. Discussion: Turning the Explanatory Hierarchy Around

The most important consequence of this investigation is a change in the order in which foundational questions are asked.

Ordinary scientific practice begins with observable phenomena, constructs mathematical models, and uses those models to explain physical behavior. This procedure is extraordinarily successful. It should not be abandoned.

But it is not the only legitimate order of inquiry.

We can also begin with the conditions under which a mathematical model becomes meaningful, ask how formal structures acquire empirical interpretation, and investigate whether consciousness can be explained completely by the same kinds of models that conscious investigators use.

This alternative order does not negate mathematical physics. It investigates its foundations.

The difference is subtle but decisive.

A theory of the universe might correctly predict the motion of every object within its domain. That achievement would not automatically explain why the theory's symbols represent those objects, how its terms acquire meaning, or whether its mathematical structure is ontologically fundamental.

Likewise, a theory of the brain might correctly predict every behavioral response associated with a conscious episode. Whether that achievement constitutes a complete explanation of subjective experience depends on the theory's account of the relationship between physical processes and phenomenal properties.

The issue is not that scientific descriptions are inherently inadequate. It is that their adequacy must be assessed relative to the phenomenon and explanatory task in question.

Neither move should be assumed without argument.

## 20.2 The universe and the structure of relations

The holistic perspective introduces a parallel distinction. A system may be described as a collection of objects, but its behavior may depend on relations that cannot be discarded without losing explanatory information.

This is not a reason to reject analysis into parts. It is a reason to recognize that decomposition is a modeling choice whose adequacy depends on the system.

In some domains, independent component descriptions are sufficient. In others, global constraints, interactions, or correlations are essential.

The relevant question is not whether parts exist, but whether the parts can be fully characterized independently of the relational structure in which they participate.

## 20.3 The status of mathematics

Mathematics occupies a distinctive position because it is simultaneously a formal discipline, a cognitive practice, and a means of representing physical regularities.

The formal consequences of a mathematical theory are constrained by its axioms and rules. Its application to physical reality requires additional interpretive and empirical connections. Its comprehension by an investigator requires cognitive capacities.

These statements are mutually compatible.

The unresolved question is whether these different roles can ultimately be unified within a single ontology or whether the distinctions reveal irreducible features of reality, knowledge, or experience.

The present analysis leaves that question open while specifying what a rigorous answer must address.

# 21. Conclusion: The Conditions Under Which Reality Becomes Intelligible

This paper has investigated the relationship between consciousness, mathematics, holistic reality, and the formal structures of quantum physics.

The analysis establishes several distinctions.

First, mathematical knowledge depends on cognitive activity, but this epistemic dependence does not establish that mathematical structures are ontologically created by consciousness.

Second, holistic and nonseparable systems can be represented rigorously. Quantum entanglement provides a precise example in which the state of a whole cannot generally be reduced to independent pure states of its subsystems. This result challenges unrestricted separability without establishing that the universe is a single conscious entity.

Third, quantum computation uses Hilbert-space mathematics to represent physical states and operations. The physical realization of those operations and the ontology of the mathematical representation are separate questions. The abstract character of Hilbert space does not imply that quantum computation occurs in a nonphysical realm.

Fourth, mathematical models can explain important aspects of consciousness without automatically establishing that every feature of subjective experience has been explained. Whether a complete reduction is possible remains a substantive question, not a conclusion that follows from the general success of mathematical physics.

Fifth, the proposed Principle of Explanatory Stratification provides a disciplined method for evaluating cross-level claims. It requires explicit relations between mathematical representation, physical realization, conscious understanding, and ontological interpretation.

The resulting position is neither a rejection of mathematics nor an assertion that consciousness has already been demonstrated to be the foundation of the universe. It is a demand for greater precision about what mathematical explanation accomplishes and what additional assumptions are needed to move from formal success to ontological conclusions.

The deepest question remains open:

What makes reality mathematically intelligible?\boxed{ \text{What makes reality mathematically intelligible?} }What makes reality mathematically intelligible?

This question has at least three possible dimensions. Reality may possess mathematical structure independently of consciousness. Conscious cognition may construct mathematical representations of a world whose existence does not depend on those representations. Or consciousness may have a more fundamental constitutive role in the relationship between mathematical structure and physical reality.

These possibilities cannot be distinguished by intuition alone. They require precise definitions, coherent theories, explicit bridge principles, and, wherever empirical differences are implied, discriminating tests.

The investigation therefore ends not with the claim that mathematics cannot explain consciousness, but with a more exact research question:

Can a mathematical theory account for the full phenomenon of conscious understanding, including the relationship between formal structure, physical realization, and the experience through which that structure becomes intelligible?

A complete answer would have to do more than reproduce observable correlations. It would have to specify the explanatory target, establish the relevant derivations, justify the connections between formal and phenomenal properties, and demonstrate why its proposed account is preferable to its alternatives.

Until then, the distinction between mathematical description and the conditions of intelligibility remains a foundational problem.

The reversal of explanatory priority is valuable not because it supplies an immediate answer, but because it reveals a question that mathematical success alone cannot settle.

# Appendix C. Open Problems

The framework identifies six major open problems for subsequent research.

1. Mathematical ontology: What conditions distinguish the existence of a mathematical structure from the formal description of that structure?

2. Phenomenal explanation: What would constitute a derivation or explanation of subjective experience rather than a prediction of its behavioral and neural correlates?

3. Physical realization: What is the most general rigorous account of the relationship between abstract state-space transformations and their physical implementations?

4. Holistic constitution: Under what conditions are global relations constitutive of a system rather than merely useful components of its description?

5. Consciousness-dependent physics: Can a precise theory assign consciousness a distinctive causal or constitutive role while producing empirically distinguishable consequences?

6. Explanatory unification: Can the epistemic, mathematical, physical, and phenomenal levels be unified within one coherent theory without reducing their distinctions to unexplained assumptions?

These problems provide a foundation for further work. Their solutions cannot be assumed from the philosophical motivation that generated them; each requires its own definitions, derivations, and evidential standards.

Final thesis. Mathematics may be extraordinarily successful at describing the structure of reality while leaving open the question of why that structure is intelligible to conscious investigators. Whether this gap reflects a temporary limitation of scientific knowledge or a fundamental feature of the relationship between consciousness and reality remains unresolved. The task is to turn that uncertainty into a precise, testable, and mathematically disciplined research program.
