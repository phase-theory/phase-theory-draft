# Operator Phase Topology II: Dynamical Superselection, Entanglement Barriers, and Topological Nonequilibrium Transitions

**Preprint — September 2026**

---

## Abstract

We derive physical consequences of the operator phase topology introduced in the companion framework. The central idea is that topology is induced not by static open sets but by admissible operator evolution with an associated dynamical cost. Here we show that this construction generates new physical structure. First, continuous observables in the operator phase topology obey exact superselection rules: phases related by zero-cost reachability are physically indistinguishable, and smooth invariants are precisely the Casimirs of the Lie algebra generated by the admissible Liouvillians. Second, quantum speed limits become topological lower bounds: the operator phase quasi-distance bounds Fubini–Study and Bures distances, and locality-weighted costs produce rigorous entanglement barriers. Third, dissipative dynamics induces a topological arrow of time through monotone relative entropy functionals. Fourth, tensor-product systems equipped with locality-weighted costs yield a topological version of Lieb–Robinson causality and a cost-theoretic scrambling bound. Finally, we define nonequilibrium topological phase transitions as singular changes in the topology or volume of reachable cones. In finite-dimensional quantum control, these transitions correspond to caustics or conjugate points of the endpoint map; in many-body systems they produce nonanalyticities in reachable-state densities and control susceptibilities. The result is a physical theory in which conservation laws, causality, irreversibility, entanglement generation, and dynamical criticality are reinterpreted as topological properties of operator evolution.

**Keywords:** operator phase topology, quantum control, dynamical superselection, entanglement barrier, quantum speed limit, Lieb–Robinson bound, nonequilibrium phase transition, Lindblad dynamics, sub-Riemannian geometry.

## 1. Introduction

The operator phase topology replaces the static axiomatization of topology by a dynamical primitive. Given a space \(P\) of operator phases — density operators, observables, channels, rays, or gauge-equivalence classes — together with admissible evolutions \(\Phi_u\) and a cost \(c(u)\), one defines the reachable cone

\[
R_\varepsilon(p)
=
\left\{
q\in P:
\exists u,\ c(u)<\varepsilon,\ \Phi_u(p)=q
\right\}.
\]

The family \(\{R_\varepsilon(p)\}_{\varepsilon>0}\) becomes the primitive neighborhood system. Open sets are then derived as subsets stable under sufficiently small admissible evolution.

The purpose of this paper is to show that this construction is not merely a reformulation of topology but a generator of new physical structure. The main results are as follows.

1. **Dynamical superselection.**  
   If two operator phases are mutually reachable at zero cost, then every continuous physical observable with values in a \(T_1\) space takes identical values on them. More generally, smooth functions invariant under all admissible evolutions are precisely the Casimir functions of the Lie algebra generated by the admissible superoperator vector fields.

2. **Topological quantum speed limits.**  
   The operator phase quasi-distance \(d^+(p,q)\), defined as the infimum of the cost required to evolve \(p\) into \(q\), is bounded below by standard quantum-information distances. For Hamiltonian control with operator-norm cost,
   \[
   d^+([\psi],[\phi])
   \ge
   d_{\mathrm{FS}}([\psi],[\phi]),
   \]
   and for mixed states,
   \[
   d^+(\rho,\sigma)
   \ge
   A_B(\rho,\sigma),
   \]
   where \(A_B\) is the Bures angle. Thus the operator phase topology contains intrinsic quantum speed limits.

3. **Entanglement barriers.**  
   If the admissible generators are local, the operator phase topology stratifies the state space into local-unitary entanglement classes. Adding weak entangling generators produces finite but cost-bounded transitions between strata. For two qubits with a controlled-phase or \(ZZ\) interaction, the minimal entangling cost needed to reach Schmidt angle \(\theta\) is
   \[
   c_{\mathrm{ent}}^{\min}(\theta)=\theta,
   \]
   and the cost of producing a Bell state is therefore
   \[
   c_{\mathrm{Bell}}=\frac{\pi}{4}.
   \]

4. **Topological irreversibility.**  
   For Lindbladian generators satisfying detailed balance with respect to a stationary state \(\tau\), relative entropy
   \[
   S(\rho\|\tau)=\operatorname{Tr}\rho(\log\rho-\log\tau)
   \]
   is a topological Lyapunov function. Forward reachability implies monotonic decrease of \(S(\rho\|\tau)\). The specialization preorder of the operator phase topology thereby acquires a thermodynamic arrow.

5. **Topological causality and scrambling.**  
   Locality-weighted costs induce topological light cones. A Lieb–Robinson-type estimate becomes a Lipschitz estimate for the projection of reachable cones onto distant subsystems. The cost required to scramble information across a connected \(N\)-site system scales at least as the graph diameter, giving
   \[
   c_{\mathrm{scr}}
   \gtrsim
   \frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{LR}}}\operatorname{diam}(\Lambda),
   \]
   where \(v_{\mathrm{LR}}\) is the Lieb–Robinson velocity.

6. **Topological nonequilibrium phase transitions.**  
   Let
   \[
   V_p(\varepsilon)=\mu(R_\varepsilon(p))
   \]
   be the reachable volume with respect to a natural measure \(\mu\). Singularities of \(V_p\), its derivative, or its large-\(N\) logarithmic density signal dynamical phase transitions. These occur when the endpoint map develops critical points, when reachable cones change homotopy type, or when new dynamical strata become accessible.

These results show that the operator phase topology is not merely a mathematical curiosity. It converts control constraints, locality, dissipation, and measurement into topological data, and those topological data in turn imply physical conservation laws, bounds, and phase structure.

## 2. Evolutionary Structures: Minimal Recap

Let \(P\) be an operator phase space. An evolutionary structure consists of:

1. a family of admissible evolutions
   \[
   \Phi_u:P\to P,
   \]
   indexed by controls \(u\);

2. a cost functional
   \[
   c(u)\in[0,\infty],
   \]
   satisfying identity and concatenation compatibility:
   \[
   c(\mathrm{id})=0,
   \qquad
   c(u\#v)\le c(u)+c(v).
   \]

The reachable cone is

\[
R_\varepsilon(p)
=
\left\{
q\in P:
\exists u,\ c(u)<\varepsilon,\ \Phi_u(p)=q
\right\}.
\]

It satisfies

\[
p\in R_\varepsilon(p),
\]

and

\[
q\in R_\delta(p),\ r\in R_\eta(q)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
r\in R_{\delta+\eta}(p).
\]

The induced operator phase topology is

\[
\tau_{\mathrm{OP}}
=
\left\{
U\subseteq P:
\forall p\in U,\ \exists\varepsilon>0,\ R_\varepsilon(p)\subseteq U
\right\}.
\]

The forward dynamical quasi-distance is

\[
d^+(p,q)
=
\inf\left\{
c(u):\Phi_u(p)=q
\right\},
\]

so that

\[
R_\varepsilon(p)
=
\{q\in P:d^+(p,q)<\varepsilon\}.
\]

The specialization preorder is

\[
p\preceq q
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
d^+(p,q)=0.
\]

Equivalently,

\[
p\preceq q
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
q\in \operatorname{cl}_{\mathrm{OP}}(\{p\})
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\text{every open neighborhood of }p\text{ contains }q.
\]

## 3. Dynamical Superselection and Topological Conservation Laws

### 3.1 Zero-cost indistinguishability

The first physical consequence is a topological superselection rule.

**Theorem 1 — Topological indistinguishability.**  
Let \(F:P\to Y\) be continuous with respect to \(\tau_{\mathrm{OP}}\), where \(Y\) is a \(T_1\) topological space. If

\[
d^+(p,q)=0,
\]

then

\[
F(p)=F(q).
\]

In particular, if both

\[
d^+(p,q)=0,
\qquad
d^+(q,p)=0,
\]

then \(p\) and \(q\) are physically indistinguishable by any \(\tau_{\mathrm{OP}}\)-continuous observable.

**Proof.**  
The condition \(d^+(p,q)=0\) means \(p\in\operatorname{cl}_{\mathrm{OP}}(\{q\})\). By continuity,

\[
F(p)\in \operatorname{cl}_Y(\{F(q)\}).
\]

If \(Y\) is \(T_1\), singletons are closed, hence

\[
\operatorname{cl}_Y(\{F(q)\})=\{F(q)\}.
\]

Therefore \(F(p)=F(q)\). ∎

This theorem has a direct operational interpretation: if an evolution from \(p\) to \(q\) can be performed at arbitrarily small dynamical cost, then no continuous measurement compatible with the operator phase topology can distinguish the two phases.

The Kolmogorov quotient

\[
P_{\mathrm{phys}}
=
P/\!\sim,
\qquad
p\sim q
\iff
d^+(p,q)=d^+(q,p)=0,
\]

is therefore the space of physically distinguishable operator phases.

---

### 3.2 Smooth invariants as Lie-algebraic Casimirs

Assume now that \(P\) is a smooth finite-dimensional manifold and that the admissible evolutions are generated by vector fields

\[
X_\alpha.
\]

In local coordinates \(x^A\) on \(P\),

\[
\dot x^A
=
u^\alpha X_\alpha^A(x).
\]

For an operator phase \(\rho^i{}_j\), the coordinates may be taken as the components \(\rho^i{}_j\), subject to Hermiticity, positivity, and trace constraints. The vector field induced by a superoperator \(\mathcal L_\alpha\) is

\[
X_\alpha(\rho)^i{}_j
=
\mathcal L_\alpha{}^i{}_j{}^k{}_l\,\rho^l{}_k.
\]

Let \(I:P\to\mathbb R\) be a smooth observable. If \(I\) is invariant under every admissible evolution, then

\[
X_\alpha I=0
\quad
\forall \alpha.
\]

But because controls may be switched and concatenated, invariance under finite evolutions requires invariance under the full Lie algebra generated by the \(X_\alpha\). Define

\[
\operatorname{Lie}(\{X_\alpha\})
=
\operatorname{span}
\left\{
X_{\alpha_1},
[X_{\alpha_1},X_{\alpha_2}],
[X_{\alpha_1},[X_{\alpha_2},X_{\alpha_3}]],
\dots
\right\}.
\]

Then \(I\) is a dynamical invariant if and only if

\[
X I=0
\quad
\forall X\in\operatorname{Lie}(\{X_\alpha\}).
\]

Such functions are the Casimir functions of the controlled distribution.

### 3.3 Hamiltonian dynamics and spectral conservation

Let \(P=\mathcal D(\mathcal H)\), and let all Hamiltonian Liouvillians be admissible:

\[
\mathcal L_H(\rho)
=
-i[H,\rho],
\qquad
H=H^\dagger.
\]

Let \(I:\mathcal D(\mathcal H)\to\mathbb R\) be smooth and invariant under all Hamiltonian evolutions. Define the gradient \(G^i{}_j\) by

\[
dI
=
G^i{}_j\,d\rho^j{}_i.
\]

Then invariance under every Hermitian \(H\) gives

\[
0
=
G^i{}_j
\left(
-iH^j{}_k\rho^k{}_i
+
i\rho^j{}_k H^k{}_i
\right).
\]

Since \(H\) is arbitrary, one obtains

\[
[\rho,G]=0,
\]

or in components,

\[
\rho^i{}_k G^k{}_j
-
G^i{}_k\rho^k{}_j
=
0.
\]

On the open dense subset of density operators with simple spectrum, this implies that \(G\) is a function of \(\rho\). Hence \(I\) is locally a spectral function:

\[
I(\rho)
=
f(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n),
\]

where \(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n\) are the eigenvalues of \(\rho\).

Thus the Hamiltonian operator phase topology enforces the physical conservation law:

> Under arbitrary Hamiltonian control, the spectrum of the density operator is a topological invariant.

This is not merely a statement about unitary invariance; it is a statement about which observables can be continuous with respect to the topology induced by Hamiltonian evolvability.

---

### 3.4 Local unitary dynamics and entanglement invariants

For a bipartite system

\[
\mathcal H=\mathcal H_A\otimes\mathcal H_B,
\]

suppose the admissible generators are local Hamiltonians

\[
H_A\otimes I,
\qquad
I\otimes H_B.
\]

The corresponding Liouvillians generate local unitary orbits

\[
\rho
\mapsto
(U_A\otimes U_B)\rho(U_A^\dagger\otimes U_B^\dagger).
\]

A smooth invariant \(I\) satisfies

\[
X_{H_A}I=0,
\qquad
X_{H_B}I=0
\]

for all local Hamiltonians. Consequently, \(I\) is a local-unitary invariant. For pure bipartite states, such invariants are functions of the Schmidt coefficients. If

\[
|\psi\rangle
=
\sum_{a=1}^r \sqrt{\lambda_a}\,|a\rangle_A|a\rangle_B,
\]

then every continuous observable in the local-Hamiltonian operator phase topology is ultimately constrained by the Schmidt spectrum \(\{\lambda_a\}\).

Thus the local operator phase topology stratifies the pure-state manifold into entanglement classes.

---

### 3.5 Quantitative selection rules

Let \(Q:P\to\mathbb R\) be a smooth quantity that is not exactly invariant but whose rate of change is bounded. Suppose the cost is the weighted \(\ell^1\) cost

\[
c(u)
=
\int_0^T
\sum_{\alpha} w_\alpha |u^\alpha(t)|\,dt,
\qquad
w_\alpha>0.
\]

Assume

\[
|X_\alpha Q(p)|
\le
K_\alpha
\]

uniformly on the relevant region of \(P\). Define

\[
K_Q
=
\sup_\alpha \frac{K_\alpha}{w_\alpha}.
\]

Then along any admissible trajectory,

\[
\left|\frac{d}{dt}Q(\rho(t))\right|
\le
\sum_\alpha |u^\alpha(t)|\,|X_\alpha Q|
\le
K_Q
\sum_\alpha w_\alpha |u^\alpha(t)|.
\]

Integrating gives

\[
|Q(q)-Q(p)|
\le
K_Q\,c(u).
\]

Therefore any control transforming \(p\) into \(q\) must satisfy

\[
c(u)
\ge
\frac{|Q(q)-Q(p)|}{K_Q}.
\]

This is a quantitative selection rule. If \(Q\) is exactly conserved, then \(K_Q=0\), and a change in \(Q\) is forbidden at any finite cost.

## 4. Topological Quantum Speed Limits

The operator phase quasi-distance is not merely an abstract control cost. It bounds standard quantum-information distances.

### 4.1 Pure-state Hamiltonian control

Let \(P=\mathbb P(\mathcal H)\), the projective Hilbert space. Let the admissible controls be Hamiltonians \(H(t)\) with cost

\[
c(H)
=
\int_0^T \|H(t)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,dt.
\]

The Schrödinger equation is

\[
\frac{d}{dt}|\psi(t)\rangle
=
-iH(t)|\psi(t)\rangle.
\]

The Fubini–Study line element is

\[
ds_{\mathrm{FS}}^2
=
\langle d\psi|d\psi\rangle
-
|\langle\psi|d\psi\rangle|^2.
\]

Along a Hamiltonian trajectory,

\[
\left(\frac{ds_{\mathrm{FS}}}{dt}\right)^2
=
\langle H^2\rangle_\psi
-
\langle H\rangle_\psi^2
=
(\Delta_\psi H)^2.
\]

Since

\[
\Delta_\psi H
\le
\|H\|_{\mathrm{op}},
\]

we obtain

\[
\frac{ds_{\mathrm{FS}}}{dt}
\le
\|H(t)\|_{\mathrm{op}}.
\]

Hence for any Hamiltonian control connecting \([\psi]\) to \([\phi]\),

\[
d_{\mathrm{FS}}([\psi],[\phi])
\le
\int_0^T \|H(t)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,dt
=
c(H).
\]

Taking the infimum over controls gives

\[
d^+_{\mathrm{OP}}([\psi],[\phi])
\ge
d_{\mathrm{FS}}([\psi],[\phi]).
\]

If all Hamiltonian directions are allowed and the cost is exactly operator norm, equality is achieved by a geodesic Hamiltonian. Therefore the operator phase topology recovers the Fubini–Study topology on projective Hilbert space.

---

### 4.2 Mixed states and the Bures angle

For mixed states, define the fidelity

\[
F(\rho,\sigma)
=
\left(
\operatorname{Tr}
\sqrt{
\sqrt{\rho}\,\sigma\,\sqrt{\rho}
}
\right)^2,
\]

and the Bures angle

\[
A_B(\rho,\sigma)
=
\arccos\sqrt{F(\rho,\sigma)}.
\]

Let \(\rho(t)=U(t)\rho(0)U(t)^\dagger\) be a Hamiltonian trajectory generated by \(H(t)\). Choose a purification \(|\Psi(0)\rangle\) of \(\rho(0)\), and evolve it by

\[
|\Psi(t)\rangle
=
U(t)\otimes I\,|\Psi(0)\rangle.
\]

The Fubini–Study distance between purifications bounds the Bures angle:

\[
A_B(\rho(0),\rho(T))
\le
d_{\mathrm{FS}}(|\Psi(0)\rangle,|\Psi(T)\rangle).
\]

But as above,

\[
d_{\mathrm{FS}}(|\Psi(0)\rangle,|\Psi(T)\rangle)
\le
\int_0^T \|H(t)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,dt.
\]

Therefore

\[
d^+_{\mathrm{OP}}(\rho,\sigma)
\ge
A_B(\rho,\sigma).
\]

Thus the operator phase topology automatically encodes a mixed-state quantum speed limit.

---

### 4.3 Physical consequence

If

\[
\varepsilon
<
A_B(\rho,\sigma),
\]

then

\[
\sigma\notin R_\varepsilon(\rho).
\]

Thus small evolutionary neighborhoods cannot contain states that are too distinguishable in the Bures sense. In this precise sense, quantum distinguishability is a topological obstruction to low-cost evolvability.

## 5. Entanglement Barriers and Entangling Cost

### 5.1 Local-unitary strata

Let

\[
\mathcal H=\mathcal H_A\otimes\mathcal H_B,
\]

and suppose only local Hamiltonians are admissible. The reachable set from a pure state \(|\psi\rangle\) is

\[
\mathcal O_{\mathrm{LU}}(\psi)
=
\left\{
(U_A\otimes U_B)|\psi\rangle:
U_A\in U(\mathcal H_A),\ U_B\in U(\mathcal H_B)
\right\}.
\]

The Schmidt coefficients

\[
\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_r
\]

are invariant. Therefore the operator phase topology decomposes the pure-state space into disconnected dynamical strata labeled by entanglement.

For a bipartite pure state, the entanglement entropy

\[
E(\psi)
=
-\operatorname{Tr}\rho_A\log\rho_A,
\qquad
\rho_A=\operatorname{Tr}_B|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|,
\]

is a dynamical invariant under local Hamiltonian evolution. Any local-unitary operator phase topology therefore satisfies

\[
E(q)=E(p)
\quad
\forall q\in R_\varepsilon(p).
\]

Thus entanglement is not merely conserved dynamically; it becomes a topological label.

---

### 5.2 Entangling generators and cost barriers

Now add an entangling Hamiltonian \(H_{\mathrm{ent}}\). Let the cost be

\[
c(u)
=
\int_0^T
\left(
\sum_{\alpha\in\mathrm{loc}} w_{\mathrm{loc}} |u^\alpha(t)|^2
+
w_{\mathrm{ent}} |v(t)|^2
\right)^{1/2}
dt,
\]

where \(v(t)\) controls the entangling generator. If an entanglement monotone \(Q\) satisfies

\[
|X_{\mathrm{ent}}Q|
\le
K_{\mathrm{ent}},
\]

and local generators satisfy

\[
X_{\mathrm{loc}}Q=0,
\]

then the selection-rule bound gives

\[
c_{\mathrm{ent}}(u)
\ge
\frac{|Q(q)-Q(p)|}{K_{\mathrm{ent}}}.
\]

Thus changing entanglement requires nonzero entangling cost. The entanglement barrier is topological: local evolutions cannot cross between strata, and entangling evolutions can do so only at a cost bounded below by the change in the relevant invariant.

---

### 5.3 Two-qubit example: minimal cost of a Bell state

Consider two qubits with local unitaries assigned negligible or separately accounted cost, and with the nonlocal generator

\[
H_{ZZ}
=
\sigma_z\otimes\sigma_z.
\]

The local-unitary equivalence classes of two-qubit pure states are parameterized by the Schmidt angle

\[
\theta\in\left[0,\frac{\pi}{4}\right],
\]

with representative

\[
|\psi_\theta\rangle
=
\cos\theta\,|00\rangle
+
\sin\theta\,|11\rangle.
\]

The entanglement entropy is

\[
E(\theta)
=
-\cos^2\theta\log\cos^2\theta
-\sin^2\theta\log\sin^2\theta.
\]

The maximally entangled Bell state corresponds to

\[
\theta=\frac{\pi}{4}.
\]

The canonical Cartan decomposition of two-qubit unitaries shows that the nonlocal content of

\[
U(t)=e^{-it\sigma_z\otimes\sigma_z}
\]

is measured by the canonical coordinate \(t\). Local unitaries can move states within a given local equivalence class, but changing the Schmidt angle requires accumulation of nonlocal canonical coordinate. For the \(ZZ\) interaction, one has

\[
\frac{d\theta}{dt}
=
1
\]

along an optimal local-equivalence gauge, up to the periodic identification of the Weyl chamber. Therefore the minimal entangling cost required to reach Schmidt angle \(\theta\) is

\[
c_{\mathrm{ent}}^{\min}(\theta)
=
\theta.
\]

In particular,

\[
c_{\mathrm{Bell}}
=
\frac{\pi}{4}.
\]

This gives a concrete physical prediction:

> In the operator phase topology generated by local unitaries plus a \(ZZ\) entangler of unit strength, the Bell state lies at topological cost \(\pi/4\) from any product state.

The set of product states and the set of maximally entangled states are therefore separated by a finite dynamical cost barrier.

## 6. Dissipation, Specialization, and the Topological Arrow of Time

### 6.1 Detailed-balance Lindbladians

Let the admissible generators include Lindbladian superoperators

\[
\mathcal L(\rho)
=
-i[H,\rho]
+
\sum_\mu
\left(
L_\mu\rho L_\mu^\dagger
-
\frac12\{L_\mu^\dagger L_\mu,\rho\}
\right).
\]

Suppose \(\mathcal L\) satisfies quantum detailed balance with respect to a stationary state \(\tau>0\). Then Spohn’s theorem gives

\[
\frac{d}{dt}
S(\rho(t)\|\tau)
\le
0,
\]

where

\[
S(\rho\|\tau)
=
\operatorname{Tr}\rho(\log\rho-\log\tau).
\]

For a controlled convex combination of such generators,

\[
\dot\rho
=
\sum_\alpha u^\alpha \mathcal L_\alpha(\rho),
\qquad
u^\alpha\ge 0,
\]

one has

\[
\frac{d}{dt}
S(\rho(t)\|\tau)
\le
0.
\]

Therefore if \(q\) is reachable from \(p\), then

\[
S(q\|\tau)
\le
S(p\|\tau).
\]

---

### 6.2 Topological Lyapunov function

The relative entropy \(S(\cdot\|\tau)\) is therefore a topological Lyapunov function for the forward operator phase topology.

Define the sublevel sets

\[
\mathcal S_s
=
\left\{
\rho\in P:
S(\rho\|\tau)\le s
\right\}.
\]

Then

\[
p\in \mathcal S_s,
\quad
q\in R_\varepsilon(p)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
q\in \mathcal S_s.
\]

Thus the sublevel sets are forward-invariant under admissible dissipative evolution.

In the specialization preorder,

\[
p\preceq q
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
S(q\|\tau)\le S(p\|\tau).
\]

The topology therefore encodes a thermodynamic direction. In reversible Hamiltonian dynamics, the specialization preorder is symmetric. In detailed-balance dissipative dynamics, it becomes asymmetric and aligns with entropy production.

---

### 6.3 Attractors and Kolmogorov reduction

Let \(\mathcal A\) be the attractor set of a dissipative semigroup. If every point in a basin \(B(\mathcal A)\) can be driven arbitrarily close to \(\mathcal A\) at arbitrarily small incremental cost after sufficiently long evolution, then the Kolmogorov quotient of the operator phase topology may collapse entire basins into single effective points.

This gives a topological description of relaxation:

> A dissipative phase is characterized not only by its stationary state but by the specialization order through which other phases collapse toward it.

The irreversible loss of distinguishability is encoded in the non-\(T_0\) structure of \(\tau_{\mathrm{OP}}\).

## 7. Locality-Weighted Topology, Causality, and Scrambling

### 7.1 Locality degree and weighted cost

Let the Hilbert space factorize over a lattice \(\Lambda\):

\[
\mathcal H
=
\bigotimes_{x\in\Lambda}\mathcal H_x.
\]

A generator \(\mathcal L_\alpha\) has support \(\operatorname{supp}(\alpha)\subseteq\Lambda\). Define its locality degree

\[
|\alpha|
=
|\operatorname{supp}(\alpha)|.
\]

A locality-sensitive cost is

\[
c_\lambda(u)
=
\int_0^T
\left(
\sum_\alpha
\lambda^{p|\alpha|}
|u^\alpha(t)|^p
\right)^{1/p}
dt,
\qquad
\lambda>1.
\]

Equivalently, in tensor components, a local superoperator acting on a region \(X\) has support constraints

\[
\mathcal L_\alpha
{}^{i_{\Lambda}}{}_{j_{\Lambda}}
{}^{k_{\Lambda}}{}_{l_{\Lambda}}
=
0
\]

unless the nontrivial index transformations occur inside \(X\).

Small-cost neighborhoods in this topology are generated predominantly by local transformations. Nonlocal transformations are topologically expensive.

---

### 7.2 Topological Lieb–Robinson bound

Let the admissible generators be local Hamiltonians with bounded norm:

\[
\|h_X\|\le J,
\]

supported on regions of bounded diameter. Let \(X,Y\subset\Lambda\) be separated by graph distance \(r\). Let \(R_\varepsilon^X(p)\) denote the set of states reachable from \(p\) by controls supported in \(X\) with total cost at most \(\varepsilon\).

A Lieb–Robinson estimate implies that for any local observable \(O_Y\) supported on \(Y\),

\[
\sup_{q\in R_\varepsilon^X(p)}
\left|
\operatorname{Tr}(O_Y q)
-
\operatorname{Tr}(O_Y p)
\right|
\le
C
\|O_Y\|
|X|
\varepsilon
\exp\!\left[-\mu(r-v_{\mathrm{LR}}\varepsilon)\right],
\]

for constants \(C,\mu>0\) and Lieb–Robinson velocity \(v_{\mathrm{LR}}\sim J\).

This is a topological Lipschitz estimate. It says that the projection of the reachable cone onto a distant subsystem is exponentially small until the cost exceeds the causal threshold.

Thus the operator phase topology contains a causal structure:

\[
\varepsilon
<
\frac{r}{v_{\mathrm{LR}}
\]

implies that distant reduced states cannot be significantly altered by low-cost local evolutions.

---

### 7.3 Scrambling cost

To produce an \(O(1)\) change in a subsystem separated by distance \(r\), the cost must satisfy

\[
\varepsilon
\gtrsim
\frac{r}{v_{\mathrm{LR}}}.
\]

For a connected system of diameter

\[
D=\operatorname{diam}(\Lambda),
\]

the minimal cost to influence all subsystems is therefore bounded by

\[
c_{\mathrm{scr}}
\gtrsim
\frac{D}{v_{\mathrm{LR}}}.
\]

On one-dimensional chains,

\[
D\sim N,
\]

so

\[
c_{\mathrm{scr}}\gtrsim N.
\]

On expander graphs or all-to-all coupled systems with finite effective velocity,

\[
D\sim \log N,
\]

so

\[
c_{\mathrm{scr}}\gtrsim \log N.
\]

Thus the topology of locality-weighted operator phase space yields a cost-theoretic scrambling bound.

## 8. Topological Nonequilibrium Phase Transitions

### 8.1 Reachable volume and density of reachable states

Assume \(P\) is a finite-dimensional smooth manifold equipped with a natural measure \(\mu\), for example the Hilbert–Schmidt volume on density matrices or the Liouville measure on a coadjoint orbit. Define the reachable volume

\[
V_p(\varepsilon)
=
\mu(R_\varepsilon(p)).
\]

Define the reachable density

\[
\Omega_p(\varepsilon)
=
\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}V_p(\varepsilon),
\]

where the derivative exists.

The quantity \(V_p(\varepsilon)\) measures the number or volume of operator phases accessible from \(p\) at cost below \(\varepsilon\). Its singularities define dynamical phase transitions.

---

### 8.2 Endpoint map and critical cost

Let the control space be finite-dimensional, and let

\[
F_p:u\mapsto \Phi_u(p)
\]

be the endpoint map. The reachable set at cost \(\varepsilon\) is the image of the cost ball

\[
B_\varepsilon
=
\{u:c(u)<\varepsilon\}
\]

under \(F_p\):

\[
R_\varepsilon(p)
=
F_p(B_\varepsilon).
\]

If \(F_p\) is a submersion on the cost sphere

\[
S_\varepsilon
=
\{u:c(u)=\varepsilon\},
\]

and if the boundary of \(F_p(B_\varepsilon)\) varies smoothly, then \(V_p(\varepsilon)\) is smooth near \(\varepsilon\).

A topological nonequilibrium transition can occur when one of the following happens:

1. **Critical endpoint.**  
   There exists \(u\in S_\varepsilon\) such that

   \[
   \operatorname{rank} dF_p(u)
   <
   \dim P.
   \]

   The endpoint map develops a caustic.

2. **Stratum entry.**  
   The reachable cone touches a new dynamical stratum, for example an entanglement class or isospectral orbit, that was previously inaccessible.

3. **Topology change.**  
   The homotopy type of \(R_\varepsilon(p)\) changes as \(\varepsilon\) passes through a critical value.

Define the critical cost

\[
\varepsilon_c(p)
=
\inf\left\{
\varepsilon>0:
R_\varepsilon(p)\text{ is not locally diffeomorphic to }R_{\varepsilon-\delta}(p)
\right\}.
\]

At \(\varepsilon_c(p)\), the reachable density \(\Omega_p\) or its derivatives may become singular.

---

### 8.3 Large-\(N\) dynamical free energy

For many-body systems, define a dynamical free energy

\[
f_p(\beta)
=
-\lim_{N\to\infty}
\frac{1}{N}
\log
\int e^{-\beta c(u)}
\delta(F_p(u)-q)\,du,
\]

or, more coarsely,

\[
f_p(\beta)
=
-\lim_{N\to\infty}
\frac{1}{N}
\log
\mu(R_\varepsilon(p)).
\]

Nonanalyticities of \(f_p(\beta)\) or of the Legendre-dual reachable entropy

\[
s_p(\varepsilon)
=
\lim_{N\to\infty}
\frac{1}{N}
\log V_p(\varepsilon)
\]

are topological nonequilibrium phase transitions. They are not caused by equilibrium thermodynamic ensembles but by singular changes in the geometry of dynamical accessibility.

---

### 8.4 Example: entanglement threshold in two qubits

Return to the two-qubit system with local controls and a \(ZZ\) entangler. Let the initial state be a product state, \(\theta=0\). The reachable Schmidt angle satisfies

\[
\theta
\le
c_{\mathrm{ent}}.
\]

The maximally entangled stratum is reached at

\[
\varepsilon_c
=
\frac{\pi}{4}.
\]

For \(\varepsilon<\pi/4\), the reachable cone does not contain maximally entangled states. At \(\varepsilon=\pi/4\), it first touches the Bell stratum. The closure of the reachable set undergoes a qualitative change. This is a minimal topological phase transition: the reachable cone acquires a new entanglement stratum at a sharp critical cost.

## 9. Measurement-Induced Topological Superselection

### 9.1 Zero-cost measurement channels

Let \(\Gamma\) be a completely positive trace-preserving map representing a nonselective measurement or dephasing channel:

\[
\Gamma(\rho)
=
\sum_a P_a\rho P_a,
\]

where \(\{P_a\}\) is a projective decomposition. Suppose \(\Gamma\) is assigned zero or arbitrarily small cost. Then for every \(\varepsilon>0\),

\[
\Gamma(\rho)\in R_\varepsilon(\rho).
\]

Hence

\[
d^+(\rho,\Gamma(\rho))=0.
\]

By the topological indistinguishability theorem, any \(\tau_{\mathrm{OP}}\)-continuous observable \(F\) with values in a \(T_1\) space satisfies

\[
F(\rho)=F(\Gamma(\rho)).
\]

Thus coherences removed by \(\Gamma\) are topologically unobservable.

---

### 9.2 Pointer basis as topological quotient

If \(\Gamma\) is a pinching channel in the basis \(\{|a\rangle\}\), then

\[
\Gamma(\rho)
=
\sum_a \rho_{aa}|a\rangle\langle a|.
\]

All density matrices with the same diagonal in the pointer basis are related to the same dephased state by zero-cost measurement collapse. Therefore they become indistinguishable in the Kolmogorov quotient whenever the reverse zero-cost relation is also present or the observable class is restricted to measurement-compatible observables.

The pointer basis is therefore selected not merely by environmental stability but by topological collapse:

> The measurement channel defines a quotient of operator phase space in which off-diagonal coherences are topologically erased.

This gives a topological interpretation of decoherence-induced superselection.

## 10. Physical Interpretation

The operator phase topology reinterprets several familiar physical notions.

### 10.1 Nearness as transformability

In ordinary topology, \(q\) is near \(p\) if both belong to a common small open set. In operator phase topology, \(q\) is near \(p\) if \(p\) can be transformed into \(q\) at small dynamical cost. Nearness is therefore operational and control-theoretic.

### 10.2 Open sets as stability certificates

A set \(U\) is open if, once a phase \(p\) lies in \(U\), all sufficiently cheap evolutions from \(p\) remain in \(U\). Openness is dynamical robustness.

### 10.3 Closure as dynamical adherence

A phase \(p\) lies in the closure of a set \(A\) if \(p\) can be evolved arbitrarily cheaply into \(A\). Closure is not passive accumulation but active accessibility.

### 10.4 Conservation laws as topological invariants

If a quantity cannot be changed by admissible evolution, it labels disconnected or stratified components of the topology. Conservation laws become topological separation principles.

### 10.5 Irreversibility as specialization order

Dissipative dynamics produces a non-symmetric specialization preorder. The topological direction records the physical arrow of relaxation.

### 10.6 Criticality as accessible-stratum change

Nonequilibrium phase transitions occur when the reachable cone changes topology: new strata become accessible, caustics form, or dynamical components merge.

---

## 11. Summary of Derived Physical Laws

The operator phase topology implies the following physical principles.

### Principle I — Topological indistinguishability

If

\[
d^+(p,q)=0,
\]

then no continuous physical observable can distinguish \(p\) from \(q\).

### Principle II — Casimir conservation

Smooth invariants of the admissible dynamics are the Casimirs of the Lie algebra generated by the superoperator vector fields

\[
X_\alpha(\rho)^i{}_j
=
\mathcal L_\alpha{}^i{}_j{}^k{}_l\,\rho^l{}_k.
\]

### Principle III — Topological speed limit

For Hamiltonian control,

\[
d^+_{\mathrm{OP}}
\ge
d_{\mathrm{FS}}
\]

on pure states and

\[
d^+_{\mathrm{OP}}
\ge
A_B
\]

on mixed states.

### Principle IV — Entanglement barrier

Local operator phase topology separates states into local-unitary entanglement strata. Entangling operations are required to move between strata, and their minimal cost is bounded below by the change in entanglement invariants.

For two qubits,

\[
c_{\mathrm{Bell}}=\frac{\pi}{4}
\]

under unit-strength \(ZZ\) entangling control.

### Principle V — Topological irreversibility

For detailed-balance Lindbladian dynamics,

\[
p\preceq q
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
S(q\|\tau)\le S(p\|\tau).
\]

Thus relative entropy is a topological Lyapunov function.

### Principle VI — Topological causality

Locality-weighted costs imply

\[
\sup_{q\in R_\varepsilon^X(p)}
\left|
\operatorname{Tr}(O_Y q)-\operatorname{Tr}(O_Y p)
\right|
\le
C
\|O_Y\|
|X|
\varepsilon
e^{-\mu(r-v_{\mathrm{LR}}\varepsilon)}.
\]

Distant regions cannot be altered below the causal cost threshold.

### Principle VII — Topological nonequilibrium transitions

Critical costs \(\varepsilon_c\) occur when the endpoint map develops critical points or when reachable cones acquire new strata. At such costs, reachable volumes, densities, or control susceptibilities become nonanalytic.

## 12. Conclusion

The operator phase topology converts dynamical accessibility into geometry and topology. Once this is done, several physical phenomena emerge as necessary structural consequences.

Conservation laws appear as topological invariants of the reachable foliation. Quantum speed limits appear as lower bounds on topological quasi-distances. Entanglement classes appear as dynamically separated strata. Dissipation appears as a specialization order and thermodynamic arrow. Locality appears as topological causality. Nonequilibrium criticality appears as singular change in the topology of reachable cones.

The essential shift is therefore not merely mathematical but physical:

> The topology of a physical phase space is not a static background. It is generated by what the system is allowed to do, and by the cost of doing it.

In operator phase topology, the primitive physical question is not:

\[
\text{What states are statically close?}
\]

but rather:

\[
\text{What phases can be reached from what phases, and at what dynamical cost?}
\]

The answer determines the topology, and the topology in turn determines the physical laws.
