# **Causal-Response Equivalence Classes (CREC)**

## **Operational Classification of Global Quantum States by Local Causal-Response Data**

**Dust LLC — Research Preprint**

## **Abstract**

Quantum-state reconstruction is commonly formulated as an inverse problem: given a family of measurement statistics, determine the state that generated them. Yet the uniqueness of reconstruction depends not merely on the number of measurements, but on the operational class of interventions available to an observer. A restricted family of local interventions may distinguish some global states while leaving other, physically distinct states operationally indistinguishable.

We formulate **Causal-Response Equivalence Classes (CREC)** as a classification of quantum states under equality of all response statistics generated by a specified family of allowed local interventions. The construction separates three objects that are frequently conflated: the global quantum state, its observable correlation structure, and the causal-response profile accessible to a restricted experimental agent.

Let \\(\mathcal H=\bigotimes\_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal H_i\\) be a finite-dimensional multipartite Hilbert space, and let \\(\mathfrak P\\) denote a specified class of admissible response experiments. Each experiment is represented by a quantum instrument or, more generally, a completely positive trace-preserving (CPTP) intervention followed by an outcome measurement. We define

\\[ \rho\sim_R\sigma \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad P\_\rho(y|p)=P\_\sigma(y|p) \quad \forall p\in\mathfrak P,\ \forall y. \\]

This relation partitions the candidate state space into response-equivalence classes. The central mathematical object is the kernel of the response map: the space of state perturbations invisible to every admissible experiment. We derive a finite-dimensional characterization in terms of the real operator span of the effective measurement effects, establish necessary and sufficient conditions for state-identifiability, and formulate lower bounds on the information that remains inaccessible under restricted local response experiments.

We further distinguish local response equivalence from equality of reduced states, equality of correlation tensors, equality of entanglement measures, and equality under arbitrary global tomography. These distinctions permit a precise investigation of which global relational properties are determined by local causal observations and which vary inside a single response class.

The framework does not assert that global quantum states are fundamentally unknowable. It establishes a conditional statement: **given a specified operational restriction, the accessible response profile determines only an equivalence class unless the response experiments are informationally complete.** The proposed research contribution is a classification theorem linking the intervention algebra, the response kernel, and the identifiability of global correlation invariants.

## **1. Introduction**

### **1.1 The inverse problem of quantum structure**

A quantum state is conventionally represented by a density operator

\\[ \rho\in\mathcal D(\mathcal H), \\]

where

\\[ \mathcal D(\mathcal H)= \left\\{ \rho\in\mathcal B(\mathcal H): \rho\geq0,\  \operatorname{Tr}\rho=1 \right\\}. \\]

The state determines probabilities for measurements and, together with a dynamical law, determines the statistics of subsequent experiments.

For a sufficiently rich family of measurements, the state can be reconstructed. For a restricted family, however, different states may produce identical statistics.

The distinction is fundamental. A measurement protocol does not reveal a state in the abstract; it reveals those features of the state to which its accessible operations are sensitive.

Suppose two multipartite states \\(\rho\\) and \\(\sigma\\) differ in their global correlations. If every admissible local intervention yields identical outcome statistics, then the observer operating within that intervention class has no operational means of distinguishing them.

This motivates the central question:

> What is the complete set of global quantum-state properties determined by a specified family of local causal-response experiments?

CREC treats this as a problem of mathematical classification.

### **1.2 From state reconstruction to response reconstruction**

Traditional tomography asks whether

\\[ \rho\mapsto\\{P\_\rho(y|p)\\}\_{p,y} \\]

is injective.

CREC asks a broader question. If this map is not injective, what exactly does it identify?

The answer is not necessarily a single state. It is an equivalence class

\\[ [\rho]\_R= \\{\sigma\in\mathcal D(\mathcal H):\sigma\sim_R\rho\\}. \\]

The class contains every candidate state compatible with the complete response record available to the specified observer.

The distinction between state and response class is not semantic. It determines whether a claim about reconstructed quantum structure is justified.

### **1.3 Research objectives**

This work develops five objectives:

1. Define causal-response equivalence for a specified intervention class.
2. Characterize the equivalence classes using operator-space methods.
3. Determine which correlation invariants are fixed by a response profile.
4. Establish quantitative bounds on unresolved global-state information.
5. Construct experimentally testable state families that share local response data but differ under joint measurements.

### **1.4 Scope and epistemic status**

The paper distinguishes:

- **Established mathematics:** quantum instruments, operational equivalence, informational completeness, operator duality, and finite-dimensional tomography.
- **Derived results:** response-kernel characterization, completeness conditions, dimension bounds, and correlation-invariant criteria under the definitions introduced here.
- **Proposed extensions:** causal-response complexity, intervention-relative correlation entropy, and stability measures.
- **Open conjectures:** universal scaling laws relating intervention locality, response-kernel dimension, and recoverable multipartite correlation order.

No claim is made that the CREC framework establishes a new ontology of quantum states or that operational indistinguishability implies physical identity.

## **2. Mathematical setting**

### **2.1 Composite quantum systems**

Let the physical system consist of \\(N\\) subsystems:

\\[ \mathcal H= \bigotimes\_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal H_i, \qquad \dim\mathcal H_i=d_i. \\]

The total Hilbert-space dimension is

\\[ D=\prod\_{i=1}^{N}d_i. \\]

A global state is an element of

\\[ \mathcal D(\mathcal H). \\]

The real vector space of Hermitian operators on \\(\mathcal H\\) has dimension \\(D^2\\). The affine space of trace-one Hermitian operators has dimension

\\[ D^2-1. \\]

The physically admissible state space is the positive semidefinite subset of this affine space.

### **2.2 Intervention regions**

Let

\\[ \mathcal I=\\{1,\ldots,N\\} \\]

denote the subsystem index set.

An intervention region is a subset

\\[ S\subseteq\mathcal I. \\]

An operation supported on \\(S\\) acts nontrivially only on

\\[ \mathcal H_S=\bigotimes\_{i\in S}\mathcal H_i. \\]

For a strictly local intervention on subsystem \\(i\\),

\\[ \mathcal E_i: \mathcal B(\mathcal H_i)\rightarrow \mathcal B(\mathcal H_i) \\]

is a CPTP map.

Its extension to the composite system is

\\[ \widetilde{\mathcal E}\_i = \mathcal E_i\otimes \operatorname{id}\_{\bar i}. \\]

For a sequence of interventions, the resulting channel is an ordered composition:

\\[ \mathcal E\_{p} = \mathcal E\_{p_m}^{(m)} \circ\cdots\circ \mathcal E\_{p_1}^{(1)}. \\]

The ordering is physically significant whenever the operations do not commute.

### **2.3 Response experiments**

A response experiment \\(p\\) consists of:

1. an admissible intervention protocol;
2. an evolution or channel following the intervention;
3. a measurement;
4. an outcome space.

For finite outcome spaces, write the experiment as a quantum instrument

\\[ \mathfrak I_p=\\{\mathcal I\_{p,y}\\}\_y, \\]

where each \\(\mathcal I\_{p,y}\\) is completely positive and

\\[ \sum_y\mathcal I\_{p,y} \\]

is trace preserving.

The probability of outcome \\(y\\) is

\\[ P\_\rho(y|p) = \operatorname{Tr}[\mathcal I\_{p,y}(\rho)]. \\]

Equivalently, in the Heisenberg picture,

\\[ P\_\rho(y|p) = \operatorname{Tr}[F\_{p,y}\rho], \\]

where

\\[ F\_{p,y} = \mathcal I\_{p,y}^{\dagger}(I) \\]

is the effective POVM effect.

Thus each admissible response experiment determines a family of linear functionals on the state space.

The entire CREC classification will follow from the structure of these functionals.

## **3. Definition of causal-response equivalence**

### **Definition 3.1 — Admissible response class**

Let

\\[ \mathfrak P \\]

be a specified family of physically admissible response experiments. The class may impose restrictions on:

- intervention locality;
- accessible subsystems;
- intervention duration;
- measurement support;
- communication between laboratories;
- allowed control Hamiltonians;
- temporal ordering;
- available ancillas;
- permitted joint measurements.

The specification of \\(\mathfrak P\\) is part of the physical problem. No response-equivalence statement is meaningful without it.

### **Definition 3.2 — Causal-response profile**

For a state \\(\rho\\), define

\\[ \mathscr R\_{\mathfrak P}(\rho) = \left( P\_\rho(y|p) \right)\_{p\in\mathfrak P,\ y\in\mathcal Y_p}. \\]

This is the complete statistical response profile of \\(\rho\\) relative to the admissible experiment class.

### **Definition 3.3 — CREC equivalence**

Two states \\(\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal D(\mathcal H)\\) are causally response-equivalent relative to \\(\mathfrak P\\) if and only if

\\[ \boxed{ \rho\sim_R\sigma \iff P\_\rho(y|p)=P\_\sigma(y|p) \quad \forall p\in\mathfrak P,\ \forall y\in\mathcal Y_p. } \\]

The equivalence class of \\(\rho\\) is

\\[ [\rho]\_R = \\{\sigma\in\mathcal D(\mathcal H): \sigma\sim_R\rho\\}. \\]

### **Proposition 3.1 — Equivalence relation**

For any fixed admissible experiment class \\(\mathfrak P\\), the relation \\(\sim_R\\) is an equivalence relation.

**Proof.**

Reflexivity follows because

\\[ P\_\rho(y|p)=P\_\rho(y|p). \\]

Symmetry follows because equality of real numbers is symmetric:

\\[ P\_\rho(y|p)=P\_\sigma(y|p) \Rightarrow P\_\sigma(y|p)=P\_\rho(y|p). \\]

Transitivity follows because

\\[ P\_\rho(y|p)=P\_\sigma(y|p) \quad\text{and}\quad P\_\sigma(y|p)=P\_\tau(y|p) \\]

imply

\\[ P\_\rho(y|p)=P\_\tau(y|p) \\]

for every admissible \\(p,y\\). Therefore,

\\[ \rho\sim_R\sigma,\quad \sigma\sim_R\tau \Rightarrow \rho\sim_R\tau. \\]

Hence \\(\sim_R\\) partitions the candidate state space into disjoint equivalence classes. \\(\square\\)

### **3.1 Why the experiment class must be fixed**

Suppose

\\[ \mathfrak P_1\subseteq\mathfrak P_2. \\]

Then \\(\mathfrak P_2\\) allows at least as many experiments as \\(\mathfrak P_1\\). Consequently,

\\[ \rho\sim\_{R,\mathfrak P_2}\sigma \Rightarrow \rho\sim\_{R,\mathfrak P_1}\sigma. \\]

The converse need not hold.

Increasing experimental capability can split existing equivalence classes but cannot merge them.

Thus CREC is intrinsically relative to an operational boundary.

## **4. The response operator and its kernel**

The central mathematical structure is obtained by representing the response experiments as a linear operator on Hermitian state perturbations.

### **4.1 Effective response effects**

Define the set of all effective effects:

\\[ \mathcal F\_{\mathfrak P} = \\{F\_{p,y}:p\in\mathfrak P,\ y\in\mathcal Y_p\\}. \\]

Let their real linear span be

\\[ \mathcal V_R = \operatorname{span}\_{\mathbb R} \mathcal F\_{\mathfrak P}. \\]

This is the response-observable space.

Every accessible response probability is determined by the expectation value of an element of \\(\mathcal V_R\\).

### **4.2 The response map**

Let

\\[ \operatorname{Herm}(\mathcal H) \\]

denote the real vector space of Hermitian operators.

Define the linear response map

\\[ \mathcal M_R: \operatorname{Herm}(\mathcal H) \rightarrow \mathbb R^{\mathfrak J}, \\]

where \\(\mathfrak J\\) indexes the pairs \\((p,y)\\), by

\\[ \left[\mathcal M_R(X)\right]\_{p,y} = \operatorname{Tr}(F\_{p,y}X). \\]

The map is defined on all Hermitian operators, although physical states occupy only the positive, trace-one subset.

### **Definition 4.1 — Response-invisible operator space**

The response kernel is

\\[ \boxed{ \mathcal N_R = \ker\mathcal M_R = \left\\{ X=X^\dagger: \operatorname{Tr}(F\_{p,y}X)=0 \quad\forall p,y \right\\}. } \\]

Using the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product,

\\[ \langle A,B\rangle\_{\mathrm{HS}} = \operatorname{Tr}(AB) \\]

for Hermitian \\(A,B\\), we obtain

\\[ \boxed{ \mathcal N_R=\mathcal V_R^\perp. } \\]

This is the central operator-space identity of CREC.

### **Theorem 4.1 — Exact response-kernel characterization**

For two density operators \\(\rho,\sigma\\),

\\[ \boxed{ \rho\sim_R\sigma \iff \rho-\sigma\in\mathcal N_R. } \\]

**Proof.**

By definition,

\\[ \rho\sim_R\sigma \\]

if and only if

\\[ \operatorname{Tr}(F\_{p,y}\rho) = \operatorname{Tr}(F\_{p,y}\sigma) \\]

for every admissible experiment and outcome.

Subtracting gives

\\[ \operatorname{Tr}[F\_{p,y}(\rho-\sigma)]=0 \\]

for every \\(p,y\\).

This is precisely the condition

\\[ \rho-\sigma\in\mathcal N_R. \\]

The implication is reversible, so the equivalence is exact. \\(\square\\)

### **4.3 Trace normalization**

Since every pair of density operators satisfies

\\[ \operatorname{Tr}(\rho-\sigma)=0, \\]

only the traceless portion of the response kernel contributes to differences between physical states.

Define

\\[ \mathcal T= \\{X=X^\dagger:\operatorname{Tr}X=0\\}. \\]

Then the physically relevant invisible directions are

\\[ \boxed{ \mathcal N_R^{(0)} = \mathcal N_R\cap\mathcal T. } \\]

Every CREC class is the intersection of the affine response fiber with the physical state space:

\\[ \boxed{ [\rho]\_R = (\rho+\mathcal N_R^{(0)}) \cap\mathcal D(\mathcal H). } \\]

This expression provides the basic geometric description of a causal-response equivalence class.

## **5. Completeness and identifiability**

The most important question is whether the response profile uniquely identifies the global state.

### **Definition 5.1 — Response informational completeness**

The experiment class \\(\mathfrak P\\) is informationally complete over \\(\mathcal D(\mathcal H)\\) if

\\[ \rho\sim_R\sigma \Rightarrow \rho=\sigma. \\]

### **Theorem 5.1 — Global-state identifiability criterion**

The following conditions are equivalent:

1. \\(\mathfrak P\\) is informationally complete over the full finite-dimensional state space.
2. \\(\mathcal N_R^{(0)}=\\{0\\}\\).
3. The response effects span the full Hermitian operator space:\
   \\[\
   \mathcal V_R=\operatorname{Herm}(\mathcal H).\
   \\]

**Proof.**

If \\(\mathcal N_R^{(0)}=\\{0\\}\\), Theorem 4.1 implies that response-equivalent density operators have zero difference, hence are identical.

Conversely, suppose there exists a nonzero

\\[ X\in\mathcal N_R^{(0)}. \\]

Choose a full-rank density operator \\(\rho_0\\), for example

\\[ \rho_0=\frac{I}{D}. \\]

Because \\(X\\) is Hermitian and traceless, there exists a sufficiently small \\(\epsilon>0\\) such that

\\[ \rho\_\pm=\frac{I}{D}\pm\epsilon X \\]

are positive semidefinite and trace one.

Since \\(X\in\mathcal N_R\\),

\\[ \rho\_+\sim_R\rho\_-. \\]

But \\(X\neq0\\), so

\\[ \rho\_+\neq\rho\_-. \\]

Therefore the experiment class is not informationally complete.

Finally, by finite-dimensional Hilbert–Schmidt duality,

\\[ \mathcal N_R=\mathcal V_R^\perp. \\]

The only invisible traceless direction is zero precisely when the response span contains every traceless Hermitian direction. Because the effects include normalized measurement structure and the identity lies in their span, this is equivalent to

\\[ \mathcal V_R=\operatorname{Herm}(\mathcal H). \\]

Thus all three conditions are equivalent. \\(\square\\)

### **5.1 Restricted-state identifiability**

Full informational completeness may be unnecessary if the candidate state family is constrained.

Let

\\[ \mathcal S\subseteq\mathcal D(\mathcal H) \\]

be a specified model class, such as pure states, Gaussian states, stabilizer states, or states satisfying a known symmetry.

The response map is identifiable over \\(\mathcal S\\) if

\\[ \mathcal M_R(\rho)=\mathcal M_R(\sigma), \quad \rho,\sigma\in\mathcal S \Rightarrow \rho=\sigma. \\]

Equivalently,

\\[ (\mathcal S-\mathcal S)\cap\mathcal N_R=\\{0\\}. \\]

This condition can hold even when the experiment class is not informationally complete over all density operators.

The distinction is important: a uniqueness theorem over a restricted state model must not be presented as unrestricted global tomography.

## **6. Dimension of the unresolved state space**

The response kernel determines how many independent Hermitian directions are invisible to the experiment class.

Let

\\[ r_R=\dim\mathcal V_R. \\]

Since

\\[ \dim\operatorname{Herm}(\mathcal H)=D^2, \\]

rank–nullity gives

\\[ \dim\mathcal N_R=D^2-r_R. \\]

If the identity belongs to \\(\mathcal V_R\\), as it does for any nonempty normalized POVM experiment, the response kernel is orthogonal to the identity and therefore consists entirely of traceless operators. In that case,

\\[ \boxed{ \dim\mathcal N_R^{(0)}=D^2-r_R. } \\]

The affine response fiber consequently has dimension \\(D^2-r_R\\) wherever its intersection with the state space contains a relative interior point.

### **Theorem 6.1 — Lower bound on unresolved global-state degrees of freedom**

For a finite-dimensional system with \\(D\\)-dimensional Hilbert space, if the accessible response-observable span has dimension \\(r_R\\), then at every full-rank state the local dimension of its response-equivalence class is

\\[ \boxed{ \dim\_{\mathrm{loc}}[\rho]\_R=D^2-r_R. } \\]

**Proof.**

At a full-rank density operator \\(\rho\\), positivity is stable under sufficiently small Hermitian perturbations. Every sufficiently small perturbation \\(X\in\mathcal N_R^{(0)}\\) produces another physical state \\(\rho+\epsilon X\\) with identical response data. Conversely, every response-equivalent state differs from \\(\rho\\) by an element of this kernel. The local class therefore has the dimension of the traceless response kernel. Since the identity is in \\(\mathcal V_R\\), the kernel is traceless, and rank–nullity yields the stated dimension. \\(\square\\)

This is an exact local dimension statement, not a universal count of distinguishable states. Positivity can truncate the affine fiber at the boundary of the density-operator space.

### **6.1 A normalized missing-information fraction**

A simple dimension-based diagnostic is

\\[ \boxed{ \mathfrak m_R = \frac{D^2-r_R}{D^2-1}. } \\]

It satisfies

\\[ 0\leq\mathfrak m_R\leq1. \\]

- \\(\mathfrak m_R=0\\): full informational completeness.
- \\(\mathfrak m_R=1\\): no nontrivial traceless state direction is resolved.

This quantity measures the fraction of the real affine state-space dimension that remains locally invisible. It is not a Shannon entropy, von Neumann entropy, or operational information measure in bits.

### **6.2 Finite response protocols**

For a finite experiment set

\\[ \mathfrak P=\\{p_1,\ldots,p_m\\}, \\]

one can construct a response matrix by expanding each effective effect in a Hermitian operator basis

\\[ \\{G\_\alpha\\}\_{\alpha=0}^{D^2-1}. \\]

Write

\\[ F\_{p,y}=\sum\_{\alpha} f\_{p,y,\alpha}G\_\alpha, \qquad \rho=\sum\_\alpha x\_\alpha G\_\alpha. \\]

Then

\\[ P\_\rho(y|p) = \sum\_{\alpha,\beta} f\_{p,y,\alpha}x\_\beta \operatorname{Tr}(G\_\alpha G\_\beta). \\]

In an orthonormal basis, the response matrix has rows given by the coefficient vectors \\(f\_{p,y,\alpha}\\). Its rank is \\(r_R\\), and its null space directly identifies the invisible operator directions.

For finite data, the exact rank should be supplemented by a singular-value analysis. Small singular values indicate directions that are formally identifiable but poorly conditioned.

## **7. Local response classes and correlation structure**

CREC becomes particularly significant in multipartite systems, where local measurements can access subsystem statistics without necessarily reconstructing the full global state.

### **7.1 Product-local measurements**

Suppose the admissible experiments consist only of local POVM measurements with effects

\\[ F\_{\mathbf a|\mathbf x} = E^{(1)}\_{a_1|x_1} \otimes\cdots\otimes E^{(N)}\_{a_N|x_N}. \\]

Here \\(x_i\\) labels the local measurement setting and \\(a_i\\) its outcome.

If every local subsystem can be measured in an informationally complete basis and all joint outcome probabilities are retained, the tensor products of the local effects span the global Hermitian operator space.

Therefore, unrestricted combinations of informationally complete local measurements, with joint recording of their outcomes, can perform global tomography without requiring a single entangling measurement device.

This point is essential. “Local measurements” alone do not imply incomplete global information.

The restriction that creates nontrivial CREC classes must be stated more carefully. Examples include:

- only one-party marginal statistics are accessible;
- only a fixed subset of local settings is available;
- joint outcomes cannot be compared or jointly recorded;
- only a restricted intervention sequence is allowed;
- measurements are limited to a particular operator algebra;
- only certain temporal response functions can be observed.

### **7.2 Marginal-only response experiments**

Consider the restricted class in which each experiment measures only a single subsystem and no joint outcome statistics are available.

The response data then determine only the one-body reduced states

\\[ \rho_i=\operatorname{Tr}\_{\bar i}\rho. \\]

The corresponding response equivalence is

\\[ \rho\sim_R\sigma \iff \rho_i=\sigma_i \quad\forall i. \\]

The global correlations can vary while all one-body marginals remain fixed.

For a bipartite system, the response map is

\\[ \mathscr R_1(\rho) = (\rho_A,\rho_B). \\]

Its kernel consists of Hermitian perturbations \\(X\\) satisfying

\\[ \operatorname{Tr}\_B X=0, \qquad \operatorname{Tr}\_A X=0. \\]

These conditions eliminate local-state changes but permit genuinely joint perturbations.

### **7.3 Operator-basis decomposition**

For \\(N\\) qubits, choose the Pauli basis

\\[ \sigma_0=I,\qquad \sigma_1=X,\qquad \sigma_2=Y,\qquad \sigma_3=Z. \\]

Any state admits the expansion

\\[ \rho= \frac{1}{2^N} \sum\_{\boldsymbol\alpha\in\\{0,1,2,3\\}^N} T\_{\boldsymbol\alpha} \bigotimes\_{i=1}^{N}\sigma\_{\alpha_i}, \\]

where

\\[ T\_{\boldsymbol\alpha} = \operatorname{Tr} \left[ \rho \bigotimes_i\sigma\_{\alpha_i} \right]. \\]

The coefficients with exactly one nonidentity Pauli operator describe one-body polarization. Coefficients with two or more nonidentity factors describe higher-order correlation structure.

If the accessible response class measures only one-body marginals, all coefficients of weight two or higher are invisible to that response profile.

For \\(N\\) qubits, the number of one-body response parameters is \\(3N\\), excluding normalization. The total number of independent state parameters is \\(4^N-1\\). Thus the number of locally invisible linear directions is

\\[ \boxed{ \dim\mathcal N_R^{(0)} = 4^N-1-3N. } \\]

This is a large degeneracy even for modest \\(N\\).

## **8. Explicit state families in the same CREC class**

We now construct examples in which local response data agree while global correlations differ.

### **8.1 Bell coherence versus classical correlation**

Consider two qubits \\(A,B\\). Define

\\[ |\Phi^+\rangle = \frac{|00\rangle+|11\rangle}{\sqrt2} \\]

and the Bell density operator

\\[ \rho\_{\Phi} = |\Phi^+\rangle\langle\Phi^+|. \\]

Compare it with the classically correlated state

\\[ \rho\_{\mathrm{cl}} = \frac12|00\rangle\langle00| + \frac12|11\rangle\langle11|. \\]

Both states have identical one-qubit marginals:

\\[ \operatorname{Tr}\_B\rho\_\Phi = \operatorname{Tr}\_B\rho\_{\mathrm{cl}} = \frac I2, \\]

\\[ \operatorname{Tr}\_A\rho\_\Phi = \operatorname{Tr}\_A\rho\_{\mathrm{cl}} = \frac I2. \\]

Therefore,

\\[ \rho\_\Phi\sim\_{R_1}\rho\_{\mathrm{cl}} \\]

under the one-body marginal response class.

Yet their correlation structures differ.

For the Bell state,

\\[ \langle X\otimes X\rangle\_{\rho\_\Phi}=1, \\]

whereas

\\[ \langle X\otimes X\rangle\_{\rho\_{\mathrm{cl}}}=0. \\]

The Bell state is entangled; the classical mixture is separable.

Their global purities also differ:

\\[ \operatorname{Tr}(\rho\_\Phi^2)=1, \\]

\\[ \operatorname{Tr}(\rho\_{\mathrm{cl}}^2)=\frac12. \\]

Thus a single local-response equivalence class contains states with different entanglement, global purity, and connected correlations.

### **8.2 GHZ phase family**

For \\(N\geq3\\), define

\\[ |\mathrm{GHZ}\_N^\pm\rangle = \frac{ |0\rangle^{\otimes N} \pm |1\rangle^{\otimes N} }{\sqrt2}. \\]

Let

\\[ \rho\_\pm= |\mathrm{GHZ}\_N^\pm\rangle \langle\mathrm{GHZ}\_N^\pm|. \\]

For every proper subsystem \\(S\subsetneq\\{1,\ldots,N\\}\\),

\\[ \boxed{ (\rho\_+)\_S=(\rho\_-)\_S. } \\]

The reduced states are

\\[ (\rho\_\pm)\_S = \frac12 \left( |0\rangle\langle0|^{\otimes |S|} + |1\rangle\langle1|^{\otimes |S|} \right). \\]

The relative phase is absent from every proper reduced state.

Nevertheless,

\\[ \langle X^{\otimes N}\rangle\_{\rho\_+}=+1, \\]

\\[ \langle X^{\otimes N}\rangle\_{\rho\_-}=-1. \\]

Hence the two states agree on all proper-subsystem reduced states but differ in an \\(N\\)-body correlation.

This example establishes a particularly sharp form of response incompleteness: even complete knowledge of every proper marginal need not determine the global state.

### **8.3 A continuous family with fixed marginals**

The GHZ construction admits a continuous generalization:

\\[ |\mathrm{GHZ}\_N(\theta)\rangle = \frac{ |0\rangle^{\otimes N} +e^{i\theta}|1\rangle^{\otimes N} }{\sqrt2}. \\]

For every proper subsystem \\(S\\),

\\[ \rho_S(\theta) = \frac12 \left( |0\rangle\langle0|^{\otimes |S|} + |1\rangle\langle1|^{\otimes |S|} \right), \\]

independent of \\(\theta\\).

But the global coherence is

\\[ \langle 0^{\otimes N}| \rho(\theta) |1^{\otimes N}\rangle = \frac12e^{-i\theta}. \\]

The phase can be reconstructed by an appropriate global correlation measurement, but not from the proper marginals alone.

The family therefore defines a continuous subset of one marginal-response equivalence class.

### **8.4 A classical counterpoint**

High-order response invisibility is not uniquely quantum.

Consider two classical binary variables with distributions

\\[ p\_+(x_1,\ldots,x_N) \quad\text{and}\quad p\_-(x_1,\ldots,x_N) \\]

that are uniform over even and odd parity strings, respectively.

Every proper marginal is uniform and identical between the two distributions, while the full parity observable distinguishes them.

Thus the existence of higher-order CREC classes is a general feature of incomplete marginal information, not by itself evidence for a specifically quantum mechanism.

The quantum-specific question concerns which invisible structures are constrained by positivity, entanglement, noncommutativity, and admissible quantum operations.

## **9. Which correlation invariants are fixed by a response profile?**

The central classification problem is not simply to find states that are indistinguishable. It is to determine which functions of the state remain constant throughout each equivalence class.

### **9.1 Response-identifiable functions**

Let

\\[ K:\mathcal D(\mathcal H)\rightarrow\mathbb R \\]

be a correlation functional.

**Definition 9.1 — Response identifiability**

The functional \\(K\\) is identifiable relative to \\(\mathfrak P\\) if

\\[ \rho\sim_R\sigma \Rightarrow K(\rho)=K(\sigma). \\]

Equivalently, \\(K\\) is constant on every CREC class.

This definition is independent of whether \\(K\\) is an entanglement measure, correlation function, purity, or another global-state property.

### **Theorem 9.1 — Exact criterion for response-identifiable linear observables**

Let

\\[ K_A(\rho)=\operatorname{Tr}(A\rho), \qquad A=A^\dagger. \\]

Then \\(K_A\\) is response-identifiable if and only if

\\[ \boxed{ A\in\mathcal V_R. } \\]

**Proof.**

If \\(A\in\mathcal V_R\\), then for any \\(\rho\sim_R\sigma\\),

\\[ \rho-\sigma\in\mathcal N_R =\mathcal V_R^\perp. \\]

Consequently,

\\[ \operatorname{Tr}[A(\rho-\sigma)]=0, \\]

and therefore

\\[ K_A(\rho)=K_A(\sigma). \\]

Conversely, suppose \\(A\notin\mathcal V_R\\). By orthogonal decomposition, there exists a nonzero component

\\[ A\_\perp\in\mathcal V_R^\perp \\]

such that

\\[ A=A\_\parallel+A\_\perp, \qquad A\_\parallel\in\mathcal V_R. \\]

Choose a full-rank reference state \\(\rho_0\\), and take sufficiently small \\(\epsilon\\) so that

\\[ \rho\_\pm=\rho_0\pm\epsilon A\_\perp \\]

are valid density operators after normalizing the perturbation to be traceless. Since \\(A\_\perp\in\mathcal N_R\\),

\\[ \rho\_+\sim_R\rho\_-. \\]

But

\\[ K_A(\rho\_+)-K_A(\rho\_-) = 2\epsilon\operatorname{Tr}(A A\_\perp) = 2\epsilon\\|A\_\perp\\|\_{\mathrm{HS}}^2 \neq0. \\]

Hence \\(K_A\\) is not response-identifiable. \\(\square\\)

### **9.2 Nonlinear correlation invariants**

For nonlinear functionals, membership in \\(\mathcal V_R\\) is not sufficient as a general test. Instead, the exact condition is constancy over the physical response fiber:

\\[ \boxed{ K\big|\_{(\rho+\mathcal N_R^{(0)})\cap\mathcal D(\mathcal H)} =\text{constant}. } \\]

This gives a direct classification procedure:

1. Construct the response kernel.
2. Determine the physical fiber through the candidate state.
3. Evaluate the proposed invariant over that fiber.
4. Classify it as identifiable if and only if it is constant.

For differentiable \\(K\\), a necessary local condition at an interior point is

\\[ \operatorname{Tr} \left[ \nabla K(\rho)X \right]=0 \quad \forall X\in\mathcal N_R^{(0)}. \\]

Equivalently,

\\[ \nabla K(\rho) \in \mathcal V_R \\]

up to the identity direction, which is irrelevant on the trace-one state space.

This is a local criterion. It does not by itself establish global constancy on a disconnected or boundary-truncated response fiber.

### **9.3 Correlation hierarchy**

For multipartite systems, correlation observables can be organized by operator support.

Let \\(\mathcal V^{(k)}\\) denote the span of all observables supported on at most \\(k\\) subsystems.

Then

\\[ \mathcal V^{(1)} \subseteq \mathcal V^{(2)} \subseteq\cdots\subseteq \mathcal V^{(N)}. \\]

If the response class spans exactly \\(\mathcal V^{(k)}\\), then all linear observables of support at most \\(k\\) are identifiable, while any observable with a component orthogonal to \\(\mathcal V^{(k)}\\) is not universally identifiable.

This provides a natural hierarchy of response completeness:

\\[ \text{one-body} \subset \text{two-body} \subset \cdots \subset \text{global}. \\]

The hierarchy is experimentally meaningful only when the allowed measurements and the recorded outcome statistics are specified precisely.

## **10. The local intervention algebra**

The previous sections characterize CREC through measurement effects. We now make explicit how intervention restrictions generate the accessible observable space.

### **10.1 Heisenberg-picture response**

Let an experiment apply a channel \\(\mathcal E_p\\), followed by a POVM effect \\(M\_{p,y}\\). Then

\\[ P\_\rho(y|p) = \operatorname{Tr} \left[ M\_{p,y}\mathcal E_p(\rho) \right]. \\]

Using the adjoint channel,

\\[ P\_\rho(y|p) = \operatorname{Tr} \left[ \mathcal E_p^\dagger(M\_{p,y})\rho \right]. \\]

Define the Heisenberg-evolved effect

\\[ F\_{p,y} = \mathcal E_p^\dagger(M\_{p,y}). \\]

The intervention class therefore generates an operator system

\\[ \mathcal O_R = \operatorname{span}\_{\mathbb R} \left\\{ \mathcal E_p^\dagger(M\_{p,y}) \right\\}. \\]

The response kernel is its orthogonal complement.

### **10.2 Intervention closure**

An experiment class may contain isolated operations or sequences of operations. To account for sequences, define the intervention closure as the smallest collection of channels containing the permitted elementary operations and closed under their allowed compositions.

If the elementary local channels are

\\[ \\{\mathcal E_i^{(a)}\\}, \\]

then admissible sequences generate effects of the form

\\[ F= \left( \mathcal E\_{i_1}^{(a_1)} \right)^\dagger \circ\cdots\circ \left( \mathcal E\_{i_m}^{(a_m)} \right)^\dagger(M). \\]

The operator span of these effects can be strictly larger than the span of the original measurement effects.

Consequently, interventions can reveal state features that passive measurement alone cannot access.

### **10.3 Intervention-relative equivalence**

Two states may be equivalent under passive observations but inequivalent under interventions:

\\[ \rho\sim\_{\mathrm{passive}}\sigma \\]

while

\\[ \rho\not\sim\_{\mathrm{interventional}}\sigma. \\]

This distinction is important because intervention experiments probe how a state responds to controlled perturbations, not merely what it looks like under an initial measurement.

### **10.4 A caution concerning local operations**

A local operation on subsystem \\(A\\) cannot, by itself, alter the unconditional reduced state of a distant subsystem \\(B\\) when the operation is trace preserving:

\\[ \operatorname{Tr}\_A[ (\mathcal E_A\otimes\operatorname{id}\_B)(\rho\_{AB}) ] = \rho_B. \\]

This no-signaling identity does not imply that all joint correlations remain unchanged.

A local operation can change joint statistics while preserving the distant marginal. Therefore, CREC must distinguish:

- local marginal responses;
- joint outcome responses;
- conditional responses after communicating outcomes;
- temporal responses following intervention sequences.

These are different experiment classes and generate different equivalence relations.

## **11. Quantifying unresolved global information**

The dimension of the response kernel measures the number of invisible linear directions. It does not fully describe the geometric size or statistical distinguishability of a physical equivalence class.

We therefore introduce complementary measures.

### **11.1 Response-fiber diameter**

Choose a metric \\(d\\) on the density-operator space. A natural choice is trace distance:

\\[ d\_{\mathrm{tr}}(\rho,\sigma) = \frac12\\|\rho-\sigma\\|\_1. \\]

Define the response-fiber diameter

\\[ \boxed{ \Delta_R(\rho) = \sup\_{\sigma\in[\rho]\_R} d\_{\mathrm{tr}}(\rho,\sigma). } \\]

This measures the greatest global-state separation compatible with the same exact response profile.

It satisfies

\\[ 0\leq\Delta_R(\rho)\leq1. \\]

If the experiment class is informationally complete,

\\[ \Delta_R(\rho)=0 \\]

for every state.

A large value indicates that the same response data are compatible with substantially different global states.

### **11.2 Correlation-invariant range**

For a bounded correlation functional \\(K\\), define

\\[ \boxed{ \operatorname{Var}\_R(K|\rho) = \sup\_{\sigma\in[\rho]\_R}K(\sigma) - \inf\_{\sigma\in[\rho]\_R}K(\sigma). } \\]

This is the response-class ambiguity of \\(K\\).

If

\\[ \operatorname{Var}\_R(K|\rho)=0, \\]

then \\(K\\) is fixed throughout that particular class.

If it is positive, the response profile fails to determine \\(K\\) at \\(\rho\\).

The distinction between local and global identifiability is important: a functional may be constant on one response class and variable on another.

### **11.3 A worst-case unresolved-correlation bound**

Let \\(\mathcal K\\) be a specified family of bounded correlation functionals. Define

\\[ \mathfrak A_R(\rho;\mathcal K) = \sup\_{K\in\mathcal K} \operatorname{Var}\_R(K|\rho). \\]

This is a family-dependent measure of unresolved correlation structure.

It is not universal until the family \\(\mathcal K\\) is specified. For example, one may choose:

- all two-body connected correlators;
- all multipartite Pauli correlators;
- entanglement witnesses with bounded operator norm;
- a finite set of experimentally relevant nonlocality witnesses.

### **11.4 Trace-distance bounds on observable uncertainty**

For any Hermitian observable \\(A\\),

\\[ \left| \operatorname{Tr}[A(\rho-\sigma)] \right| \leq \\|A\\|\_\infty\\|\rho-\sigma\\|\_1. \\]

Thus, for response-equivalent states,

\\[ \left| \langle A\rangle\_\rho-\langle A\rangle\_\sigma \right| \leq 2\\|A\\|\_\infty d\_{\mathrm{tr}}(\rho,\sigma). \\]

This yields a simple bound on the variation of any bounded observable over a CREC class:

\\[ \boxed{ \operatorname{Var}\_R(K_A|\rho) \leq 2\\|A\\|\_\infty\Delta_R(\rho). } \\]

The bound is general but need not be tight.

### **11.5 Finite-sample response indistinguishability**

Exact equivalence is an idealization. In a finite experiment, response distributions can differ while remaining statistically indistinguishable.

For a finite protocol set, define

\\[ d\_{\mathrm{resp}}(\rho,\sigma) = \sup\_{p\in\mathfrak P} \operatorname{TV} \left( P\_\rho(\cdot|p), P\_\sigma(\cdot|p) \right), \\]

where

\\[ \operatorname{TV}(P,Q) = \frac12\sum_y|P(y)-Q(y)|. \\]

Then

\\[ d\_{\mathrm{resp}}(\rho,\sigma)=0 \iff \rho\sim_R\sigma. \\]

For a tolerance \\(\epsilon\geq0\\), define approximate CREC equivalence:

\\[ \boxed{ \rho\sim_R^\epsilon\sigma \iff d\_{\mathrm{resp}}(\rho,\sigma)\leq\epsilon. } \\]

For \\(\epsilon>0\\), this is generally not an equivalence relation because the triangle inequality gives only

\\[ d\_{\mathrm{resp}}(\rho,\tau) \leq d\_{\mathrm{resp}}(\rho,\sigma) + d\_{\mathrm{resp}}(\sigma,\tau), \\]

so two \\(\epsilon\\)-close pairs can be \\(2\epsilon\\)-apart. Approximate indistinguishability should therefore be treated as a tolerance relation or represented by metric neighborhoods, rather than automatically called an equivalence relation.

## **12. A completeness theorem for correlation reconstruction**

We now formulate the principal classification result in a form suitable for a finite-dimensional experiment.

Let

\\[ \mathcal W\subseteq\operatorname{Herm}(\mathcal H) \\]

be the real span of a specified family of correlation observables. The corresponding correlation signature is

\\[ \mathscr C\_{\mathcal W}(\rho) = \left( \operatorname{Tr}(W\_\alpha\rho) \right)\_{\alpha}. \\]

### **Theorem 12.1 — Correlation identifiability theorem**

For a specified response class \\(\mathfrak P\\) and correlation-observable space \\(\mathcal W\\), the following are equivalent:

1. Every correlation observable in \\(\mathcal W\\) is constant on every CREC class.
2. Every difference of response-equivalent states is orthogonal to \\(\mathcal W\\):\
   \\[\
   \mathcal N_R^{(0)}\subseteq\mathcal W^\perp.\
   \\]
3. The correlation-observable space is contained in the response-observable space, modulo the identity:\
   \\[\
   \boxed{\
   \mathcal W\subseteq\mathcal V_R+\operatorname{span}\_{\mathbb R}{I}.\
   }\
   \\]

**Proof.**

Condition 1 states that for every \\(W\in\mathcal W\\) and every \\(\rho\sim_R\sigma\\),

\\[ \operatorname{Tr}[W(\rho-\sigma)]=0. \\]

By Theorem 4.1, the differences \\(\rho-\sigma\\) lie in the response kernel. At full-rank states, every sufficiently small traceless kernel direction is realized by a physical state difference. Hence the condition is equivalent to

\\[ \operatorname{Tr}(WX)=0 \quad \forall X\in\mathcal N_R^{(0)}. \\]

This is condition 2.

By Hilbert–Schmidt duality,

\\[ (\mathcal V_R^\perp)^\perp=\mathcal V_R \\]

in finite dimensions. The identity component does not affect expectations on differences of trace-one states. Therefore condition 2 is equivalent to condition 3. \\(\square\\)

### **12.1 Interpretation**

The theorem establishes a precise relationship:

> A linear correlation property is recoverable from the response profile exactly when its observable lies in the linear span of the accessible response observables, up to an irrelevant identity component.

This is stronger than saying that local measurements sometimes fail to reconstruct global states. It specifies the complete linear information content of a given response class.

### **12.2 Limits of the theorem**

The theorem concerns linear observables and exact response statistics in finite dimensions.

It does not by itself classify:

- arbitrary nonlinear functions of the state;
- infinite-dimensional systems with domain and convergence issues;
- unknown measurement devices;
- state-dependent restrictions on the intervention class;
- response equivalence under finite statistical precision;
- unmodeled environmental degrees of freedom.

These require additional analysis.

## **13. Multipartite response completeness and correlation order**

A natural question is how the maximum intervention support controls the order of correlations that can be reconstructed.

### **13.1 Support-filtered operator spaces**

For a subset \\(S\subseteq\mathcal I\\), let

\\[ \mathcal A_S = \operatorname{Herm}(\mathcal H_S) \otimes I\_{\bar S}. \\]

Define

\\[ \mathcal V^{(k)} = \operatorname{span}\_{\mathbb R} \left\\{ \mathcal A_S:|S|\leq k \right\\}. \\]

The sequence

\\[ \mathcal V^{(1)} \subseteq\mathcal V^{(2)} \subseteq\cdots \subseteq\mathcal V^{(N)} \\]

is a filtration of the global observable space.

For homogeneous \\(N\\)-qubit systems, the Pauli strings of support \\(j\\) number

\\[ \binom Nj3^j. \\]

Therefore,

\\[ \dim\mathcal V^{(k)} = 1+ \sum\_{j=1}^{k}\binom Nj3^j. \\]

The dimension of the orthogonal complement is

\\[ \boxed{ \dim(\mathcal V^{(k)})^\perp = \sum\_{j=k+1}^{N}\binom Nj3^j. } \\]

This counts operator directions of support greater than \\(k\\).

### **13.2 Theorem 13.1 — Support-order lower bound**

If all response effects have support on at most \\(k\\) subsystems, then no response protocol in that class can universally identify any linear observable with a nonzero component in the orthogonal complement of \\(\mathcal V^{(k)}\\).

**Proof.**

By hypothesis,

\\[ \mathcal V_R\subseteq\mathcal V^{(k)}. \\]

Consequently,

\\[ (\mathcal V^{(k)})^\perp \subseteq \mathcal V_R^\perp = \mathcal N_R. \\]

Any nonzero traceless operator in this subspace is invisible to all admissible responses. Theorem 9.1 then implies that any linear observable with a nonzero component in this invisible space cannot be universally response-identifiable. \\(\square\\)

### **13.3 Important qualification: intervention support is not always measurement support**

An operation may be local while the subsequent measurement is global. Similarly, local operations followed by a shared entangling evolution may generate effective global response effects.

Therefore the correct bound is determined by the support of the *effective Heisenberg-picture effects*, not merely by the spatial support of the initial control operation.

The theorem applies when the entire response process preserves the stated support restriction.

### **13.4 Operational depth**

To characterize sequential experiments, define the intervention depth \\(m\\) as the maximum number of elementary intervention stages.

The response space may be written

\\[ \mathcal V_R^{(\leq m)} = \operatorname{span} \left\\{ \mathcal E\_{p_m}^\dagger\circ\cdots\circ \mathcal E\_{p_1}^\dagger(M) \right\\}. \\]

The resulting spaces need not coincide with a simple support filtration. Their growth depends on the generated operator algebra and the physical dynamics between interventions.

A potentially fruitful problem is to determine when increasing intervention depth generates the full global operator algebra and when a nontrivial invariant commutant remains.

## **14. CREC and quantum nonlocality witnesses**

A state pair can be response-equivalent under one experiment class while differing under a nonlocality witness that is not included in that class.

The distinction is important because entanglement, Bell nonlocality, steering, and generic correlation are not interchangeable.

### **14.1 Bell witness**

For two parties with dichotomic observables \\(A_0,A_1\\) and \\(B_0,B_1\\), define

\\[ \mathcal B = A_0\otimes(B_0+B_1) + A_1\otimes(B_0-B_1). \\]

The CHSH value is

\\[ S(\rho)=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho\mathcal B). \\]

A local hidden-variable model satisfies

\\[ |S(\rho)|\leq2. \\]

Quantum theory permits values up to

\\[ 2\sqrt2. \\]

If a response class does not contain the measurement effects required to evaluate \\(\mathcal B\\), then the CHSH value may vary inside a CREC class.

### **14.2 Entanglement witnesses**

An entanglement witness is a Hermitian operator \\(W\\) satisfying

\\[ \operatorname{Tr}(W\sigma)\geq0 \\]

for all separable states \\(\sigma\\), while

\\[ \operatorname{Tr}(W\rho)<0 \\]

for at least one entangled state \\(\rho\\).

If \\(W\notin\mathcal V_R+\operatorname{span}\\{I\\}\\), its expectation need not be determined by the response profile.

The Bell-versus-classical example in Section 8 provides a simple illustration: one-body marginal data do not determine whether the state is entangled.

### **14.3 CREC does not identify a unique hierarchy of correlations**

Different correlation properties require different witnesses. A state may be:

- entangled but Bell-local;
- steerable but not Bell-nonlocal;
- correlated but separable;
- nontrivially correlated at high order while all proper marginals are identical.

Consequently, a complete CREC classification should not use a single scalar correlation measure as a proxy for all global relational structure.

The appropriate object is a family of observables or functionals whose identifiability can be tested independently.

## **15. Stability under experimental imperfections**

Exact response equivalence is a mathematical ideal. Actual experiments estimate probabilities with finite samples and imperfect controls.

### **15.1 Approximate response fibers**

For a measured response vector \\(r\\), define the compatible-state set

\\[ \mathcal F\_\epsilon(r) = \left\\{ \rho\in\mathcal D(\mathcal H): \\|\mathscr R\_{\mathfrak P}(\rho)-r\\|\leq\epsilon \right\\}. \\]

The norm should be chosen according to the data model. For independent categorical outcomes, a natural choice is a weighted sum of total-variation distances or a likelihood-based confidence region.

The diameter

\\[ \Delta\_\epsilon(r) = \sup\_{\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal F\_\epsilon(r)} d\_{\mathrm{tr}}(\rho,\sigma) \\]

measures the maximum state ambiguity consistent with the experimental precision.

### **15.2 Conditioning of the inverse response map**

In a finite-dimensional representation, let \\(M_R\\) be the response matrix restricted to the traceless Hermitian subspace.

If the smallest nonzero singular value is

\\[ s\_{\min}^{+}(M_R), \\]

then perturbations in the identifiable subspace are amplified by at most a factor proportional to

\\[ \frac{1}{s\_{\min}^{+}(M_R)}. \\]

A small singular value signals poor conditioning even if the response map is formally injective.

A well-conditioned experiment should therefore maximize not merely rank, but also the smallest nonzero singular value under an appropriate normalization of the measurement design.

### **15.3 Proposition 15.1 — Stability on the identifiable subspace**

Let \\(X\\) be a traceless Hermitian perturbation orthogonal to the exact response kernel, and suppose

\\[ \\|M_R X\\|\_2\leq\epsilon. \\]

Then

\\[ \boxed{ \\|X\\|\_2 \leq \frac{\epsilon}{s\_{\min}^{+}(M_R)}. } \\]

**Proof.**

Expand \\(X\\) in the right-singular-vector basis of \\(M_R\\). Since \\(X\\) is orthogonal to the kernel, only singular vectors with nonzero singular values contribute. The smallest such singular value bounds the response norm from below:

\\[ \\|M_R X\\|\_2 \geq s\_{\min}^{+}(M_R)\\|X\\|\_2. \\]

Rearranging gives the result. \\(\square\\)

This result does not constrain components lying in the exact kernel. Those directions remain invisible even with arbitrarily precise data from the same experiment class.

### **15.4 Calibration and model error**

A further distinction is required between:

1. uncertainty due to finite samples;
2. uncertainty in the implemented measurement effects;
3. uncertainty in the assumed dynamical model;
4. genuine response-kernel degeneracy.

Only the fourth is intrinsic to the chosen idealized experiment class. The first three may be reduced by better calibration, improved statistics, or model refinement.

A robust CREC experiment must quantify all four separately.

## **16. Relation to established frameworks**

CREC is situated at the intersection of several mature areas. Its novelty cannot be established merely by renaming their existing constructions.

### **16.1 Quantum tomography**

Quantum tomography reconstructs states from measurement statistics. Informational completeness is exactly the injectivity of the measurement map.

CREC generalizes the perspective by treating the non-injective case as a structured classification problem and asking which correlation invariants survive within the resulting fibers.

The response-kernel theorem is a direct operator-space formulation of this familiar linear inverse problem.

### **16.2 Operational equivalence**

Operational approaches to quantum theory identify preparations, transformations, or measurements that cannot be distinguished by a specified operational theory.

CREC is a state-focused, intervention-relative specialization. Its defining feature is the explicit analysis of the global correlation structure left unresolved by local causal-response experiments.

The equivalence relation itself is not new.

### **16.3 Quantum marginal problem**

The quantum marginal problem asks which collections of reduced states are compatible with a global state and whether the global state is uniquely determined.

CREC includes this situation when the response class consists of marginal measurements. It broadens the question to arbitrary intervention-generated response profiles, including sequential experiments and restricted causal control.

### **16.4 Quantum error correction**

Quantum error correction studies information encoded in subspaces or subsystems that is inaccessible to specified noise or measurement processes.

There is a structural analogy: both frameworks use operator algebras and invisible directions. But CREC does not assume an encoded logical subsystem, a noise channel, or a recovery map. Its object is the classification of all physical states that share a response profile.

### **16.5 No-signaling and causal models**

No-signaling constrains how marginal outcome distributions depend on remote measurement choices. CREC concerns equality of complete response profiles under a specified class of interventions.

No-signaling is therefore neither equivalent to CREC nor sufficient to establish a nontrivial CREC degeneracy. A system can satisfy no-signaling while its global state remains fully reconstructible through sufficiently rich joint measurement statistics.

### **16.6 Process tomography and quantum combs**

For multi-time experiments, a state alone may not describe the operational object being reconstructed. The relevant object can be a quantum process, channel, or quantum comb.

CREC can be extended by replacing candidate states with candidate processes and defining equivalence through equality of all admissible multi-time response statistics.

This extension is mathematically natural but requires a separate treatment of causal ordering, memory, and process normalization.

## **17. An experimental programme**

A decisive CREC test must demonstrate three things:

1. a well-defined restriction on admissible response experiments;
2. two or more candidate states that are indistinguishable within that restriction;
3. a global observable or expanded experiment class that distinguishes them.

### **17.1 Two-qubit benchmark**

Prepare the states

\\[ \rho\_\Phi=|\Phi^+\rangle\langle\Phi^+| \\]

and

\\[ \rho\_{\mathrm{cl}} = \frac12(|00\rangle\langle00| + |11\rangle\langle11|). \\]

Perform local single-subsystem tomography independently on each qubit.

The predicted marginals are identical:

\\[ \rho_A=\rho_B=I/2. \\]

Then add joint correlation measurements:

\\[ XX,\quad YY,\quad ZZ. \\]

For the Bell state,

\\[ \langle XX\rangle=1,\qquad \langle YY\rangle=-1,\qquad \langle ZZ\rangle=1. \\]

For the classical mixture,

\\[ \langle XX\rangle=0,\qquad \langle YY\rangle=0,\qquad \langle ZZ\rangle=1. \\]

The extended protocol splits the original response class.

This is a controlled benchmark for verifying the relationship between response-space enlargement and equivalence-class refinement.

### **17.2 Multipartite GHZ benchmark**

Prepare

\\[ |\mathrm{GHZ}\_N(\theta)\rangle = \frac{|0\rangle^{\otimes N} +e^{i\theta}|1\rangle^{\otimes N}}{\sqrt2}. \\]

Reconstruct all proper reduced states and compare different values of \\(\theta\\).

The predicted result is:

- all proper marginals agree;
- the global state varies with \\(\theta\\);
- an appropriate \\(N\\)-body coherence measurement distinguishes the states.

The benchmark directly tests high-order information missing from lower-order marginals.

### **17.4 Required measurement sequence**

A robust protocol should include:

**Stage A — Define the operational boundary.**

Specify exactly which interventions and measurements are permitted. Record whether joint outcomes can be compared, whether classical communication is allowed, and whether postselection is included.

**Stage B — Characterize the response space.**

Reconstruct the effective response effects and determine their operator span. Estimate its rank and singular-value spectrum.

**Stage C — Prepare candidate state families.**

Use analytically known states, randomized state preparation, and independently characterized reference states.

**Stage D — Test response equivalence.**

Compare all statistics accessible under the restricted experiment class. Use confidence regions or likelihood-ratio tests rather than interpreting a failure to reject a difference as proof of exact equality.

**Stage E — Test global distinguishability.**

Add joint-state tomography or a carefully selected witness outside the original response class.

**Stage F — Expand the intervention class.**

Add new interventions systematically and determine which equivalence classes split.

### **17.6 Statistical criterion**

For each candidate pair \\(\rho,\sigma\\), estimate

\\[ \widehat d\_{\mathrm{resp}}(\rho,\sigma) = \sup\_{p\in\mathfrak P} \operatorname{TV} \left( \widehat P\_\rho(\cdot|p), \widehat P\_\sigma(\cdot|p) \right). \\]

The experiment should report a confidence interval for this quantity.

A successful approximate CREC demonstration requires:

- an upper confidence bound below a predetermined equivalence tolerance for the restricted response class;
- a statistically significant difference under an expanded measurement or intervention class;
- independent validation that the distinguishing observable was excluded from the original class.

This avoids the common error of equating insufficient statistical power with operational equivalence.

## **18. Falsifiability and failure conditions**

CREC is a mathematical framework, but any proposed physical application must be exposed to falsification.

### **18.1 Failure of the claimed equivalence**

A proposed pair of states fails the CREC test if an admissible experiment is found such that

\\[ P\_\rho(y|p)\neq P\_\sigma(y|p). \\]

A single verified distinguishing response invalidates the claim that the states belong to the same class.

### **18.2 Failure of the proposed completeness theorem**

A proposed classification theorem fails if it claims that an observable is determined by the response profile while that observable has a nonzero variation over the corresponding response fiber.

For linear observables, Theorem 9.1 provides a direct algebraic test.

### **18.3 Failure of a proposed lower bound**

A dimension or information lower bound must be checked against the actual response span, including all effective observables generated by permitted intervention sequences.

Counting only the initially available measurement settings can underestimate the accessible operator space.

### **18.4 Failure of physical interpretation**

A nontrivial response kernel does not establish that global information is fundamentally inaccessible. If additional physically permitted interventions distinguish the states, the degeneracy is relative to the original operational restriction.

Similarly, a state pair with identical marginals but different entanglement does not establish that entanglement is causally disconnected from all local observations.

### **18.5 Failure of experimental reproducibility**

A claimed CREC class must survive independent state preparation, calibration, and measurement reconstruction. Apparent equivalence caused by drift, detector inefficiency, state-preparation error, or insufficient statistical power is not a genuine mathematical degeneracy.

## **19. Novelty assessment**

### **19.1 Established components**

The following ingredients are established:

- operational equivalence relations;
- quantum measurement statistics and POVMs;
- informationally complete tomography;
- quantum marginal reconstruction;
- operator-space kernels and orthogonal complements;
- multipartite correlation hierarchies;
- Bell, steering, and entanglement witnesses;
- process tomography and intervention-based characterization.

The definition of CREC is therefore not, by itself, a new mathematical discovery.

### **19.2 Candidate original contribution**

The strongest candidate contribution is the unified classification of global correlation information by an intervention-generated response operator space.

The principal theorem is:

\\[ \boxed{ \text{A linear global-state property is identifiable} \iff \text{its observable lies in the accessible response span} } \\]

with the identity direction factored out on the trace-one state space.

This is an exact completeness criterion for linear correlation observables under a specified intervention class.

The associated response-kernel dimension gives a rigorous lower bound on the unresolved affine degrees of freedom.

However, both results are closely connected to standard linear inverse-problem and quantum-tomography theory. Their novelty as a standalone mathematical contribution remains limited unless the intervention-relative classification produces additional structure or new results beyond this operator-space formulation.

### **19.3 Where genuine novelty could emerge**

Five research directions offer a stronger opportunity.

**A. Intervention-algebra classification theorem**

Characterize the response-observable algebra generated by a restricted family of local interventions and determine its commutant, invariant subspaces, and irreducible components.

A theorem connecting the algebraic structure of admissible interventions to the complete classification of global correlation invariants would be a substantive extension.

**B. Sharp physical-fiber dimension bounds**

The linear kernel dimension gives a local dimension at full-rank states. A stronger result would characterize the global geometry of

\\[ (\rho+\mathcal N_R^{(0)}) \cap\mathcal D(\mathcal H) \\]

for pure states, low-rank states, symmetry-constrained states, and states with specified entanglement structure.

**C. Intervention-depth completeness**

Determine the minimum intervention depth or control complexity required to resolve a specified class of global correlations.

One possible quantity is

\\[ m\_{\min}(K) = \inf\left\\{ m: K\text{ is identifiable under depth-}m \text{ interventions} \right\\}. \\]

This would turn CREC into a theory of operational complexity for global-state reconstruction.

**D. Quantitative missing-correlation bounds**

Develop tight bounds on the maximum possible variation of selected correlation invariants within a response class:

\\[ \operatorname{Var}\_R(K|\rho). \\]

A useful theorem would connect this variation to the response kernel, state rank, subsystem dimensions, and experimentally accessible control resources.

**E. Multi-time causal-response equivalence**

Extend the classification from states to quantum processes, where response profiles encode temporal correlations, memory, and intervention ordering.

The corresponding equivalence classes could classify physically distinct processes that remain indistinguishable under a restricted family of causal interventions.

### **19.4 Novelty verdict**



Mathematically well-defined framework

Novelty not yet established



CREC is a coherent and technically useful framework for studying what restricted causal-response experiments identify about global quantum states.

Its exact response-kernel characterization is rigorous. Its correlation-identifiability theorem gives a clean necessary-and-sufficient condition for linear observables. Its dimension results quantify the unresolved state-space directions.

The framework should not yet be presented as a new theory of quantum causation or as a discovery that local observations cannot recover global quantum structure. Those broader claims would exceed the demonstrated results.

The strongest path toward a novel contribution is an intervention-algebra theorem or a sharp quantitative bound that cannot be reduced to ordinary informational completeness and the quantum marginal problem.

## **20. Principal conjectures and open problems**

### **Conjecture 20.1 — Intervention-algebra completeness**

For a finite-dimensional system, suppose the allowed intervention sequences generate an operator system \\(\mathcal O_R\\). The full set of linear state properties identifiable under those interventions is exactly the self-adjoint linear span of \\(\mathcal O_R\\), modulo normalization.

The linear statement follows from the response-space theorem. The unresolved part is a useful classification of the intervention-generated structure for physically meaningful classes of local controls.

### **Conjecture 20.2 — Correlation-order recovery threshold**

For a family of multipartite systems with fixed local dimension, there may exist a minimal intervention support or depth required to identify all correlation observables up to a specified order.

The threshold should depend on both the support of the accessible effects and the algebra generated by the permitted dynamics. It is not determined by intervention locality alone.

### **Conjecture 20.3 — State-rank dependence of CREC geometry**

The physical dimension and topology of response fibers may depend strongly on the rank and symmetry sector of the reference state.

In particular, full-rank states admit local affine kernel directions, whereas pure states lie on the boundary of the positive cone and may exhibit a substantially smaller physical fiber.

A rigorous stratification theorem would improve upon the linear dimension count.

### **Conjecture 20.4 — Operational reconstruction complexity**

For a selected family of global correlation observables \\(\mathcal W\\), the resources required for identification may be characterized by a complexity functional

\\[ \mathfrak C_R(\mathcal W) = \inf\_{\mathfrak P:\\, \mathcal W\subseteq\mathcal V_R+\mathbb RI} \operatorname{Cost}(\mathfrak P). \\]

Here \\(\operatorname{Cost}\\) could encode intervention depth, number of settings, control precision, spatial support, or communication resources.

This would distinguish information that is mathematically recoverable from information that is recoverable only at prohibitive experimental cost.

### **Open problem 20.5 — Infinite-dimensional response classes**

Extend the response-kernel classification to infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces, continuous-variable systems, and quantum fields.

The finite-dimensional operator-span argument must be replaced by appropriate topological closures, operator-domain conditions, and convergence assumptions.

### **Open problem 20.6 — Process-level equivalence**

Determine whether a useful process-level analogue of CREC admits a complete causal-order-sensitive classification under restricted interventions.

Such a theory must distinguish equality of output statistics for one initial state from equality of the underlying process for all admissible inputs.

## **21. Conclusion**

Causal-Response Equivalence Classes provide a rigorous language for distinguishing a quantum state from the information about that state recoverable through a specified family of experiments.

The fundamental construction is

\\[ \rho\sim_R\sigma \iff \mathscr R\_{\mathfrak P}(\rho) = \mathscr R\_{\mathfrak P}(\sigma). \\]

Its operator-space representation is

\\[ \boxed{ \rho\sim_R\sigma \iff \rho-\sigma\in\mathcal V_R^\perp. } \\]

This identity converts the reconstruction problem into a classification of observable and invisible directions in the space of Hermitian operators.

The resulting equivalence classes are physical-state fibers, not arbitrary mathematical groupings. Their geometry specifies precisely which state differences remain compatible with the same accessible response profile.

The central identifiability result is equally direct:

\\[ \boxed{ K_A(\rho)=\operatorname{Tr}(A\rho) \text{ is response-identifiable} \iff A\in\mathcal V_R } \\]

up to the irrelevant identity component.

This establishes a precise relationship between accessible causal-response data and recoverable linear correlation structure.

The Bell-state/classical-mixture example demonstrates that identical local marginals can conceal different entanglement and correlation structure. The GHZ family demonstrates that even complete knowledge of every proper subsystem marginal can leave a global coherence undetermined. Both examples are compatible with standard quantum theory and illustrate the operational restrictions that CREC is designed to classify.

The framework's most important limitation is also its defining discipline: equivalence is always relative to a declared experiment class. Adding measurements or interventions can refine the classes, and sufficiently rich experiments can eliminate them entirely through informational completeness.

Accordingly, CREC does not establish a universal barrier to global-state reconstruction. It provides a method for proving exactly what a particular family of causal-response experiments can reconstruct, what it cannot reconstruct, and how much unresolved global structure remains.

The next decisive mathematical step is to classify the operator algebras generated by physically constrained local intervention sequences and derive sharp state-dependent bounds on the geometry of their response fibers. That result would move CREC beyond a reformulation of tomography into a substantive theory of intervention-relative global-state identifiability.
