# The Spectral Cosmology Theory

## A Mathematical Framework for Cosmological Dynamics, Spectral Geometry, and Primordial Observables

Research status: Proposed theoretical framework with explicitly conditional results, a parameter-controlled prediction program, and a falsifiability protocol.

## Abstract

The Spectral Cosmology Theory (SCT) investigates whether observable properties of the universe can be derived from the spectrum of an effective operator whose coefficients and domain are determined by cosmological dynamics. Its central hypothesis is that the primordial perturbation spectrum, the evolution of cosmological modes, and selected geometric observables may be organized by a spectral structure that evolves with the expansion history of spacetime.

The hypothesis must be distinguished from the weaker claim that a spectrum can be fitted to cosmological data. A spectral parametrization becomes explanatory only when the underlying cosmological equations determine the operator, the operator determines its spectrum, and the resulting spectrum yields observable predictions without unconstrained adjustments at each scale.

This paper develops SCT as a research program grounded in established spectral theory, relativistic cosmology, quantum field theory in curved spacetime, and cosmological perturbation theory. It defines a cosmological operator family, derives the relation between its spectral data and the primordial curvature spectrum in a specified inflationary model, constructs a parameter-controlled oscillatory feature, and establishes conditions under which the feature is absent, suppressed, or falsified. The analysis separates exact mathematical results from model-dependent physical assumptions and conjectural extensions.

The principal result is a conditional derivation: if a cosmological background generates a specified time-dependent mode operator and its effective frequency contains a controlled, localized modulation, then the primordial power spectrum acquires a calculable scale-dependent feature. The amplitude, location, width, and phase of that feature are determined by the modulation parameters and the background evolution. This result does not establish that the universe actually possesses such a modulation. That stronger claim requires a dynamical mechanism that generates it and independent observational validation.

The central objective is therefore not to replace cosmology with spectral language, but to determine whether spectral data can serve as a compact, mathematically controlled representation of cosmological dynamics with measurable predictive power.

Keywords: spectral cosmology; spectral geometry; primordial perturbations; curvature power spectrum; effective operators; inflation; Sturm–Liouville theory; cosmological inverse problems; spectral stability; observational falsifiability.

## 1. Foundational statement and scope

### 1.1 The central question

Modern cosmology connects the expansion history of the universe to its geometry, matter content, and perturbations. In a homogeneous and isotropic background, the scale factor \\(a(t)\\) determines how physical distances evolve. Perturbations about that background evolve according to differential equations whose coefficients depend on the geometry and the underlying fields.

These equations can be interpreted spectrally.

The research question is:

\\[ \boxed{ \text{Can cosmological dynamics determine a spectrum that predicts observables?} } \\]

A positive answer requires more than assigning eigenvalues to cosmological quantities. It requires a chain of derivation,

\\[ \text{dynamical law} \longrightarrow \text{background solution} \longrightarrow \text{effective operator} \longrightarrow \text{spectral data} \longrightarrow \text{observable}. \\]

Every arrow must be defined and justified.

The inverse chain is also important. If observations suggest a spectral feature, one must determine whether it can be generated by a physically admissible background and operator, rather than merely fitting a convenient function to the measured spectrum.

### 1.2 What SCT does and does not assert

SCT is proposed as a framework for studying the spectral organization of cosmological observables. It does not, at this stage, establish a new fundamental law of gravity, a new substance underlying spacetime, or a unique operator governing all cosmological phenomena.

The following distinctions are essential:

- Established physics: General relativity, standard cosmological perturbation theory, and the spectral analysis of linear differential operators.
- Model-dependent derivation: The mapping from a particular cosmological background and effective mode equation to a primordial power spectrum.
- SCT hypothesis: A dynamically determined spectral representation may yield a more predictive and structurally informative description of selected cosmological observables.
- Open conjecture: A small set of spectral invariants may organize several apparently distinct cosmological observables without requiring an independent parametrization for each.

The conjecture is not assumed in the derivations that follow.

### 1.3 The explanatory criterion

A spectral description qualifies as explanatory only if it satisfies four conditions.

1. Dynamical determination: The operator is derived from specified field equations and physical assumptions.
2. Spectral well-posedness: Its domain, boundary or initial conditions, and relevant spectral quantities are defined.
3. Observable map: A mathematical procedure connects the spectrum to a measurable quantity.
4. Predictive closure: At least one observable feature is predicted from independently specified parameters, with uncertainties and possible failure conditions stated in advance.

Without these conditions, spectral cosmology risks becoming a change of notation rather than a theory.

## 2. Mathematical foundations

### 2.1 Cosmological spacetime and background dynamics

Let the homogeneous background be described by a spatially flat Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker metric,

\\[ ds^2=-dt^2+a^2(t)\delta\_{ij}dx^i dx^j, \\]

where \\(a(t)\\) is the scale factor and

\\[ H(t)=\frac{\dot a}{a} \\]

is the Hubble parameter. In conformal time \\(d\eta=dt/a(t)\\), the metric becomes

\\[ ds^2=a^2(\eta)\left(-d\eta^2+\delta\_{ij}dx^i dx^j\right). \\]

For Einstein gravity with reduced Planck mass \\(M\_{\mathrm{Pl}}\\), the homogeneous background obeys

\\[ H^2=\frac{\rho}{3M\_{\mathrm{Pl}}^2}, \qquad \dot H=-\frac{\rho+p}{2M\_{\mathrm{Pl}}^2}, \\]

where \\(\rho\\) and \\(p\\) are the total energy density and pressure. These equations constrain the expansion history once the matter model and its initial conditions are specified.

The dimensionless parameter

\\[ \epsilon_H=-\frac{\dot H}{H^2} \\]

measures the departure from exact de Sitter expansion. In canonical single-field inflation, \\(\epsilon_H\\) is determined by the scalar field trajectory and its potential. It is therefore not an independent spectral parameter if the model is to possess explanatory closure.

The essential SCT principle is that spectral quantities must inherit their values from the dynamics of \\(a(t)\\), the matter fields, and their perturbations.

### 2.2 The cosmological perturbation operator

Consider a canonical scalar field driving inflation. At linear order, the gauge-invariant comoving curvature perturbation \\(\mathcal R\\) can be represented by the Mukhanov variable

\\[ v(\eta,\mathbf x)=z(\eta)\mathcal R(\eta,\mathbf x), \qquad z(\eta)=a(\eta)\frac{\dot\phi}{H} =a(\eta)\sqrt{2\epsilon_H}\\,M\_{\mathrm{Pl}}, \\]

where the final equality assumes a canonical scalar field and \\(\dot\phi^2=2\epsilon_HM\_{\mathrm{Pl}}^2H^2\\).

For each comoving Fourier mode \\(k=|\mathbf k|\\), the Mukhanov–Sasaki equation is

\\[ v_k''+ \left(k^2-\frac{z''}{z}\right)v_k=0. \\]

Equivalently, define the differential expression

\\[ \mathcal L\_{\mathrm{MS}} = -\frac{d^2}{d\eta^2} +\frac{z''}{z}. \\]

Then

\\[ \boxed{\mathcal L\_{\mathrm{MS}}v_k=k^2v_k.} \\]

This form supplies a natural starting point for SCT: the expansion and matter dynamics determine \\(z(\eta)\\), which determines the effective potential \\(z''/z\\), which governs the evolution of the perturbation modes.

However, an important mathematical distinction must be preserved. This equation resembles a stationary spectral eigenvalue problem, but cosmological evolution is ordinarily an initial-value problem on a time-dependent background. Unless a specific domain, boundary conditions, and self-adjoint realization are established, \\(k^2\\) should not automatically be interpreted as an eigenvalue in the discrete-spectrum sense.

The operator language is useful without pretending that every cosmological mode equation is a conventional eigenvalue problem.

### 2.3 Operator families and spectral data

SCT may be formalized by a parameter-dependent family

\\[ \mathfrak L(\lambda) = -\frac{d^2}{d\eta^2}+U(\eta;\lambda), \\]

where \\(\lambda\\) denotes physical parameters and \\(U\\) is determined by the background and perturbation theory.

For the canonical single-field example,

\\[ U(\eta;\lambda)=\frac{z''(\eta;\lambda)} {z(\eta;\lambda)}. \\]

The parameter set may include parameters of the inflationary potential, initial conditions, and coefficients of a specified effective field theory. It must not contain arbitrary values assigned independently to each observed wavenumber.

Depending on the problem, the relevant spectral information can include:

- discrete eigenvalues and their multiplicities, when a well-defined self-adjoint boundary-value problem exists;
- scattering coefficients and mode-mixing amplitudes for time-dependent evolution;
- the spectral density of an elliptic operator on spatial slices;
- eigenfunctions and their overlap with observable perturbations;
- gap or stability information for an appropriate linearized operator;
- asymptotic scaling laws of the mode solutions.

These quantities are not interchangeable. A spatial Laplacian spectrum, a time-dependent mode equation, and the eigenvalues of a cosmological stability operator describe different mathematical structures. A viable SCT model must identify which structure is being used and why it controls the observable of interest.

### 2.4 The forward map

Let \\(\mathcal B\\) denote a cosmological background solution and let \\(\mathcal I\\) denote the initial-state prescription for perturbations. A forward model has the form

\\[ (\mathcal B,\mathcal I) \longrightarrow \mathfrak L \longrightarrow \\{v_k\\} \longrightarrow \mathcal P\_{\mathcal R}(k). \\]

The dimensionless primordial curvature power spectrum is defined by

\\[ \left\langle \mathcal R\_{\mathbf k}\mathcal R\_{\mathbf k'} \right\rangle = (2\pi)^3\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf k+\mathbf k') \frac{2\pi^2}{k^3}\mathcal P\_{\mathcal R}(k). \\]

For the canonical scalar-field model,

\\[ \boxed{ \mathcal P\_{\mathcal R}(k) = \frac{k^3}{2\pi^2} \lim\_{\eta\to 0^-} \left|\frac{v_k(\eta)}{z(\eta)}\right|^2 } \\]

when the late-time limit exists and the curvature perturbation freezes outside the horizon.

The limit is written for the usual inflationary conformal-time convention. Other backgrounds require the appropriate evaluation surface and late-time prescription.

This formula provides an observable map, but it does not alone constitute a prediction. The initial conditions, the background trajectory, and the evolution of the mode equation must all be fixed by the physical model.

## 3. A candidate spectral-cosmology model

### 3.1 The model tuple

Define a spectral cosmology model by

\\[ \mathfrak C= (\mathcal M,g\_{\mu\nu},\Psi,\Lambda, \mathcal E,\mathfrak L,\mathcal I,\mathcal O), \\]

where:

- \\(\mathcal M\\) is the spacetime manifold;
- \\(g\_{\mu\nu}\\) is the cosmological metric;
- \\(\Psi\\) denotes matter and perturbation fields;
- \\(\Lambda\\) is the physical parameter space;
- \\(\mathcal E=0\\) represents the specified background field equations;
- \\(\mathfrak L\\) is the derived perturbation operator or operator family;
- \\(\mathcal I\\) specifies initial-state and boundary data;
- \\(\mathcal O\\) is the map from solutions to observables.

The dependence of the operator on the physical model must be explicit:

\\[ \mathfrak L= \mathfrak L[g\_{\mu\nu}(\lambda),\Psi(\lambda)]. \\]

The background is determined by

\\[ \mathcal E[g\_{\mu\nu},\Psi;\lambda]=0, \\]

subject to initial and constraint conditions. The perturbation operator follows from the second variation of the action, after gauge redundancies and nondynamical constraints have been treated correctly.

This is a methodological definition, not a claim that one universal operator has already been identified.

### 3.2 Deriving the operator from an action

Suppose the fundamental description is specified by an action

\\[ S[g,\Psi;\lambda]. \\]

Let \\(\Psi=\bar\Psi+\delta\Psi\\) and \\(g\_{\mu\nu}=\bar g\_{\mu\nu}+\delta g\_{\mu\nu}\\), where the barred fields solve the background equations. After expanding to quadratic order and eliminating nondynamical variables, one obtains a reduced quadratic action of the form

\\[ S^{(2)} = \frac12\int d\eta\\,d^3x\\, \left[ (v')^2-c_s^2(\eta)(\nabla v)^2 +\frac{z''}{z}v^2 \right] \\]

for the single propagating scalar mode in a standard effective description, with the canonical normalization understood.

Variation yields

\\[ v_k''+ \left[ c_s^2(\eta)k^2-\frac{z''}{z} \right]v_k=0. \\]

Here \\(c_s\\) is the effective propagation speed of the scalar perturbation. In canonical single-field inflation \\(c_s=1\\); more general effective theories may give a time-dependent value.

The operator formulation is therefore downstream of the action, background solution, and constraint structure. An independently chosen \\(U(\eta)\\) is not a derivation of cosmological dynamics.

### 3.3 Spectral equivalence and physical equivalence

Two operator families can share some spectral properties without representing the same physical universe. Conversely, two different formulations can yield identical observable predictions over a restricted range.

SCT must therefore distinguish:

\\[ \text{operator equivalence} \\;\not\Rightarrow\\; \text{physical equivalence}, \\]

and

\\[ \text{agreement of one observable} \\;\not\Rightarrow\\; \text{equivalence of cosmological models}. \\]

A meaningful equivalence relation should specify which structures are preserved: the background geometry, the perturbation equations, the initial state, the observable map, or only a selected subset of spectral invariants.

For cosmological inference, observational equivalence over a finite range of \\(k\\) is weaker than mathematical equivalence of the underlying operator families.

## 4. The central conditional result: spectral data determine the primordial spectrum

### 4.1 Mode-evolution proposition

Proposition 4.1 — Determinacy of the primordial spectrum.

Let \\(U(\eta)\\) be a specified real effective potential on an interval \\((\eta_i,\eta_f)\\). Assume:

1. the mode equation\
   \\[\
   v_k''+[k^2-U(\eta)]v_k=0\
   \\]\
   is well posed for the wavenumbers of interest;
2. the initial conditions \\(v_k(\eta_i)\\) and \\(v_k'(\eta_i)\\) are uniquely specified;
3. a fixed evaluation prescription defines \\(\mathcal R_k=v_k/z\\) at the end of the relevant evolution;
4. \\(z(\eta)\\) is nonzero at the evaluation surface.

Then the mode solution is uniquely determined for each \\(k\\), and hence the power spectrum

\\[ \mathcal P\_{\mathcal R}(k) = \frac{k^3}{2\pi^2} \left|\frac{v_k}{z}\right|^2 \\]

is uniquely determined by \\(U\\), the initial conditions, and \\(z\\).

Proof. The mode equation is a linear second-order ordinary differential equation. Under the stated regularity and initial-value assumptions, uniqueness of solutions fixes \\(v_k\\) for each \\(k\\). The evaluation prescription and the nonvanishing normalization \\(z\\) then determine \\(\mathcal R_k\\). The definition of the two-point function fixes the corresponding power spectrum. \\(\square\\)

The proposition is a standard consequence of differential-equation theory, not a new theorem of cosmology. Its significance for SCT is methodological: a spectral model becomes predictive only when its operator and initial data are themselves fixed.

### 4.2 What this proposition does not establish

It does not prove that the spectrum is uniquely determined by the expansion history alone. Two models with the same scale factor may have different perturbation sectors, sound speeds, field content, or initial states.

It also does not prove that the primordial spectrum determines a unique expansion history. The inverse problem may be nonunique: distinct background histories and effective actions can yield nearly identical two-point functions.

SCT must consequently be formulated as a constrained forward and inverse problem, not as an assumption of one-to-one correspondence.

## 5. A parameter-controlled prediction: a localized feature in the primordial power spectrum

The most useful initial test of SCT is not a claim about the spectrum of the entire universe. It is a concrete prediction for a specified observable.

We choose the primordial scalar curvature power spectrum and investigate whether a localized perturbation of the effective mode potential produces a calculable oscillatory feature.

This example is deliberately restricted. It tests the spectral method without presupposing that every cosmological observable shares the same operator or invariant.

### 5.1 Reference cosmology

Consider a reference inflationary model with effective potential

\\[ U_0(\eta)=\frac{z_0''}{z_0}. \\]

Its mode equation is

\\[ v\_{k,0}''+[k^2-U_0(\eta)]v\_{k,0}=0. \\]

For a slowly varying background, the leading-order horizon-crossing estimate is

\\[ \mathcal P\_{\mathcal R}^{(0)}(k) \simeq \frac{H\_\*^2} {8\pi^2 M\_{\mathrm{Pl}}^2\epsilon\_{H,\*}}, \\]

where the star denotes evaluation near \\(c_s k=aH\\), and the formula assumes a canonical single-field model in its usual vacuum state. This is a leading slow-roll result, not an exact expression for an arbitrary cosmology.

The reference spectrum is nearly a power law,

\\[ \mathcal P\_{\mathcal R}^{(0)}(k) = A_s\left(\frac{k}{k\_\*}\right)^{n_s-1}, \\]

up to the running and higher-order effects retained by a more accurate background calculation.

The spectral-cosmology question is whether a localized dynamical event can produce a controlled deviation from this reference spectrum.

### 5.2 Effective-potential deformation

Introduce the decomposition

\\[ U(\eta)=U_0(\eta)+\delta U(\eta), \\]

with a localized Gaussian deformation

\\[ \boxed{ \delta U(\eta) = \frac{\varepsilon_f}{\tau^2} \exp\left[ -\frac{(\eta-\eta_f)^2}{2\tau^2} \right]. } \\]

The parameters are:

- \\(\varepsilon_f\\): dimensionless signed amplitude;
- \\(\eta_f\\): conformal time of the feature;
- \\(\tau\\): conformal-time width.

Because \\(U\\) has dimensions of inverse conformal time squared, the expression is dimensionally consistent.

This deformation is a test profile, not yet a fundamental cosmological law. To become a physical SCT model, it must be derived from a specified potential, sound-speed history, field-space trajectory, or other dynamical mechanism.

The deformation leads to

\\[ v_k''+ \left[ k^2-U_0(\eta)-\delta U(\eta) \right]v_k=0. \\]

For a sufficiently weak deformation, one can approximate the effect using first-order scattering theory. In the subhorizon regime, where the reference modes are approximately oscillatory, the induced negative-frequency admixture has the schematic form

\\[ \beta_k \simeq \frac{i}{2k} \int d\eta\\, \delta U(\eta)e^{-2ik\eta}, \\]

up to a convention-dependent overall sign or phase determined by the mode normalization.

The physically important structure is the Fourier transform of the localized potential deformation.

Substituting the Gaussian gives

\\[ \begin{aligned} \int d\eta\\,\delta U(\eta)e^{-2ik\eta} &= \frac{\varepsilon_f}{\tau^2} \int d\eta\\, e^{-(\eta-\eta_f)^2/(2\tau^2)} e^{-2ik\eta}\\\\[4pt] &= \frac{\sqrt{2\pi}\\,\varepsilon_f}{\tau} e^{-2k^2\tau^2} e^{-2ik\eta_f}. \end{aligned} \\]

Thus,

\\[ \boxed{ \beta_k \simeq i\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}\\,\varepsilon_f}{2k\tau} e^{-2k^2\tau^2}e^{-2ik\eta_f}. } \\]

This approximation assumes weak scattering and a regime in which the oscillatory reference-mode treatment is valid. It is not a uniformly valid expression at arbitrarily small \\(k\\). The full background mode equation must be solved outside that regime.

### 5.3 Induced power-spectrum feature

A small admixture of positive- and negative-frequency solutions changes the late-time mode amplitude through interference. At first order, the fractional power-spectrum correction is proportional to the real part of the induced mode-mixing coefficient, with the precise phase depending on the reference solution and normalization.

A convenient phenomenological representation of the resulting feature is

\\[ \boxed{ \frac{\Delta\mathcal P\_{\mathcal R}(k)} {\mathcal P\_{\mathcal R}^{(0)}(k)} \simeq A_f\\,W(k\tau) \cos(2k|\eta_f|+\varphi_f). } \\]

Here \\(W\\) is an envelope determined by the event profile, \\(A_f\\) is the induced feature amplitude, and \\(\varphi_f\\) is a phase fixed by the reference dynamics and the scattering convention. For the Gaussian test profile, the leading subhorizon calculation produces a Gaussian Fourier envelope proportional to \\(e^{-2k^2\tau^2}\\), with a prefactor scaling as \\(\varepsilon_f/(k\tau)\\). Consequently, \\(A_f\\) is not an independent free amplitude once the physical deformation and background have been specified.

The central prediction is structural:

- the feature is oscillatory in \\(k\\);
- its characteristic oscillation spacing is set by \\(|\eta_f|\\);
- its high-\\(k\\) suppression is controlled by \\(\tau\\);
- its amplitude is controlled by the strength of the dynamical deformation;
- its phase and detailed envelope depend on the reference background and the mode-matching calculation.

The oscillatory form is not unique to SCT. Transient changes in the inflationary expansion rate or sound speed are already known to generate primordial spectral features. SCT's potential contribution would be to derive those features from a constrained operator model and show that its parameters have independent physical meaning. See the discussion of feature-generating mechanisms in Palma and the observational analysis by the Planck Collaboration.&#x20;

### 5.4 Feature location and width

The oscillatory phase is approximately

\\[ \Theta(k)=2k|\eta_f|+\varphi_f. \\]

Successive extrema separated by a phase increment of order \\(2\pi\\) are therefore separated in comoving wavenumber by approximately

\\[ \Delta k\simeq\frac{\pi}{|\eta_f|}. \\]

This relation identifies the conformal-time scale of the event from the oscillation spacing, subject to the validity of the approximation and to the mapping between the primordial spectrum and the measured observables.

The envelope

\\[ W(k\tau)\propto e^{-2k^2\tau^2} \\]

suppresses the feature when \\(k\tau\gg1\\). A narrower event in conformal time has a broader Fourier-space envelope; a broader event has a more restricted range of affected wavenumbers.

These relations are mathematically controlled properties of the test deformation. They are not, by themselves, predictions that such an event occurred during the actual history of the universe.

### 5.5 What makes the result parameter-controlled?

A fit in which \\(A_f\\), \\(\eta_f\\), \\(\tau\\), and \\(\varphi_f\\) are all adjusted independently to the observed spectrum is only a phenomenological template.

A predictive SCT construction must instead specify a dynamical parameter set \\(\lambda\\) and derive

\\[ \lambda \longrightarrow a(\eta;\lambda),\\,z(\eta;\lambda) \longrightarrow U(\eta;\lambda) \longrightarrow \mathcal P\_{\mathcal R}(k;\lambda). \\]

The feature parameters then become functions of the underlying model:

\\[ A_f=A_f(\lambda),\quad \eta_f=\eta_f(\lambda),\quad \tau=\tau(\lambda),\quad \varphi_f=\varphi_f(\lambda). \\]

The model must not use the observed feature to define its own dynamical input and then claim the same feature as a prediction. The event mechanism and its parameters must be specified independently or constrained by additional observables.

### 5.6 Consistency with perturbation theory

A valid feature model must respect the approximation used to derive it. In particular:

1. The induced mode mixing must remain small where first-order perturbation theory is used.
2. The deformation must not excite frequencies beyond the validity range of the effective theory.
3. Higher-order interactions and non-Gaussian signatures must be checked if the feature is sufficiently sharp or large.
4. The background must satisfy its field equations and stability conditions.
5. The calculation must transition to the full numerical mode equation when the scattering approximation is inadequate.

These restrictions are physical, not merely numerical. A formal fit that requires an event too sharp for the effective theory to describe consistently is not a viable SCT explanation.

Research on inflationary features explicitly emphasizes the need for effective-theory consistency and cross-checks involving higher-point observables.&#x20;

## 6. Spectral geometry beyond the primordial mode equation

The preceding example uses the one-dimensional time-evolution operator for cosmological perturbations. SCT may also investigate spatial spectral geometry, but the two constructions must not be conflated.

### 6.1 Spatial Laplacian spectrum

On a spatial hypersurface \\(\Sigma_t\\) with induced metric \\(h\_{ij}\\), define the positive-sign Laplace–Beltrami operator

\\[ \mathcal L\_\Sigma=-\Delta_h. \\]

When \\(\Sigma_t\\) is compact and suitable boundary conditions are imposed, its spectrum is discrete:

\\[ \mathcal L\_\Sigma\psi_n=\lambda_n\psi_n, \qquad 0\leq\lambda_0\leq\lambda_1\leq\cdots. \\]

For a noncompact spatial slice, the spectrum may instead be continuous, or contain both continuous and discrete components depending on the geometry and operator.

The eigenvalues \\(\lambda_n\\) depend on the spatial geometry and the boundary or global conditions. They may encode geometric information, but they do not uniquely determine every aspect of the geometry in all settings. Isospectral geometries provide a warning against assuming that spectral data always determine a unique spacetime.

### 6.2 The heat trace

A standard spectral summary is the heat trace

\\[ K(s)=\operatorname{Tr}\left(e^{-s\mathcal L\_\Sigma}\right) =\sum_n e^{-s\lambda_n}, \\]

where the sum is interpreted appropriately for the spectrum.

For a smooth compact \\(d\\)-dimensional Riemannian manifold without boundary, the short-\\(s\\) asymptotic expansion begins

\\[ K(s)\sim \frac{1}{(4\pi s)^{d/2}} \left[ \operatorname{Vol}(\Sigma) +\frac{s}{6} \int\_\Sigma R_h\\,dV_h +O(s^2) \right], \\]

where \\(R_h\\) is the scalar curvature of the spatial metric.

The expansion illustrates how spectral data encode geometric quantities such as volume and integrated curvature. Its precise coefficients and higher-order terms depend on the operator, dimension, boundary conditions, and geometric assumptions.

A cosmological extension could examine how \\(K(s,t)\\) evolves with the expanding spatial geometry. But the expansion history does not automatically supply a new independent spectral law: in a specified geometry, the Laplacian and its heat trace follow from the metric.

### 6.3 A potential SCT invariant

One candidate is the spectral dimension,

\\[ d_s(s) = -2\frac{d\ln K(s)}{d\ln s}, \\]

when the heat trace is well-defined and the derivative exists.

For a smooth \\(d\\)-dimensional compact Riemannian geometry, the short-distance asymptotic regime yields \\(d_s\to d\\). A scale-dependent spectral dimension can arise in other geometric or effective settings, but such behavior must be derived from a specified operator and scale regime.

SCT should not assume that the spectral dimension changes during cosmic evolution simply because the universe expands. In a uniformly rescaled spatial geometry, the Laplacian eigenvalues rescale in a predictable way, while dimensionless geometric information may remain unchanged. A claimed change in spectral dimension must distinguish ordinary expansion from a genuine change in spectral structure.

### 6.4 Why several spectral objects are needed

The primordial perturbation spectrum, the spatial Laplacian spectrum, and the heat trace are distinct objects:

| Spectral object                   | Mathematical role                                           | Possible cosmological application           |
| --------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Time-dependent mode operator      | Controls evolution of perturbation modes                    | Primordial power spectrum                   |
| Spatial Laplace–Beltrami operator | Encodes spatial geometric structure                         | Curvature and topology constraints          |
| Heat trace                        | Aggregates eigenvalues across scales                        | Geometric invariants and spectral dimension |
| Linearized stability operator     | Describes growth or decay of perturbations about a solution | Stability of cosmological branches          |

A theory that proposes a common invariant across these objects must prove the mapping between them. Similar terminology does not establish mathematical equivalence.

## 7. The observational prediction program

### 7.1 From primordial spectrum to measured sky

The primordial spectrum is not measured directly. Observations of cosmic microwave background (CMB) anisotropies and large-scale structure are related to it through transfer functions, projection effects, astrophysical contributions, and instrumental response.

For example, the angular CMB temperature power spectrum has the schematic form

\\[ C\_\ell^{TT} = 4\pi\int d\ln k\\, \mathcal P\_{\mathcal R}(k) \left|\Delta\_\ell^T(k)\right|^2, \\]

where \\(\Delta\_\ell^T(k)\\) is the temperature transfer function. More complete likelihood calculations include polarization, lensing, foregrounds, and experimental effects.

A primordial oscillation does not map one-to-one onto an angular oscillation with the same frequency. Projection integrates over wavenumbers, and the resulting feature can be smoothed or shifted in apparent significance.

Consequently, the test of SCT is not simply whether a cosine fits a reconstructed primordial spectrum. The model must be propagated through the observable prediction machinery and compared against measured data.

### 7.2 A reference observational baseline

The Planck 2018 analysis found the scalar spectral index to be approximately \\(n_s=0.965\\), with uncertainties at the level of a few parts in a thousand, and found no compelling evidence for deviations from a pure power-law primordial spectrum or for the tested classes of oscillatory features. Those results are an important historical benchmark, not a substitute for a fresh analysis of all currently available data.&#x20;

The implication for SCT is methodological. A proposed spectral feature must demonstrate a statistically justified improvement over a strong baseline model, with the extra parameter freedom and the search over feature locations and frequencies accounted for.

A feature that merely improves the best-fit likelihood after extensive scanning may not be significant once the full search procedure is included.

### 7.3 Preregistered prediction

For the Gaussian test profile, a useful preregistered prediction would specify:

\\[ \lambda\_{\mathrm{phys}} \longrightarrow \left\\{ A_f,\eta_f,\tau,\varphi_f \right\\} \longrightarrow \left\\{ \mathcal P\_{\mathcal R}(k),C\_\ell^{TT},C\_\ell^{TE},C\_\ell^{EE} \right\\}. \\]

The following must be fixed before examining the target residuals:

- the dynamical model generating the event;
- the prior ranges and independent constraints on its physical parameters;
- the mode-equation solver and approximation error;
- the predicted feature location, width, amplitude, and phase;
- the predicted correlations with other observables;
- the baseline model and statistical decision criterion.

The feature should be tested on data not used to select its preferred location or shape, where feasible. The same physical parameters should be required to explain all observables included in the model.

### 7.4 The bispectrum as an independent consistency check

A feature in the two-point function may imply a correlated pattern in the primordial bispectrum,

\\[ \left\langle \mathcal R\_{\mathbf k_1} \mathcal R\_{\mathbf k_2} \mathcal R\_{\mathbf k_3} \right\rangle = (2\pi)^3 \delta^{(3)}\\!\left( \mathbf k_1+\mathbf k_2+\mathbf k_3 \right) B\_{\mathcal R}(k_1,k_2,k_3). \\]

The bispectrum is sensitive to interactions and background evolution that are not fully characterized by the power spectrum alone. Different mechanisms can generate similar two-point features but predict different three-point shapes.

An SCT model should therefore derive the relevant higher-order action and calculate the associated bispectrum whenever the proposed mechanism produces one at a measurable level. It must not infer a unique bispectrum from the power-spectrum feature without a dynamical derivation.

A joint fit of the power spectrum and bispectrum can reject otherwise plausible phenomenological templates and can help distinguish a physical mechanism from a flexible statistical fit.&#x20;

### 7.5 Distinguishing a physical feature from a parametrization

The following hierarchy separates increasingly strong claims.

Level I — Spectral fit

An oscillatory or localized template describes data better than a baseline.

Evidence for a descriptive pattern only.

Level II — Dynamical realization

A specified background and effective action generate the template.

A physical explanation within a model class.

Level III — Independent prediction

The model fixes additional observables or a new parameter-controlled feature before those data are fitted.

A stronger test of explanatory power.

Level IV — Replicated constraint

Independent data favor the prediction over alternatives, and the model survives consistency tests.

Evidence for the proposed physical mechanism, not proof of a unique underlying theory.

SCT currently belongs at the level of a proposed framework with a conditional calculation program. The Gaussian deformation illustrates how a prediction could be constructed, but no observed feature has been established here as a consequence of a new spectral-cosmology law.

## 8. A conditional theorem about spectral stability

A second mathematical result concerns the robustness of spectral information under controlled perturbations.

### 8.1 Gap stability for self-adjoint operators

Let \\(A\\) be a self-adjoint operator with an isolated pair of eigenvalues \\(E_0\<E_1\\), separated by a gap

\\[ \Delta=E_1-E_0>0. \\]

Let \\(B=A+V\\), where \\(V\\) is a bounded self-adjoint perturbation. The min–max principle gives the eigenvalue perturbation bound

\\[ |E_j(B)-E_j(A)|\leq\\|V\\| \\]

for the relevant discrete eigenvalues under the stated assumptions. Hence

\\[ \boxed{ \Delta(B)\geq\Delta(A)-2\\|V\\|. } \\]

Therefore, if

\\[ 2\\|V\\|<\Delta(A), \\]

the perturbed gap remains positive.

This is an established operator-theoretic bound. It is not an original theorem of SCT.

### 8.2 Cosmological interpretation

The bound suggests a useful stability question: how much can an effective perturbation operator change before a spectral feature, mode separation, or isolated spectral sector becomes unstable?

However, applying the inequality requires identifying an appropriate self-adjoint operator, its domain, and a well-defined spectral gap. A time-dependent Mukhanov–Sasaki evolution problem cannot simply be treated as a static eigenvalue problem without further construction.

For cosmological perturbations, the bound may be applicable to a spatial operator, a suitably defined instantaneous stability operator, or another well-posed self-adjoint realization. The physical implication must then be derived for that particular system.

The legitimate SCT research target is not to rename the inequality, but to establish whether a physically meaningful cosmological operator admits a useful stability bound whose consequences can be tested.

## 9. Inverse spectral cosmology

### 9.1 The reconstruction problem

The forward problem asks for the observable given a cosmological model. The inverse problem asks which models are compatible with a measured observable.

Schematically,

\\[ \mathcal P\_{\mathcal R}(k) \longrightarrow \left\\{ a(\eta),\\,z(\eta),\\,U(\eta),\\,\mathcal I \right\\}. \\]

This mapping is generally underdetermined without additional assumptions. The power spectrum supplies a modulus-squared amplitude, whereas the mode solution contains phase information and depends on the initial-state prescription. Different combinations of background evolution and perturbation dynamics may therefore produce nearly identical spectra.

A successful inverse theory must characterize the equivalence class of models compatible with the observations rather than assuming a unique reconstruction.

### 9.2 Identifiability

Let the predicted observable be \\(O(\lambda)\\), where \\(\lambda\in\Lambda\\) denotes the physical parameter vector. Local identifiability requires that small parameter changes produce distinguishable observable changes in the measured domain.

A local diagnostic is the sensitivity matrix

\\[ J\_{ij}=\frac{\partial O_i}{\partial\lambda_j}. \\]

If the relevant columns of \\(J\\) are linearly dependent, some combinations of parameters cannot be separated using those observables alone. In a statistical analysis, this issue is reflected in degeneracies of the likelihood or Fisher information matrix.

The spectral formulation does not remove these degeneracies automatically. It can make them more transparent by expressing the dependence through the operator, its solutions, and the observable map.

### 9.3 Regularization and prior assumptions

Reconstructing a function \\(U(\eta)\\) from a finite and noisy range of power-spectrum data is ill-conditioned unless one imposes regularity conditions, physical priors, or a restricted parameterization.

A defensible inverse-SCT analysis must report:

- the prior assumptions on the background and operator;
- the smoothing or regularization procedure;
- the resolution in wavenumber;
- the parameter combinations that remain unidentifiable;
- the dependence of reconstructed features on the assumed initial state;
- the uncertainty propagated into predictions for other observables.

A smooth reconstructed spectrum is not proof that the underlying dynamics are smooth; nor is a localized reconstructed feature proof of a localized physical event. Both claims require a model comparison and a physical reconstruction.

## 10. What a universal spectral description would require

### 10.1 The common-invariant conjecture

The strongest version of SCT would posit that a restricted set of spectral quantities captures important aspects of several cosmological observables.

One possible candidate descriptor is

\\[ \mathfrak S(\mathfrak L)= \left( \mathcal I\_{\mathrm{spec}}, \mathcal G\_{\mathrm{spec}}, \mathcal Q\_{\mathrm{spec}}, \mathcal M\_{\mathrm{spec}} \right), \\]

where:

- \\(\mathcal I\_{\mathrm{spec}}\\) denotes relevant spectral invariants;
- \\(\mathcal G\_{\mathrm{spec}}\\) denotes gap, stability, or mode-separation information;
- \\(\mathcal Q\_{\mathrm{spec}}\\) denotes asymptotic scaling or spectral-density information;
- \\(\mathcal M\_{\mathrm{spec}}\\) denotes the mapping from spectral data to specified observables.

This tuple is a proposed organizational device, not a demonstrated universal invariant. Its components are meaningful only after the operator class, domain, equivalence relation, and observable map have been fixed.

For example, the eigenvalue distribution of a spatial Laplacian and the Bogoliubov coefficients of a time-dependent mode equation cannot be substituted for one another without a derivation.

### 10.2 A conditional classification theorem

Proposition 10.1 — Observable equivalence under complete forward-data equivalence.

Let two well-posed cosmological perturbation models, \\(M_1\\) and \\(M_2\\), have identical normalized mode solutions at the evaluation surface for every wavenumber in a domain \\(K\\), and let their observable maps be identical on those solutions. Then they predict identical primordial curvature power spectra on \\(K\\), and identical downstream observables wherever the same transfer functions and cosmological parameters apply.

Proof. Equality of the normalized mode solutions implies equality of their modulus-squared amplitudes. By the definition of \\(\mathcal P\_{\mathcal R}(k)\\), the primordial spectra coincide on \\(K\\). Applying the same observable map and transfer functions then yields identical downstream predictions for the observables covered by the assumptions. \\(\square\\)

The result is mathematically straightforward. It does not establish that equality of a finite list of spectral invariants implies equality of the mode solutions. That is the substantive open problem.

### 10.3 The missing sufficiency theorem

The genuinely ambitious SCT target is to identify a compact set of invariants \\(\mathfrak S\\) and prove a statement of the form

\\[ \mathfrak S(M_1)=\mathfrak S(M_2) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad O(M_1)=O(M_2) \\]

for a precisely specified model class and observable \\(O\\).

Such a theorem would need to establish:

1. the admissible operator class;
2. the exact equivalence relation on models;
3. the sufficiency of the proposed invariant set;
4. the conditions under which the implication holds;
5. the limits of the result, including counterexamples.

For a single observable over a finite wavenumber range, the required invariant may be relatively compact. For the full collection of cosmological observables, it may require far more information than a few eigenvalues or spectral moments.

SCT should not assume that a finite-dimensional universal signature exists.

## 11. Relationship to established cosmology

SCT overlaps with several mature areas of research. Its novelty, if any, must be established relative to these existing approaches.

### 11.1 Standard cosmological perturbation theory

The Mukhanov–Sasaki formalism already derives primordial perturbations from a background-dependent differential equation. Rewriting this equation as an operator equation does not constitute a new physical result.

SCT can contribute if it yields a new structural classification, a useful inverse method, a provable invariant, or a prediction that is more constrained than the existing background-and-perturbation calculation.

### 11.2 Effective field theory of inflation

Effective field theory organizes the perturbation dynamics using symmetry, degrees of freedom, and coefficients of allowed interactions. Changes in the background expansion rate or sound speed can generate features in the primordial spectra. These mechanisms overlap directly with the test model developed above.&#x20;

A change of representation is useful when it exposes a new invariant, a computational simplification, a robust relation among observables, or a principled reduction of parameter freedom.

### 11.3 Spectral geometry

Spectral geometry studies the relation between differential operators and geometric structure. The heat-kernel expansion provides a standard route from spectra to local geometric invariants. SCT may apply this mathematics to evolving cosmological geometries, but it must specify the spatial operator, hypersurface, boundary conditions, and physical meaning of its spectral data.

The fact that geometric quantities can be encoded in spectral asymptotics does not imply that the full spacetime history can be reconstructed from a single spatial spectrum.

### 11.4 Quantum field theory in curved spacetime

The evolution of quantum modes in a time-dependent background can produce particle creation or mode mixing relative to a chosen vacuum prescription. The Bogoliubov coefficient used in the feature calculation is part of this established mathematical structure.

SCT should not present mode mixing itself as a new discovery. Its proposed contribution would be a dynamical, predictive classification of when such mixing occurs and how it constrains cosmological observables.

### 11.5 Cosmological data analysis

A spectral model is also a statistical model. It must be compared with alternatives using a likelihood that includes relevant nuisance parameters, covariance, foreground treatment, and the complexity introduced by searching over features.

A statistically preferred spectral template is not necessarily a physically preferred theory. The distinction between goodness of fit, parameter evidence, and explanatory mechanism is central to the evaluation of SCT.

## 12. Fundamental limitations and possible failure modes

### 12.1 Operator nonuniqueness

Different actions, fields, or initial-state choices may generate the same or nearly the same effective operator over a limited regime. The inverse problem may therefore have multiple solutions.

Consequence: SCT cannot infer a unique microscopic cosmology from an observable spectrum without additional constraints.

### 12.2 Dependence on the initial state

The same background-dependent mode equation can produce different power spectra for different admissible initial states.

Consequence: The operator alone is insufficient unless the initial-state prescription is also specified or independently constrained.

### 12.3 Gauge and constraint structure

Cosmological perturbations include gauge redundancies and nondynamical variables. A naive Hessian or linearized operator can contain unphysical directions or apparent zero modes.

Consequence: A spectral classification built before gauge fixing or constraint reduction may classify artifacts rather than physical degrees of freedom.

### 12.4 Effective-theory breakdown

A sharply varying potential or sound speed can excite frequencies above the cutoff of the effective theory or make higher-order corrections important.

Consequence: A formal spectral feature may be mathematically consistent but physically uncontrolled.

### 12.5 Projection and observational degeneracy

Different primordial spectra can yield similar angular CMB spectra after transfer functions and projection effects are included.

Consequence: The existence of a fitted primordial feature does not by itself identify the physical mechanism that generated it.

### 12.6 No guaranteed universal operator

Scalar perturbations, tensor perturbations, spatial geometry, matter clustering, and late-time gravitational dynamics may require different operators and different notions of spectral data.

Consequence: A universal spectral cosmology cannot be assumed merely because each subsystem admits an operator description.

### 12.7 No evidence for a new law from notation alone

The standard perturbation equations already possess spectral interpretations. Introducing the name SCT does not add predictive content.

Consequence: The theory must be judged by derivations, novel mathematical results, and independent predictions rather than conceptual vocabulary.

## 13. A research program for establishing SCT

The appropriate development strategy is incremental. Each stage should be completed before the next is treated as established.

### I

Define the physical model

Specify the action, background equations, initial state, perturbation variables, and regime of validity.

Deliverable: a closed, gauge-consistent dynamical system.

### II

Derive the operator

Obtain the quadratic perturbation action and the associated operator family. Establish the domain and the relevant initial or boundary conditions.

Deliverable: an operator determined by the dynamics, not an arbitrary template.

### III

Derive the observable

Solve the mode equation analytically where possible and numerically otherwise. Propagate the resulting spectrum through the cosmological transfer functions.

Deliverable: a reproducible prediction for a specified observable.

### IV

Prove a nontrivial spectral result

Establish a stability bound, a classification theorem, or an invariant that is not merely a reformulation of a standard result.

Deliverable: a mathematical result with stated hypotheses and counterexample boundaries.

### V

Test and attempt to falsify

Compare against baseline cosmology, perform consistency checks, test additional observables, and evaluate on independent data where possible.

Deliverable: a measured gain in predictive performance, or a clear failure of the proposed model.

### 13.1 Minimal benchmark suite

At least four benchmark classes should be tested:

1. A smooth slow-roll inflationary background producing an approximately power-law spectrum.
2. A localized change in the potential or sound speed producing a transient feature.
3. A nontrivial background with significant departures from slow roll, requiring numerical mode evolution.
4. A spatial spectral-geometry problem in which the geometric spectrum is independently known or controlled.

The first three test the primordial perturbation machinery. The fourth tests the separate spectral-geometric branch. Agreement across them should not be presumed merely because the same notation can be used.

### 13.2 Numerical validation

A robust implementation should compare at least two independent calculations where feasible:

- direct integration of the background and mode equations;
- an approximation based on scattering, slow-roll, or a controlled asymptotic expansion;
- convergence tests under changes in time step, integration range, and initial-condition initialization;
- recovery of the reference result when the deformation parameter tends to zero.

For the localized test profile, the numerical solution should satisfy

\\[ \lim\_{\varepsilon_f\to0} \mathcal P\_{\mathcal R}(k;\varepsilon_f) = \mathcal P\_{\mathcal R}^{(0)}(k). \\]

The induced oscillation spacing and high-\\(k\\) envelope should approach the analytical estimates in the regime where the approximation is valid.

A mismatch outside that regime is not automatically a failure of the full theory; it may indicate a failure of the approximation. The two must be distinguished by solving the complete mode equation.

### 13.3 Statistical validation

A complete observational analysis should:

- use a documented likelihood and baseline model;
- specify priors before examining the target residuals;
- account for the look-elsewhere effect if the feature location or frequency is scanned;
- report parameter degeneracies and posterior predictive checks;
- compare Bayesian evidence or another justified model-selection criterion, not just the best-fit likelihood;
- test consistency with polarization, lensing, bispectrum, and relevant large-scale-structure data;
- state what outcome would count as failure.

The goal is not to find some feature that fits the data. It is to determine whether the physically derived feature predicted by the model is supported.

## 14. Falsifiability and decisive tests

SCT must permit outcomes that count against its central hypothesis, rather than treating every observed spectrum as compatible with a sufficiently flexible operator.

### 14.1 Falsification criteria

The following tests are decisive for the specific models developed here.

Test A — Dynamical closure. If the proposed action and background equations do not generate the claimed operator deformation, the corresponding physical explanation fails. A separately inserted Gaussian profile cannot rescue the derivation.

Test B — Analytical and numerical agreement. If the predicted perturbative feature disagrees with the numerical solution within the regime where the approximation is claimed to apply, the analytical result or its assumptions must be revised.

Test C — Parameter consistency. If one set of independently specified physical parameters cannot simultaneously reproduce the predicted feature location, amplitude, and width, the proposed mechanism is disfavored or falsified in that parameter regime.

Test D — Independent observables. If a model predicts correlated signatures in the bispectrum, polarization, or another observable and those signatures are excluded at the model's required strength, the physical mechanism fails even if its two-point spectrum fits.

Test E — Comparative predictive value. If SCT's spectral organization produces no mathematical, computational, or predictive advantage over the equivalent standard perturbation calculation, the claim that it provides a distinct explanatory framework remains unsubstantiated.

Test F — Universal classification. If two models share the proposed complete SCT invariant but yield different values for the observable that the invariant is claimed to determine, the proposed sufficiency theorem is false.

### 14.2 Null results are informative

A null result can constrain the physical parameters responsible for a feature, eliminate a class of event-generating mechanisms, or demonstrate that the simplest cosmological background remains sufficient.

It cannot, by itself, establish that all spectral-cosmology models are false. The scope of the conclusion must match the scope of the model tested.

Conversely, detecting an oscillatory feature would not uniquely establish SCT. The same observable might be generated by mechanisms already present in inflationary model building. A positive result must therefore be accompanied by a comparison of physical explanations and, ideally, an independent prediction that distinguishes them.

## 15. The unresolved foundational problem

The deepest problem is not whether cosmological perturbations can be described by operators. They already can. Nor is it whether operators possess spectra. That is established mathematics.

The unresolved problem is whether cosmological dynamics select a spectral structure with additional explanatory and predictive content.

Three questions define the central research frontier.

### 15.1 Can the spectral representation be derived without inserting the desired result?

A proposed spectrum must follow from the background action, its solution, and the perturbation dynamics. If its shape is stipulated independently, the model has not explained that shape.

The localized deformation in Section 5 demonstrates a conditional mechanism, not the existence of a new cosmological principle.

### 15.2 Can spectral information be compressed without losing predictive power?

A compact spectral descriptor could be valuable if it predicts several observables with fewer independent assumptions than a direct parametrization. But the relevant invariants, equivalence relation, and domain of validity remain to be established.

The possibility of such compression is a research conjecture, not a demonstrated universal property of cosmological models.

### 15.3 Can the same structure organize different cosmological regimes?

A compelling theory would connect early-universe perturbations, evolving spatial geometry, and perhaps late-time stability through a mathematically specified relation. Yet these systems need not share the same operator class or physical degrees of freedom.

Any cross-regime correspondence must be derived. A single symbol for multiple operators does not establish a common mechanism.

## 17. Conclusion

The Spectral Cosmology Theory is most defensible as a program for deriving cosmological observables from dynamically determined operator families, rather than as a claim that the universe is governed by a single universal spectrum.

The central mathematical chain is

\\[ \boxed{ \text{Cosmological dynamics} \rightarrow \text{effective operator} \rightarrow \text{mode evolution} \rightarrow \text{spectral response} \rightarrow \text{observable prediction}. } \\]

The framework admits a concrete demonstration: a localized modulation of the effective perturbation potential produces a controlled oscillatory response in the primordial curvature power spectrum. Its characteristic spacing and envelope are determined by the conformal-time location and width of the event, while its amplitude is fixed by the deformation and the background within the applicable approximation.

That result is conditional, and the distinction matters. The modulation must be generated by a physical model before it can count as a cosmological explanation. Existing inflationary theories already generate several classes of primordial features, so the spectral formulation must earn its standing through a new invariant, a more restrictive derivation, or a successful independent prediction.

The longer-term ambition is to identify spectral quantities that organize more than one cosmological observable and to prove when those quantities are sufficient to classify physical predictions. Such a result would require precise operator domains, a declared equivalence relation, an explicit observable map, and rigorous treatment of nonuniqueness.

The appropriate foundational claim is therefore limited but substantive:

Cosmological observables can be organized through spectral properties of effective operators, provided those operators are determined by the dynamics and the mapping from spectral data to observables is derived. Whether this organization yields a genuinely new and more predictive cosmological theory remains an open question.
