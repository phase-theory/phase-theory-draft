THEORY OF GENERATIVE REALITY

A Mathematical Foundation for the Emergence of Existence, Structure, Geometry, and Physical Law

FOUNDATIONAL THEORETICAL PHYSICS AND MATHEMATICS · RESEARCH PREPRINT · NOVEMBER 2026

Abstract

We propose the Theory of Generative Reality (TGR), a foundational mathematical framework intended to investigate the origin of physical existence, structural differentiation, relational organization, geometry, dimensionality, causality, dynamics, and the laws governing observable reality.

The central proposition is that physical reality should not be treated as the primitive starting point of fundamental physics. Rather, physical reality may be understood as the realized consequence of a deeper mathematical structure whose capacity for generating distinctions, relations, and internally consistent configurations precedes the appearance of physical objects and their governing laws.

TGR introduces a primitive generative domain , a space of potential distinctions, a relational construction operator, a generative transformation, and a global admissibility functional. These structures are investigated as the foundations of a mathematical hierarchy in which distinction precedes relation, relation supports topology, topology permits geometric realization, and geometric organization supports dimensionality, causal ordering, dynamics, and ultimately physical observables.

The theory seeks to establish whether the existence and organization of a physical universe can be derived from a minimal collection of mathematical conditions rather than assumed through an initial choice of spacetime, matter, fields, or dynamical equations.

A central objective is to formulate an existence theorem for nontrivial generative structures, a uniqueness or selection theorem for admissible realizations, and a universality theorem explaining how different generative foundations may converge toward equivalent macroscopic physical laws.

We develop a preliminary mathematical architecture using relational structures, category theory, operator-valued transformations, variational admissibility, topological realization, metric emergence, and dynamical ordering. We identify the assumptions required for each construction, distinguish mathematical consequences from physical conjectures, and formulate a program of falsifiable predictions.

TGR is presented as a proposed foundational research program. Its axioms, mathematical constructions, and theorems are not asserted to constitute an experimentally established physical theory. The principal result of this work is a coherent formal research architecture and a set of precise mathematical problems whose resolution would determine whether generative reality can serve as a genuinely fundamental description of nature.

Keywords: generative ontology; mathematical foundations of reality; emergence; structural admissibility; relational mathematics; topology; geometric genesis; dimensional emergence; causal ordering; physical law; universality; foundational physics.

1. Introduction

1.1 The unresolved problem of physical existence

The history of theoretical physics has been characterized by increasingly successful mathematical descriptions of physical phenomena. Classical mechanics describes motion through dynamical laws. Electromagnetism describes the organization and propagation of electromagnetic phenomena. Relativistic physics incorporates spacetime geometry into the description of gravitation. Modern field theories describe elementary interactions through mathematical structures of considerable abstraction.

Yet a fundamental question remains.

Why does a physically realizable mathematical structure exist at all?

Most physical theories begin with an assumed mathematical domain. This may be a manifold, a configuration space, a Hilbert space, a collection of fields, a causal structure, or an algebra of observables.

The theory then specifies how the elements of that domain behave.

Even when the underlying domain is abstract, its existence is generally taken as part of the theoretical starting point.

This leaves a deeper problem unresolved:

\boxed{
\text{What generates the mathematical conditions under which physical reality becomes possible?}
}

The Theory of Generative Reality investigates this question as a mathematical problem.

Its purpose is not initially to describe the behavior of objects within an already existing universe. Its purpose is to investigate the conditions under which a universe possessing distinguishable structures, relationships, geometry, and physical laws could arise.

1.2 The problem of assumed foundations

A foundational physical theory commonly presupposes several structures:

\mathcal F=
\{
\mathcal S,\mathcal T,\mathcal G,\mathcal D,\mathcal O
\},

where:

* denotes a state space.
* denotes an underlying temporal or ordering structure.
* denotes geometric organization.
* denotes dynamical laws.
* denotes observable quantities.

These objects are not logically independent.

A state space requires a criterion for distinguishing states. Geometry requires a structure of relations. Dynamics requires a notion of admissible transformation. Observability requires a distinction between possible outcomes.

Consequently, it is reasonable to investigate whether the mathematical foundations of these objects can be derived from a more primitive structure.

The difficulty is avoiding circularity.

If one begins with physical states, one has already assumed distinguishability. If one begins with a metric, one has assumed geometric organization. If one begins with time, one has assumed an ordering structure.

TGR therefore seeks a formulation in which these structures are constructed rather than postulated.

The primitive is not an object possessing physical properties. It is a formal basis from which the possibility of structurally distinguishable realizations may be investigated.

This distinction is essential.

If the generative foundation is simply another physical field, the theory has not explained the origin of physical structure. It has only moved the assumed foundation to a different mathematical object.

3. Axiomatic foundation

We now establish the preliminary axiomatic structure.

These axioms define a candidate mathematical framework. They are not claimed to be logically inevitable or experimentally established.

There exists a primitive generative structure from which candidate realizations may be constructed.

\exists\mathfrak G:

The existence statement here is internal to the proposed mathematical framework. It does not independently prove that a physical universe exists.

Any realized structure containing multiple distinguishable elements admits a nontrivial distinction relation.

\delta(d_\alpha,d_\beta)\neq 0

This axiom prevents the theory from treating an entirely undifferentiated collection as a structured physical realization.

It does not require that every realization contain multiple elements. A singleton realization is mathematically possible.

Distinctions may participate in relational structures.

There exists a relation operator

The theory permits unary, binary, and higher-order relations.

The importance of higher-order relations is that the organization of a structure need not be reducible to pairwise relationships.

The transformation need not represent temporal evolution.

Physical time may emerge only after an appropriate ordering structure is established.

5. Relational mathematics

5.1 Why relation must precede geometry

Geometry ordinarily presupposes a domain of points together with additional structure.

TGR investigates whether the relevant domain and its organization can be constructed from relations among distinctions.

Let:

\mathcal D=\{d_\alpha\}_{\alpha\in I}.

Define a binary relation:

R\subseteq\mathcal D\times\mathcal D.

Then:

d_\alpha R d_\beta

means that the two distinctions participate in a specified structural relationship.

No distance is implied.

No direction of physical influence is implied.

No temporal separation is implied.

The relation may encode adjacency, compatibility, dependence, incidence, or another mathematically defined connection.

5.2 Relational structure as a graph

A simple realization is a directed graph:

E\subseteq V\times V.

The adjacency operator is:

\begin{cases}
1,&(d_\alpha,d_\beta)\in E,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}

The adjacency matrix is not a metric tensor.

It describes relational organization.

For finite graphs, the degree of a vertex is:

The graph Laplacian is:

Its spectrum provides information about connectivity, component structure, and certain forms of graph diffusion.

These are established mathematical constructions. Their interpretation as foundations of physical reality remains a TGR hypothesis.

5.3 Higher-order relations

Pairwise relations may be insufficient.

5.4 Relational equivalence

Two configurations may have different internal descriptions while possessing the same relational organization.

8. Emergence of geometry

8.1 From relational organization to distance

Topology alone does not determine a metric.

A metric requires a function:

d(x,y)=0\iff x=y,d(x,y)=d(y,x),d(x,z)\leq d(x,y)+d(y,z).

The metric must be constructed from the generative relational structure.
