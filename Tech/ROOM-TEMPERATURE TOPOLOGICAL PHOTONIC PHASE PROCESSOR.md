ROOM-TEMPERATURE TOPOLOGICAL PHOTONIC PHASE PROCESSOR

A Physical Implementation of Generative Phase Quantum Mechanics Using Integrated Photonics, Silicon-Carbide Quantum Registers, and Topological Phase Transport

Abstract

This paper proposes a physical implementation of Generative Phase Quantum Mechanics (GPQM) as a room-temperature, integrated, topological photonic quantum-processing architecture.

GPQM reformulates quantum mechanics in terms of real geometric phase vectors rather than complex scalar amplitudes. The fundamental phase object is a two-dimensional real vector,

[
\Psi=
\begin{pmatrix}
\psi_1\
\psi_2
\end{pmatrix},
]

with phase rotations generated by the real quarter-turn operator

[
\mathsf Q=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&-1\
1&0
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\mathsf Q^2=-\mathsf I.
]

The corresponding dynamical equation is

[
\boxed{
\hbar\partial_t\Psi+
\mathsf Q\widehat H\Psi=0.
}
]

The proposed hardware maps this mathematical structure onto a hybrid photonic–solid-state platform. Integrated silicon-nitride or silicon-carbide photonic circuits provide propagating phase degrees of freedom, interferometric coupling, phase rotation, and optical readout. Silicon-carbide color centers provide localized quantum phase memory and matter–photon interfaces. Topological photonic networks provide controlled winding, geometric transport, and holonomy.

The central architecture is therefore

[
\boxed{
\text{phase memory}
\rightarrow
\text{phase rotation}
\rightarrow
\text{phase coupling}
\rightarrow
\text{topological transport}
\rightarrow
\text{holonomy}
\rightarrow
\text{measurement}.
}
]

The proposed processor is deliberately designed to operate without a dilution refrigerator at its primary computational layer. This is physically plausible because integrated photonic quantum operations can be compatible with room-temperature Gaussian processing, while silicon-carbide defects provide room-temperature optically addressable spin systems. Recent experiments have demonstrated integrated GKP-state generation on silicon nitride, manufacturable integrated photonic quantum circuits, room-temperature SiC spin readout, and room-temperature SiC quantum registers integrated into waveguides. (Nature)

The resulting device is not proposed merely as another photonic quantum computer. Its experimental purpose is more specific:

[
\boxed{
\text{to determine whether quantum dynamics can be physically organized and experimentally validated as generative phase transport.}
}
]

The paper develops the mathematical-to-hardware mapping, proposes device layers and unit cells, gives ASCII engineering schematics, defines control equations, specifies candidate materials and components, establishes measurement protocols, identifies failure modes, and proposes a staged experimental program extending from a single phase cell to a topological many-cell processor.

1. Introduction

Quantum mechanics is extraordinarily successful experimentally, yet its conventional mathematical representation begins with complex amplitudes,

[
\psi\in\mathbb C.
]

Generative Phase Quantum Mechanics takes a different starting point.

The wave function is represented as a real two-component phase vector,

[
\Psi\in\mathbb R^2,
]

and the operation normally represented by multiplication by the imaginary unit is represented geometrically by a real quarter-turn,

[
\mathsf Q=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&-1\
1&0
\end{pmatrix}.
]

The distinction is conceptual as well as algebraic.

The conventional representation says:

[
\psi=\psi_R+i\psi_I.
]

GPQM instead says:

[
\Psi=
\begin{pmatrix}
\psi_R\
\psi_I
\end{pmatrix}.
]

The two formulations are algebraically equivalent for ordinary complex quantum mechanics. Therefore, a physical implementation cannot establish GPQM merely by demonstrating that a complex wavefunction can be rewritten as two real components.

The experimental opportunity begins at the next layer.

GPQM proposes that phase is not merely a coordinate attached to an already-defined state. It is a physical organizational structure associated with generative histories, transport, connections, curvature, and holonomy.

That motivates a hardware platform in which phase itself is directly manipulated.

Photonic systems are unusually suitable because optical fields possess:

* phase,
* amplitude,
* interference,
* path dependence,
* mode coupling,
* geometric phase,
* polarization,
* frequency modes,
* spatial modes,
* programmable interferometers.

Silicon carbide adds localized quantum degrees of freedom and semiconductor-compatible integration. SiC is particularly attractive because optically addressable defects can be operated at room temperature and the material can support integrated photonics. A 2025 experiment demonstrated coherent photoelectrical readout of individual SiC spins at room temperature. (Nature)

A separate 2024/2025 experimental result demonstrated an electron–nuclear spin quantum register in SiC integrated into a SiC-on-insulator waveguide, with entanglement fidelity remaining high after photonic integration. (Nature)

This paper therefore proposes a room-temperature topological photonic phase processor in which:

[
\boxed{
\text{optical phase}
+
\text{localized spin phase}
+
\text{topological transport}
}
]

form one experimental system.

3.1 Real phase space

Define the phase plane

[
E_\Phi\simeq\mathbb R^2
]

with oriented orthonormal basis

[
e_1=
\begin{pmatrix}
1\
0
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
e_2=
\begin{pmatrix}
0\
1
\end{pmatrix}.
]

The quarter-turn is

[
\mathsf Qe_1=e_2,
\qquad
\mathsf Qe_2=-e_1.
]

Therefore,

[
\mathsf Q^2=-\mathsf I.
]

A general rotation is

[
R(\theta)

e^{\theta\mathsf Q}

\cos\theta,\mathsf I+
\sin\theta,\mathsf Q.
]

Explicitly,

[
R(\theta)

\begin{pmatrix}
\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\
\sin\theta&\cos\theta
\end{pmatrix}.
]

Thus

[

]

is the magnitude and

[

The phase number itself is

[

]

The proposed hardware should therefore independently control:

1. magnitude;
2. orientation;
3. coupling;
4. transport;
5. measurement.

5. Core GPQM Dynamical Equation

The fundamental evolution law is

[
\boxed{
\hbar\partial_t\Psi+
\mathsf Q\widehat H\Psi=0.
}
]

Equivalently,

[
\partial_t\Psi

-\frac{1}{\hbar}
\mathsf Q\widehat H\Psi.
]

For a scalar Hamiltonian (H),

[
\partial_t\Psi

-\frac{H}{\hbar}\mathsf Q\Psi.
]

The state therefore undergoes phase rotation.

For a spatially distributed system,

[
\Psi=\Psi(\mathbf r,t),
]

and a conventional kinetic-plus-potential Hamiltonian may be represented schematically as

[
\widehat H

-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2+V(\mathbf r).
]

The physical processor does not have to literally implement this differential operator in free space. It must implement a finite-dimensional or discretized representation whose continuum limit approaches the equation.

35. The Critical Falsification Experiment

A rigorous test must construct two systems that are equivalent under ordinary quantum mechanics but differ in their proposed generative phase structure.

Let

[
\Gamma_A,\Gamma_B
]

be two histories with the same ordinary endpoint state.

Under conventional endpoint-only characterization,

[
\rho_A=\rho_B.
]

GPQM would predict an additional dependence on the generative phase transport if the histories differ in a physically relevant GPQM connection.

Therefore the experiment must ask:

[
\boxed{
\text{Can two histories with identical ordinary endpoint descriptions produce measurably different phase transport?}
}
]

If not, the generative layer has no additional empirical content in that regime.

This is a feature, not a weakness: it defines a falsifiable research program.
