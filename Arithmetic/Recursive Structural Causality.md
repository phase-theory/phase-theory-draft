# Recursive Structural Causality  
## A Categorical Foundation for Atemporal Causal Generation  

**Preprint**

---

## Abstract

We develop **Recursive Structural Causality (RSCa)**, a general mathematical framework in which causality is treated as an abstract structural relation rather than as a primitive temporal or probabilistic notion. In RSCa, structures generate one another through recursively defined causal morphisms. The central objects of study are **recursive causal categories**: symmetric monoidal categories enriched in pointed domains, equipped with trace/feedback operators and closed under least fixed points. This yields a uniform semantics for causal generation in logic, category theory, dynamical systems, and theoretical computer science.

The paper gives:

1. an axiomatic definition of recursive structural causality;
2. a syntax and free categorical semantics for recursive causal morphisms;
3. existence and uniqueness theorems for recursive causal equations;
4. a structural generation theory based on algebras, coalgebras, and hylomorphisms;
5. a tensorial calculus of causal influence, including an implicit-feedback derivative formula;
6. applications to Horn logic, fixed-point logics, categorical recursion, dynamical systems, and recursive computation.

The central claim is that **causality is best understood as a recursively closed morphismic relation between structures**, while temporal order is a possible derived representation rather than a foundational ingredient.

**Keywords:** causality, category theory, recursion, traced monoidal categories, fixed points, coalgebra, dynamical systems, logic, denotational semantics.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Motivation

Most mathematical accounts of causality are tied to one of three interpretations:

- **temporal:** causes precede effects in time;
- **probabilistic:** causes alter conditional probabilities;
- **interventionist:** causes are variables manipulated by external agents.

Each is useful, but each is also restrictive. In logic, proofs generate conclusions without physical time. In category theory, algebraic structures are generated from operations and equations. In programming semantics, recursive definitions determine meanings through fixed points. In dynamical systems, feedback loops produce causal relations that are not naturally ordered by a global time variable.

We therefore require a more primitive notion:

> **Causality is a structural relation by which one object generates, determines, or transforms another through morphisms that are closed under recursive composition.**

This is the starting point of **Recursive Structural Causality**.

### 1.2 Foundational principles

RSCa is organized around three principles.

#### Principle 1: Structural thesis

Causal relations are morphisms in an abstract category of structures. If \(A\) and \(B\) are structures, a causal relation from \(A\) to \(B\) is represented by a morphism

\[
f : A \longrightarrow B
\]

belonging to a distinguished class of **causal morphisms**.

#### Principle 2: Recursive thesis

Causal morphisms are closed under recursive generation. If a causal morphism is defined by an equation

\[
f = \Phi(f),
\]

where \(\Phi\) is itself built from causal operations, then the least solution

\[
f = \mu x.\, \Phi(x)
\]

is again a causal morphism.

#### Principle 3: Atemporal thesis

The recursion index, derivation depth, or fixed-point approximation is not intrinsically temporal. It is an order-theoretic or structural measure of generation. Temporal causality may be recovered as a special representation, but it is not primitive.

### 1.3 Scope

The framework is intentionally general. It applies whenever structures are generated by rules, operations, transitions, proofs, programs, or transformations. The main examples treated here are:

- logical consequence and proof generation;
- categorical algebra and coalgebra;
- discrete and smooth dynamical systems;
- recursive programs, dataflow networks, and process semantics.

## 2. Preliminaries

### 2.1 Pointed \(\omega\)-complete partial orders

Let \(\mathbf{CPO}_{\bot}\) denote the category of pointed \(\omega\)-complete partial orders and strict continuous functions. An object \(D\) has a least element

\[
\bot_D \in D,
\]

and every \(\omega\)-chain

\[
d_0 \le d_1 \le d_2 \le \cdots
\]

has a supremum

\[
\bigsqcup_{n < \omega} d_n.
\]

A function \(F : D \to E\) is continuous if it preserves such suprema:

\[
F\left(\bigsqcup_n d_n\right)
=
\bigsqcup_n F(d_n).
\]

In RSCa, the order on morphisms represents **approximation of causal information**. If

\[
f \le g : A \to B,
\]

then \(f\) carries no more causal information than \(g\). The bottom morphism

\[
\bot_{A,B} : A \to B
\]

represents absence of causal determination.

---

### 2.2 Enriched monoidal categories

A category \(\mathcal{C}\) is enriched over \(\mathbf{CPO}_{\bot}\) if each hom-set

\[
\mathcal{C}(A,B)
\]

is a pointed \(\omega\)-cpo and composition

\[
\circ : \mathcal{C}(B,C) \times \mathcal{C}(A,B) \longrightarrow \mathcal{C}(A,C)
\]

is continuous in each variable.

A symmetric monoidal structure

\[
(\mathcal{C}, \otimes, I, \sigma)
\]

is \(\mathbf{CPO}_{\bot}\)-enriched if the tensor product on homs

\[
\otimes : \mathcal{C}(A,B) \times \mathcal{C}(A',B')
\longrightarrow
\mathcal{C}(A \otimes A', B \otimes B')
\]

is continuous. We require the monoidal unit \(I\) and symmetry

\[
\sigma_{A,B} : A \otimes B \longrightarrow B \otimes A.
\]

Throughout, we write composition diagrammatically from left to right when convenient, but equations use standard categorical notation.

---

### 2.3 Traced monoidal categories

A traced monoidal category is a symmetric monoidal category equipped with a trace operator

\[
\operatorname{Tr}^X_{A,B} :
\mathcal{C}(A \otimes X, B \otimes X)
\longrightarrow
\mathcal{C}(A,B)
\]

satisfying the standard trace axioms.

For morphisms of appropriate types:

1. **Naturality**

\[
\operatorname{Tr}^X(k \circ f \circ (h \otimes \mathrm{id}_X))
=
k \circ \operatorname{Tr}^X(f) \circ h.
\]

2. **Dinaturality**

For \(f : A \otimes Y \to B \otimes X\) and \(h : X \to Y\),

\[
\operatorname{Tr}^Y(f \circ (\mathrm{id}_A \otimes h))
=
\operatorname{Tr}^X((\mathrm{id}_B \otimes h) \circ f).
\]

3. **Vanishing**

\[
\operatorname{Tr}^I(f) = f,
\]

and

\[
\operatorname{Tr}^{X \otimes Y}(f)
=
\operatorname{Tr}^X(\operatorname{Tr}^Y(f)).
\]

4. **Superposing**

\[
\operatorname{Tr}^X(f \otimes g)
=
\operatorname{Tr}^X(f) \otimes g.
\]

5. **Yanking**

\[
\operatorname{Tr}^X(\sigma_{X,X})
=
\mathrm{id}_X.
\]

The trace abstracts feedback. In RSCa, it is the basic mechanism by which recursive causal loops are interpreted without requiring temporal ordering.

## 3. Recursive Causal Categories

### 3.1 Causal signatures

A **monoidal causal signature** is a pair

\[
\Sigma = (T, G),
\]

where:

- \(T\) is a set of base types;
- \(G\) is a set of generators, each of the form

\[
g : A_g \longrightarrow B_g,
\]

where \(A_g\) and \(B_g\) are monoidal types generated from \(T\) by \(\otimes\) and the unit \(I\).

A generator \(g\) is an elementary causal rule. For example,

\[
g : A \otimes B \longrightarrow C
\]

says that the structure \(C\) is causally generated from \(A\) and \(B\).

---

### 3.2 Recursive structural causal categories

A **recursive structural causal category**, or **RSCa category**, is a tuple

\[
(\mathcal{C}, \mathbf{Cau}, \operatorname{Tr})
\]

such that:

1. \(\mathcal{C}\) is a symmetric monoidal category enriched over \(\mathbf{CPO}_{\bot}\);
2. \(\mathbf{Cau}\) is a wide sub-enrichment of \(\mathcal{C}\), i.e. for every pair of objects \(A,B\),

\[
\mathbf{Cau}(A,B) \subseteq \mathcal{C}(A,B)
\]

is a pointed sub-\(\omega\)-cpo;
3. identities belong to \(\mathbf{Cau}\);
4. \(\mathbf{Cau}\) is closed under composition and tensor product;
5. the trace operator restricts to causal morphisms:

\[
\operatorname{Tr}^X_{A,B} :
\mathbf{Cau}(A \otimes X, B \otimes X)
\longrightarrow
\mathbf{Cau}(A,B);
\]

6. \(\mathbf{Cau}\) is closed under least fixed points of continuous causal functionals.

A morphism belonging to \(\mathbf{Cau}(A,B)\) is called a **causal morphism**.

---

### 3.3 Recursive causal morphisms

Let

\[
\Phi : \mathbf{Cau}(A,B) \longrightarrow \mathbf{Cau}(A,B)
\]

be a continuous functional. A morphism \(f : A \to B\) is a **recursive causal morphism** if it is a fixed point

\[
f = \Phi(f).
\]

The canonical recursive causal morphism determined by \(\Phi\) is its least fixed point

\[
\mu \Phi
=
\bigsqcup_{n < \omega} \Phi^n(\bot_{A,B}).
\]

The morphism \(\mu \Phi\) is the causal morphism generated by iterating the recursive rule \(\Phi\) from no prior causal information.

---

### 3.4 Existence theorem

**Theorem 3.1 — Existence of recursive causal morphisms.**  
Let \(\mathcal{C}\) be an RSCa category and let

\[
\Phi : \mathbf{Cau}(A,B) \to \mathbf{Cau}(A,B)
\]

be continuous. Then \(\Phi\) has a least fixed point

\[
\mu \Phi \in \mathbf{Cau}(A,B),
\]

given by

\[
\mu \Phi
=
\bigsqcup_{n < \omega} \Phi^n(\bot_{A,B}).
\]

Moreover, \(\mu \Phi\) is the least pre-fixed point of \(\Phi\): if \(\Phi(f) \le f\), then

\[
\mu \Phi \le f.
\]

**Proof.**  
Define the chain

\[
f_0 = \bot_{A,B},
\qquad
f_{n+1} = \Phi(f_n).
\]

Since \(\Phi\) is monotone and \(\bot\) is least,

\[
f_0 \le f_1 \le f_2 \le \cdots.
\]

Because \(\mathbf{Cau}(A,B)\) is an \(\omega\)-cpo, the supremum

\[
f_\infty = \bigsqcup_n f_n
\]

exists. By continuity,

\[
\Phi(f_\infty)
=
\Phi\left(\bigsqcup_n f_n\right)
=
\bigsqcup_n \Phi(f_n)
=
\bigsqcup_n f_{n+1}
=
f_\infty.
\]

Thus \(f_\infty\) is a fixed point. If \(g\) is any pre-fixed point, \(\Phi(g) \le g\), then by induction \(f_n \le g\) for all \(n\), hence \(f_\infty \le g\). Therefore \(f_\infty\) is least. \(\square\)

---

### 3.5 Uniqueness under contraction

Least fixed points give canonical solutions, but not necessarily unique solutions. Uniqueness can be obtained under contractive conditions.

Suppose each hom \(\mathbf{Cau}(A,B)\) is additionally equipped with a complete metric \(d\), and composition and tensor are nonexpansive. A functional \(\Phi\) is **contractive** if there exists \(0 \le c < 1\) such that

\[
d(\Phi(f), \Phi(g)) \le c\, d(f,g)
\]

for all \(f,g\).

**Theorem 3.2 — Contractive uniqueness.**  
If \(\Phi : \mathbf{Cau}(A,B) \to \mathbf{Cau}(A,B)\) is contractive and continuous, then \(\Phi\) has a unique fixed point, which coincides with \(\mu \Phi\).

**Proof.**  
By the Banach fixed-point theorem adapted to complete metric spaces, a contraction has a unique fixed point. Since \(\Phi\) is continuous, the least fixed point exists by Theorem 3.1. Any fixed point is a pre-fixed point, hence lies above \(\mu \Phi\). But uniqueness forces equality. \(\square\)

Contractivity arises naturally from guarded recursion, delay operators, and stable dynamical feedback.

## 4. Syntax of Recursive Causal Morphisms

We now give a formal language for recursive structural causality.

### 4.1 Types

Given a causal signature \(\Sigma = (T,G)\), the types are generated by

\[
A,B ::= I \mid X \mid A \otimes B,
\]

where \(X \in T\).

---

### 4.2 Terms

For each arrow type \(A \to B\), terms are generated by the grammar

\[
t ::=
\bot_{A,B}
\mid
\mathrm{id}_A
\mid
\sigma_{A,B}
\mid
g
\mid
x
\mid
t_2 \circ t_1
\mid
t_1 \otimes t_2
\mid
\operatorname{Tr}^X(t)
\mid
\operatorname{fix}\, x^{A \to B}.\, t.
\]

Here:

- \(g \in G\) is a primitive causal generator;
- \(x\) is a variable of specified arrow type;
- \(\bot_{A,B}\) is the null causal morphism;
- \(\sigma_{A,B}\) is the symmetry;
- \(\circ\) is composition;
- \(\otimes\) is parallel composition;
- \(\operatorname{Tr}^X\) is feedback;
- \(\operatorname{fix}\, x^{A \to B}.\, t\) is recursive definition.

---

### 4.3 Equational theory

The equational theory of RSCa consists of:

1. the axioms of symmetric monoidal categories;
2. the axioms of traced monoidal categories;
3. strictness of bottom:

\[
\bot \circ f = \bot,
\qquad
f \circ \bot = \bot;
\]

4. unfolding:

\[
\operatorname{fix}\, x.\, t
=
t[\operatorname{fix}\, x.\, t / x];
\]

5. fixed-point induction:

if

\[
y = t[y/x],
\]

then

\[
\operatorname{fix}\, x.\, t \le y.
\]

The order \(\le\) is interpreted as causal approximation.

---

### 4.4 Simultaneous recursion

A finite system of recursive causal equations

\[
x_i = t_i(x_1,\dots,x_n),
\qquad
x_i : A_i \to B_i,
\]

is interpreted as a single fixed point on the product

\[
\prod_{i=1}^n \mathbf{Cau}(A_i,B_i).
\]

Because products of pointed \(\omega\)-cpos are pointed \(\omega\)-cpos, simultaneous recursive definitions have least solutions under continuity.

---

### 4.5 Free RSCa category

Let \(\mathbf{RSCa}(\Sigma)\) be the category whose:

- objects are monoidal types generated from \(T\);
- morphisms are equivalence classes of RSCa terms modulo the equational theory;
- hom-orders are generated by the approximation order.

Then \(\mathbf{RSCa}(\Sigma)\) is an RSCa category.

**Theorem 4.1 — Universal property.**  
For every RSCa category \(\mathcal{C}\) and every interpretation of the generators \(g \in G\) as causal morphisms in \(\mathcal{C}\), there exists a unique continuous traced monoidal functor

\[
\llbracket - \rrbracket :
\mathbf{RSCa}(\Sigma)
\longrightarrow
\mathcal{C}
\]

preserving identities, composition, tensor, trace, bottom, and recursive fixed points.

**Proof sketch.**  
Interpret each term recursively:

- generators by the assigned semantic morphisms;
- composition, tensor, symmetry, and trace by the corresponding structure in \(\mathcal{C}\);
- \(\bot\) by the bottom morphism;
- \(\operatorname{fix}\, x.\, t\) by the least fixed point of the continuous functional induced by \(t\).

Soundness of the equational theory follows from the trace axioms, continuity, and fixed-point induction. Uniqueness follows because every morphism in the free category is generated by these operations. \(\square\)

## 5. Structural Generation: Algebras, Coalgebras, and Hylomorphisms

RSCa is not only a theory of morphisms; it is a theory of structures generating structures.

---

### 5.1 Causal endofunctors

Let \(\mathcal{C}\) be an RSCa category. An endofunctor

\[
F : \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{C}
\]

is **causal** if:

1. it maps objects to objects;
2. it maps causal morphisms to causal morphisms;
3. it is locally continuous:

\[
F\left(\bigsqcup_n f_n\right)
=
\bigsqcup_n F(f_n)
\]

for every increasing chain of causal morphisms.

A typical polynomial causal functor has the form

\[
F(X)
=
\coprod_{\sigma \in \Sigma}
B_\sigma \otimes X^{\otimes n_\sigma}.
\]

Here each operation symbol \(\sigma\) generates a new structure \(B_\sigma\) from \(n_\sigma\) recursive substructures.

---

### 5.2 Causal algebras and coalgebras

An **\(F\)-causal algebra** is a pair

\[
(A, \alpha)
\]

with \(\alpha : F A \to A\) a causal morphism.

An **\(F\)-causal coalgebra** is a pair

\[
(S, \xi)
\]

with \(\xi : S \to F S\) a causal morphism.

Intuitively:

- an algebra \(\alpha : F A \to A\) assembles a structure from immediate causal constituents;
- a coalgebra \(\xi : S \to F S\) decomposes a structure into immediate causal constituents.

---

### 5.3 Recursive structural transformations

Given:

- an \(F\)-coalgebra \(\xi : S \to F S\);
- an \(F\)-algebra \(\alpha : F T \to T\);

a morphism

\[
h : S \to T
\]

is an **\(F\)-recursive causal transformation** if it satisfies

\[
h
=
\alpha \circ F(h) \circ \xi.
\]

This equation says:

1. decompose the source \(S\) into immediate causal constituents via \(\xi\);
2. recursively transform those constituents via \(F(h)\);
3. assemble the target \(T\) via \(\alpha\).

Thus \(h\) is a structurally generated causal morphism.

---

### 5.4 Hylomorphisms as recursive causal morphisms

Define the functional

\[
\Phi_{\alpha,\xi} :
\mathbf{Cau}(S,T)
\longrightarrow
\mathbf{Cau}(S,T)
\]

by

\[
\Phi_{\alpha,\xi}(h)
=
\alpha \circ F(h) \circ \xi.
\]

If \(F\) is locally continuous, then \(\Phi_{\alpha,\xi}\) is continuous. Hence the least solution exists.

**Definition 5.1.**  
The **hylomorphism** generated by \((\alpha,\xi)\) is

\[
\operatorname{hylo}(\alpha,\xi)
=
\mu h.\, \alpha \circ F(h) \circ \xi.
\]

It is the least recursive causal transformation from \((S,\xi)\) to \((T,\alpha)\).

---

### 5.5 Approximation theorem

**Theorem 5.1 — Hylomorphic approximation.**  
Let \(\xi : S \to F S\) and \(\alpha : F T \to T\) be causal. Define

\[
h_0 = \bot_{S,T},
\qquad
h_{n+1}
=
\alpha \circ F(h_n) \circ \xi.
\]

Then

\[
\operatorname{hylo}(\alpha,\xi)
=
\bigsqcup_{n < \omega} h_n.
\]

**Proof.**  
This is exactly Kleene iteration for the continuous functional

\[
h \mapsto \alpha \circ F(h) \circ \xi.
\]

By Theorem 3.1, the supremum of the chain is the least fixed point. \(\square\)

This theorem gives the constructive content of recursive structural causality: each \(h_n\) represents causal generation up to depth \(n\).

---

### 5.6 Initial algebras and final coalgebras

When \(F\) has an initial algebra

\[
(\mu F, \mathrm{in} : F(\mu F) \to \mu F),
\]

recursive generation corresponds to structural induction.

For any \(F\)-algebra \((A,\alpha)\), there is a unique causal morphism

\[
\operatorname{fold}(\alpha) : \mu F \to A
\]

such that

\[
\operatorname{fold}(\alpha) \circ \mathrm{in}
=
\alpha \circ F(\operatorname{fold}(\alpha)).
\]

Dually, if \(F\) has a final coalgebra

\[
(\nu F, \mathrm{out} : \nu F \to F(\nu F)),
\]

then for any \(F\)-coalgebra \((S,\xi)\), there is a unique causal morphism

\[
\operatorname{unfold}(\xi) : S \to \nu F
\]

such that

\[
\mathrm{out} \circ \operatorname{unfold}(\xi)
=
F(\operatorname{unfold}(\xi)) \circ \xi.
\]

Initial algebras model finite recursive generation; final coalgebras model potentially infinite coinductive generation.

---

### 5.7 Causal bisimulation

Let

\[
\xi : X \to F X,
\qquad
\zeta : Y \to F Y
\]

be \(F\)-coalgebras. A relation \(R \subseteq X \times Y\) is an **\(F\)-causal bisimulation** if related states generate related one-step causal decompositions.

In the final coalgebra \((\nu F, \mathrm{out})\), bisimilar states are identified:

\[
x \sim y
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\operatorname{unfold}(\xi)(x)
=
\operatorname{unfold}(\zeta)(y).
\]

Thus RSCa supports a structural theory of observational equivalence for recursively generated causal systems.

## 6. Tensorial Calculus of Causal Influence

In differentiable settings, RSCa admits a tensorial calculus describing how perturbations propagate through recursive causal structures.

Assume \(\mathcal{C}\) is a differentiable RSCa category, for example a category of smooth manifolds, Euclidean spaces, or Banach manifolds. For a causal morphism

\[
f : A \to B,
\]

its derivative is a linear causal morphism

\[
Df : TA \to TB.
\]

In local coordinates \(a^\alpha\) on \(A\) and \(b^i\) on \(B\), the **causal influence tensor** of \(f\) is

\[
J[f]^i{}_{\alpha}
=
\frac{\partial b^i}{\partial a^\alpha}.
\]

This tensor expresses the first-order influence of variations in the input structure on variations in the output structure.

---

### 6.1 Recursive iteration and sensitivity

Let

\[
f : M \to M
\]

be a causal endomorphism. Define recursively

\[
x_{n+1} = f(x_n).
\]

The \(n\)-fold composite is

\[
f^n : M \to M.
\]

The sensitivity tensor of the \(n\)-step recursive causal transformation is

\[
C_n = D(f^n).
\]

In coordinates,

\[
(C_0)^i{}_j = \delta^i{}_j,
\]

and

\[
(C_{n+1})^i{}_j
=
\frac{\partial f^i}{\partial x^k}(x_n)
(C_n)^k{}_j.
\]

Thus causal influence propagates recursively by tensor contraction.

The integer \(n\) need not represent physical time. It may represent proof depth, computation depth, unfolding depth, or any other recursive generation parameter.

---

### 6.2 Implicit feedback

Consider a recursive feedback system specified by

\[
x = F_X(a,x),
\qquad
b = F_B(a,x),
\]

where

\[
F_X : A \times X \to X,
\qquad
F_B : A \times X \to B.
\]

Equivalently, define

\[
F : A \otimes X \longrightarrow X \otimes B
\]

by

\[
F(a,x) = (F_X(a,x), F_B(a,x)).
\]

The traced causal morphism

\[
G = \operatorname{Tr}^X(F) : A \to B
\]

is given, when the fixed point exists uniquely, by

\[
G(a) = F_B(a, x(a)),
\]

where

\[
x(a) = F_X(a, x(a)).
\]

Assume the implicit equation is locally solvable and the tensor

\[
M^p{}_q
=
\delta^p{}_q
-
\frac{\partial F_X^p}{\partial x^q}
\]

is invertible. Differentiating the fixed-point equations gives

\[
db^i
=
\frac{\partial F_B^i}{\partial a^\alpha} da^\alpha
+
\frac{\partial F_B^i}{\partial x^p} dx^p,
\]

and

\[
dx^p
=
\frac{\partial F_X^p}{\partial a^\alpha} da^\alpha
+
\frac{\partial F_X^p}{\partial x^q} dx^q.
\]

Hence

\[
\left(
\delta^p{}_q
-
\frac{\partial F_X^p}{\partial x^q}
\right)
dx^q
=
\frac{\partial F_X^p}{\partial a^\alpha} da^\alpha.
\]

Therefore

\[
dx^p
=
(M^{-1})^p{}_q
\frac{\partial F_X^q}{\partial a^\alpha}
da^\alpha.
\]

Substitution yields the recursive causal influence tensor of the traced morphism:

\[
\boxed{
\frac{\partial G^i}{\partial a^\alpha}
=
\frac{\partial F_B^i}{\partial a^\alpha}
+
\frac{\partial F_B^i}{\partial x^p}
(M^{-1})^p{}_q
\frac{\partial F_X^q}{\partial a^\alpha}
}
\]

or, invariantly,

\[
DG
=
D_a F_B
+
D_x F_B
\,
(I - D_x F_X)^{-1}
\,
D_a F_X.
\]

This is the central tensorial formula of recursive structural causality in differentiable feedback systems. It expresses the total causal influence of \(A\) on \(B\) through a recursively closed channel \(X\).

---

### 6.3 Linear feedback systems

As a special case, let the recursive equations be linear:

\[
x = A x + B u,
\qquad
y = C x + D u.
\]

Here:

- \(u\) is the input structure;
- \(x\) is the recursively determined internal structure;
- \(y\) is the output structure.

If \(I - A\) is invertible, then

\[
x = (I - A)^{-1} B u,
\]

and therefore

\[
y
=
\left(
D + C(I - A)^{-1}B
\right) u.
\]

In index notation,

\[
y^a
=
D^a{}_\alpha u^\alpha
+
C^a{}_p
\left((I - A)^{-1}\right)^p{}_q
B^q{}_\alpha
u^\alpha.
\]

Thus the total causal morphism from \(u\) to \(y\) is

\[
\boxed{
D + C(I-A)^{-1}B
}
\]

This is the atemporal structural content of many feedback, control, and network equations. Temporal transfer functions arise only after additional representation, for example by introducing a shift operator.

## 7. Acyclicity, Well-Foundedness, and Causal Depth

### 7.1 Dependency graphs

Given a recursive system

\[
x_i = t_i(x_1,\dots,x_n),
\]

define its **dependency graph** as follows:

- vertices are the variables \(x_i\);
- there is an edge \(x_j \to x_i\) if \(x_j\) occurs freely in \(t_i\).

A recursive causal system is **acyclic** if this graph has no directed cycles. It is **well-founded** if it has no infinite dependency chain.

---

### 7.2 Well-founded recursion

**Theorem 7.1 — Well-founded collapse.**  
If a finite recursive causal system is acyclic, then its least fixed point is obtained by finite substitution along a topological ordering.

More generally, if the dependency relation is well-founded with ordinal rank \(\rho\), then the least fixed point is reached by transfinite iteration at stage \(\rho\).

**Proof sketch.**  
In the acyclic finite case, choose a topological ordering of variables. Each \(x_i\) depends only on previously solved variables, so substitution yields a finite term. In the well-founded transfinite case, assign to each variable the ordinal rank of its longest descending dependency chain. Iterating the recursive functional through the ordinals stabilizes at the supremum of these ranks. \(\square\)

Thus acyclic causality is a special case of recursive structural causality.

---

### 7.3 Cyclic causality

RSCa permits cyclic dependency graphs. A cycle

\[
x_1 \to x_2 \to \cdots \to x_1
\]

does not necessarily represent a temporal paradox. It represents a recursive constraint.

The least fixed point gives the minimal consistent causal interpretation. Greatest fixed points, when available, give maximal coinductive interpretations. Contractive or invertibility conditions give unique interpretations.

Thus RSCa treats cycles as fixed-point constraints rather than temporal inconsistencies.

---

### 7.4 Causal depth

For a recursive causal morphism \(f = \mu \Phi\), define its **causal depth** as the least ordinal \(\delta\) such that

\[
\Phi^\delta(\bot)
=
\Phi^{\delta+1}(\bot).
\]

When the depth is finite, the morphism is generated by a finite recursive derivation. When the depth is infinite, the morphism represents a coinductive or infinite causal structure.

Causal depth replaces temporal duration by structural generation depth.

---

### 7.5 Causal invariants

Let \(P_A \subseteq A\) be a subobject representing a property of structures. A causal morphism \(f : A \to B\) **preserves** \(P\) if

\[
f(P_A) \subseteq P_B.
\]

Suppose:

1. all generators preserve \(P\);
2. \(P\) is preserved by composition, tensor, and trace;
3. \(P\) is closed under directed suprema.

Then every recursive causal morphism generated by the signature preserves \(P\).

**Theorem 7.2 — Invariance under recursive causality.**  
Under the above assumptions, if \(f\) is a recursive causal morphism, then \(f\) preserves \(P\).

**Proof.**  
Let \(f = \bigsqcup_n f_n\) be the Kleene chain. Since \(\bot\) preserves \(P\), and the operations generating \(\Phi\) preserve \(P\), induction gives that each \(f_n\) preserves \(P\). Closure under directed suprema implies that \(f\) preserves \(P\). \(\square\)

This provides a general mechanism for conservation laws, safety properties, and structural invariants in recursive causal systems.

## 8. Applications

We now develop four major application domains.

---

# 8.1 Logic

## 8.1.1 Horn logic as recursive structural causality

Let \(P\) be a set of propositional atoms. A Horn clause has the form

\[
H \; :- \; B_1,\dots,B_n.
\]

Interpret each atom as a type or object, and interpret the clause as a causal generator

\[
B_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes B_n \longrightarrow H.
\]

Thus logical inference becomes causal generation.

Define the immediate consequence operator

\[
T_P : 2^P \to 2^P
\]

by

\[
T_P(S)
=
\left\{
H \in P
\;\middle|\;
\exists (H :- B_1,\dots,B_n),\;
\{B_1,\dots,B_n\} \subseteq S
\right\}.
\]

The operator \(T_P\) is monotone:

\[
S \subseteq S'
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
T_P(S) \subseteq T_P(S').
\]

Because Horn clauses have finite bodies, \(T_P\) is \(\omega\)-continuous. Hence its least fixed point exists:

\[
M_P
=
\mu T_P
=
\bigsqcup_{n < \omega} T_P^n(\varnothing).
\]

**Theorem 8.1 — Least Herbrand model as recursive causality.**  
The least fixed point \(M_P\) is the set of atoms possessing finite proof trees under the Horn clauses.

**Proof.**  
We prove by induction on \(n\) that \(T_P^n(\varnothing)\) is the set of atoms with proof trees of height at most \(n\).

Base case: \(n=0\). \(T_P^0(\varnothing)=\varnothing\), corresponding to atoms with proof height \(0\), of which there are none unless facts are treated as nullary clauses. If facts are included as clauses with empty body, they appear in \(T_P(\varnothing)\).

Inductive step: suppose the claim holds for \(n\). An atom \(H\) lies in \(T_P^{n+1}(\varnothing)\) iff there is a clause

\[
H :- B_1,\dots,B_k
\]

such that each \(B_i \in T_P^n(\varnothing)\). By induction, each \(B_i\) has a proof tree of height at most \(n\). Attaching these trees beneath \(H\) gives a proof tree for \(H\) of height at most \(n+1\). Conversely, every such proof tree yields membership in \(T_P^{n+1}(\varnothing)\). Taking the union over \(n\) gives exactly finite proof trees. \(\square\)

Thus Horn consequence is a canonical instance of recursive structural causality.

---

## 8.1.2 Fixed-point logics

In modal \(\mu\)-calculus and related fixed-point logics, formulas are generated by least and greatest fixed-point operators:

\[
\mu X.\, \varphi(X),
\qquad
\nu X.\, \varphi(X).
\]

Given a transition structure, a formula \(\varphi(X)\) positive in \(X\) defines a monotone operator

\[
F_\varphi : 2^S \to 2^S.
\]

The semantics is

\[
\llbracket \mu X.\varphi \rrbracket
=
\mu F_\varphi,
\]

and

\[
\llbracket \nu X.\varphi \rrbracket
=
\nu F_\varphi.
\]

RSCa interprets these fixed points as recursive causal generation over the state structure. The least fixed point captures finite causal derivability; the greatest fixed point captures coinductive invariance.

# 8.2 Category Theory

## 8.2.1 RSCa as a theory of recursive traced monoidal categories

RSCa categories combine several categorical structures:

- enrichment over domains;
- symmetric monoidal structure;
- trace;
- fixed-point closure.

This places RSCa in relation to:

- traced monoidal categories;
- Conway categories and iteration theories;
- domain theory;
- algebraic theories of recursion.

The free RSCa category over a signature provides a universal algebra of causal recursion.

---

## 8.2.2 Operadic interpretation

A causal rule with multiple inputs and one output,

\[
A_1,\dots,A_n \longrightarrow B,
\]

is naturally an operadic operation. Recursive closure under composition gives an operad of causal morphisms. Trace adds feedback, leading to cyclic or traced operadic structures.

Thus RSCa may be understood as an operadic theory of recursively composable causal operations.

---

## 8.2.3 Monads and algebraic theories

Given a causal signature \(\Sigma\), the free RSCa construction determines a monadic structure on the category of generated causal theories. A model of the theory in an RSCa category \(\mathcal{C}\) is a structure-preserving interpretation of the generators.

In particular, ordinary algebraic theories appear as special cases where recursion is absent or well-founded. RSCa extends them by explicit feedback and fixed-point generation.

---

# 8.3 Dynamical Systems

## 8.3.1 Discrete systems as causal endomorphisms

Let \(M\) be a state space and

\[
f : M \to M
\]

a causal endomorphism. The recursively generated orbit structure is given by the iterates

\[
f^n : M \to M.
\]

No temporal interpretation is required. The integer \(n\) may denote derivation depth, scale, or recursive unfolding.

The sensitivity tensors satisfy

\[
C_0 = \mathrm{id},
\qquad
C_{n+1}
=
Df(x_n) \circ C_n.
\]

In coordinates,

\[
(C_{n+1})^i{}_j
=
\frac{\partial f^i}{\partial x^k}(x_n)
(C_n)^k{}_j.
\]

This gives a tensorial theory of propagated causal influence.

---

## 8.3.2 Feedback systems

A feedback system

\[
x = F_X(a,x),
\qquad
b = F_B(a,x)
\]

is interpreted as a traced causal morphism

\[
\operatorname{Tr}^X(F) : A \to B.
\]

When the fixed point is locally unique, the total causal influence is

\[
DG
=
D_a F_B
+
D_x F_B
(I - D_x F_X)^{-1}
D_a F_X.
\]

This formula unifies:

- algebraic feedback;
- implicit equations;
- control-theoretic block diagram reduction;
- recursive network propagation.

---

## 8.3.3 Linear systems

For the linear algebraic feedback system

\[
x = A x + B u,
\qquad
y = C x + D u,
\]

the total input-output causal morphism is

\[
y
=
\left(
D + C(I-A)^{-1}B
\right)u.
\]

This is the RSCa reduction of a recursive causal loop to an effective causal morphism.

If a shift operator \(z\) is introduced, one obtains the familiar transfer function

\[
D + C(zI - A)^{-1}B.
\]

In RSCa, the transfer function is a temporal representation of an underlying atemporal recursive structure.

# 8.4 Theoretical Computer Science

## 8.4.1 Recursive programs

Let \(\Sigma\) be a state space. The denotation of a while loop

\[
\texttt{while } b \texttt{ do } c
\]

is a least fixed point on a domain of partial state transformers.

Let

\[
D = [\Sigma_\bot \to \Sigma_\bot]
\]

be a pointed domain of partial functions. Define

\[
\Phi : D \to D
\]

by

\[
\Phi(F)(s)
=
\begin{cases}
F(c(s)), & b(s) = \mathrm{true}, \\
s, & b(s) = \mathrm{false}.
\end{cases}
\]

The semantics is

\[
\llbracket \texttt{while } b \texttt{ do } c \rrbracket
=
\mu \Phi.
\]

The recursive self-call is not a temporal primitive; it is a structural causal dependency of the denotation on itself.

---

## 8.4.2 Dataflow networks

In Kahn-style dataflow semantics, a network is specified by equations

\[
x_i = f_i(\text{inputs to } x_i).
\]

The denotation is the least fixed point of a monotone operator on a domain of streams.

The dependency graph of the equations determines structural causality. Cycles are interpreted by least fixed points. Thus dataflow networks are concrete recursive causal systems.

---

## 8.4.3 Process algebra and concurrency

Process calculi often define recursive processes by equations

\[
P = \mathcal{F}(P).
\]

The semantic object is a fixed point in a domain of processes, transition systems, or event structures. RSCa provides an abstract morphismic account of such recursive process generation.

Causal dependencies between events can be recovered from the dependency graph of recursive definitions, while RSCa additionally allows quotienting by structural equations.

## 9. Relation to Existing Causal Frameworks

### 9.1 Structural causal models

A classical structural causal model consists of variables \(V_i\) and equations

\[
V_i = f_i(\mathrm{Pa}_i, U_i).
\]

When the dependency graph is acyclic, this is a well-founded recursive causal system. In RSCa:

- variables are objects;
- structural equations are causal generators;
- interventions are morphisms in slice categories or replacements of generators;
- acyclic models correspond to finite well-founded recursion;
- cyclic models require fixed-point semantics.

Thus RSCa generalizes acyclic structural causal models and gives a systematic semantics for cyclic and recursively defined causal systems.

---

### 9.2 Event structures and partial-order causality

Event-structure models treat causality as a partial order on events. RSCa abstracts further: events become objects or morphisms, and causal dependency becomes morphismic generation.

For well-founded recursive causal terms, the dependency graph recovers a partial-order structure. RSCa additionally supports:

- quotienting by equations;
- feedback through trace;
- coinductive infinite causality;
- tensorial parallel composition.

---

### 9.3 Probabilistic causality

RSCa is not intrinsically probabilistic. However, it can be combined with probabilistic categories by enriching homs in spaces of stochastic maps, measures, or Markov kernels. Recursive structural causality then provides the underlying dependency and fixed-point structure on top of probabilistic semantics.

---

## 10. Conclusion

We have introduced **Recursive Structural Causality (RSCa)**, a categorical framework in which causality is defined as a recursively closed morphismic relation between structures. The central constructions are:

1. **recursive causal categories**, combining domain enrichment, monoidal structure, and trace;
2. **recursive causal morphisms**, defined as least fixed points of continuous causal functionals;
3. **hylomorphic generation**, in which structures are decomposed into causes and reassembled into effects;
4. **tensorial influence calculus**, giving explicit derivative formulas for recursive feedback;
5. **applications** to logic, category theory, dynamical systems, and computation.

The framework separates causality from time. Temporal succession is not denied; it is reinterpreted as one possible representation of recursive structural generation. This permits a unified treatment of proofs, programs, dynamical feedback, and algebraic generation under a single mathematical theory.

Future work includes:

- higher-dimensional and \(\infty\)-categorical RSCa;
- probabilistic and quantum RSCa;
- geometric and metric theories of causal influence;
- applications to machine learning architectures and causal discovery;
- proof theory and type systems for recursive causal logics.
