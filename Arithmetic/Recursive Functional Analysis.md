# Recursive Functional Analysis

## Co-Evolving Banach and Hilbert Spaces, Norms, Operators, and Spectra

**Preprint**

---

## Abstract

Classical functional analysis is normally organized around a fixed ambient space \(X\), a norm \(\|\cdot\|_X\), and one or more operators acting on \(X\). This paper develops a different paradigm, **Recursive Functional Analysis** (RFA), in which the function space itself is generated recursively. Starting from a seed Banach or Hilbert space \(X_0\), one constructs a sequence

\[
X_{n+1}=\Phi(X_n),
\]

where \(\Phi\) is a space-generating map or functor. Crucially, the norm, the distinguished operators, and their spectra are not held fixed: they evolve together with the spaces. We formulate a general theory of recursive Banach and Hilbert systems, prove basic existence, stability, and spectral-mapping results, and introduce a tensorial formalism for recursive metrics. The central Hilbert construction uses graph-type inner products

\[
\langle u,v\rangle_{n+1}
=
\langle u,v\rangle_n
+
\langle B_n u,B_n v\rangle_n,
\]

where \(B_n\) is a self-adjoint operator on \(H_n\). Defining

\[
C_n=(I+B_n^2)^{1/2},
\qquad
H_{n+1}=D(B_n),
\]

one obtains a unitary identification \(C_n:H_{n+1}\to H_n\). For any real-valued continuous function \(f_n\), the recursive operator

\[
B_{n+1}=C_n^{-1} f_n(B_n) C_n
\]

is self-adjoint on \(H_{n+1}\), and its spectrum satisfies

\[
\sigma(B_{n+1})=\overline{f_n(\sigma(B_n))}.
\]

In particular, for \(f_n(t)=\sqrt{1+t^2}\), the eigenvalues obey

\[
\lambda_{n+1,k}^2=\lambda_{n,k}^2+1,
\qquad
\lambda_{n,k}^2=\lambda_{0,k}^2+n.
\]

Thus spaces, norms, operators, and spectra evolve as a coupled recursive system. We develop applications to partial differential equations, quantum mechanics, numerical analysis, and signal processing.

## 1. Introduction

In the standard formulation of linear analysis, one begins with a Banach space \(X\), often a Hilbert space \(H\), and studies operators

\[
A:D(A)\subset X\to X.
\]

The space \(X\) is treated as a fixed background. Sobolev spaces, domains of powers of operators, interpolation spaces, and form domains are then constructed relative to \(X\). While this approach is extremely powerful, it does not fully exploit the possibility that the ambient function space itself may be part of a dynamical or recursive construction.

Recursive Functional Analysis proposes the following shift:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Instead of analyzing operators on a fixed space }X,
\text{ analyze a recursively generated system }
X_{n+1}=\Phi(X_n).
}
\]

The essential point is not merely that one obtains a sequence of spaces. Rather, the norm, the operator, and the spectrum are allowed to co-evolve:

\[
\begin{aligned}
X_{n+1} &= \Phi_n(X_n,A_n),\\
\|\cdot\|_{n+1} &= \mathcal N_n(\|\cdot\|_n,A_n),\\
A_{n+1} &= \Psi_n(A_n,X_n),\\
\sigma_{n+1} &= \Sigma_n(\sigma_n).
\end{aligned}
\]

This leads to a theory in which functional-analytic objects are no longer static but are generated by recursive laws.

The central motivations are as follows.

1. **Partial differential equations.** Regularity naturally appears in towers of spaces. If \(L\) is an elliptic operator, then solving \(Lu=f\) often maps data in one space to solutions in a more regular space. Recursive spaces provide a systematic language for such regularity ladders.

2. **Quantum mechanics.** Hamiltonians often define not only time evolution but also natural energy spaces. Recursive Hilbert spaces allow one to encode higher regularity, renormalization, or scale-dependent effective observables.

3. **Numerical analysis.** Multilevel, multigrid, and adaptive methods implicitly construct hierarchies of spaces and norms. RFA makes this hierarchy explicit and operator-theoretic.

4. **Signal processing.** Multiresolution analysis, filter banks, wavelet packets, and learned recursive transforms naturally produce sequences of representation spaces. RFA provides a functional-analytic foundation for such constructions.

The purpose of this paper is to lay a rigorous foundation for RFA and to demonstrate its structural consequences.

## 2. Recursive Functional Systems

### 2.1. Category-theoretic formulation

Let \(\mathbf{Ban}_1\) denote the category whose objects are Banach spaces and whose morphisms are linear contractions. A **recursive space functor** is an endofunctor

\[
\Phi:\mathbf{Ban}_1\to \mathbf{Ban}_1.
\]

Given an initial Banach space \(X_0\), one obtains a sequence

\[
X_{n+1}=\Phi(X_n),
\qquad n=0,1,2,\dots.
\]

In many applications, however, the recursion depends not only on the space but also on a distinguished operator. We therefore use a more flexible notion.

A **recursive functional system** is a quadruple

\[
\mathcal R=(X_0,A_0,\Phi,\Psi),
\]

where:

1. \(X_0\) is a Banach space;
2. \(A_0:D(A_0)\subset X_0\to X_0\) is a closed, densely defined operator;
3. \(\Phi\) assigns to a pair \((X,A)\) a new Banach space \(\Phi(X,A)\);
4. \(\Psi\) assigns to \((X,A)\) a new closed operator \(A^+=\Psi(X,A)\) acting in \(\Phi(X,A)\).

The recursion is

\[
X_{n+1}=\Phi(X_n,A_n),
\qquad
A_{n+1}=\Psi(X_n,A_n).
\]

We often abbreviate

\[
(X_{n+1},A_{n+1})=\mathcal T(X_n,A_n),
\]

where \(\mathcal T\) is the recursive transformation.

The fundamental difference from ordinary perturbation theory is that the ambient space itself changes at each step.

---

### 2.2. Graph-type recursive Banach spaces

The most basic recursive construction is the graph-space construction.

Let \(X_n\) be a Banach space and let

\[
A_n:D(A_n)\subset X_n\to X_n
\]

be closed and densely defined. For \(1\le p<\infty\), define

\[
X_{n+1}=D(A_n)
\]

with norm

\[
\|x\|_{n+1}
=
\left(
\|x\|_n^p+\|A_nx\|_n^p
\right)^{1/p}.
\]

For \(p=\infty\), define

\[
\|x\|_{n+1}
=
\max\{\|x\|_n,\|A_nx\|_n\}.
\]

This is the **graph recursion**.

---

#### Theorem 2.1 — Banach property of graph recursion

Let \(A_n:D(A_n)\subset X_n\to X_n\) be closed. Then \(X_{n+1}=D(A_n)\), equipped with the graph norm, is a Banach space. The embedding

\[
i_n:X_{n+1}\hookrightarrow X_n
\]

is continuous with

\[
\|i_n\|\le 1.
\]

If \(A_n\) is densely defined, then \(X_{n+1}\) is dense in \(X_n\).

**Proof.** Let \((x_m)\) be Cauchy in \(X_{n+1}\). Then \((x_m)\) is Cauchy in \(X_n\), and \((A_nx_m)\) is Cauchy in \(X_n\). Hence there exist \(x,y\in X_n\) such that

\[
x_m\to x,
\qquad
A_nx_m\to y.
\]

Since \(A_n\) is closed, \(x\in D(A_n)\) and \(A_nx=y\). Therefore \(x\in X_{n+1}\), and

\[
\|x_m-x\|_{n+1}\to 0.
\]

Thus \(X_{n+1}\) is complete.

Moreover,

\[
\|x\|_n\le \|x\|_{n+1},
\]

so the embedding is continuous with norm at most \(1\). Density follows from the density of \(D(A_n)\) in \(X_n\). \(\square\)

---

### 2.3. Stability of recursive norms

A recursive theory is useful only if it is stable under perturbations. The following result gives a basic perturbation estimate.

Let \(A\) and \(\widetilde A\) be closed operators with common domain \(D(A)=D(\widetilde A)\). Suppose that for some constants \(a,b\ge 0\), with \(b<1\),

\[
\|(\widetilde A-A)x\|
\le
a\|x\|+b\|Ax\|,
\qquad x\in D(A).
\]

Define the graph norms

\[
\|x\|_A=\|x\|+\|Ax\|,
\qquad
\|x\|_{\widetilde A}=\|x\|+\|\widetilde Ax\|.
\]

Then

\[
\|x\|_{\widetilde A}
\le
(1+b)\|x\|_A+a\|x\|,
\]

and

\[
(1-b)\|x\|_A-a\|x\|
\le
\|x\|_{\widetilde A}.
\]

In particular, the two graph norms are equivalent.

This result shows that recursive constructions are robust under controlled operator perturbations. It is especially important for numerical discretizations, where \(A_n\) is replaced by a discrete approximation \(A_{n,h}\).

## 3. Recursive Hilbert Spaces

The richest structure appears in the Hilbert setting, where inner products, adjoints, and spectral theory are available.

Let \(H_0\) be a Hilbert space with inner product \(\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_0\). Let

\[
B_0:D(B_0)\subset H_0\to H_0
\]

be self-adjoint. For simplicity, assume \(B_0\ge 0\). The construction below also works for general self-adjoint operators after replacing \(B_n^2\) by \(B_n^*B_n\).

Define

\[
C_0=(I+B_0^2)^{1/2}.
\]

Set

\[
H_1=D(B_0)
\]

with inner product

\[
\langle u,v\rangle_1
=
\langle C_0u,C_0v\rangle_0.
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\langle u,v\rangle_1
=
\langle u,v\rangle_0
+
\langle B_0u,B_0v\rangle_0.
\]

Thus

\[
\|u\|_1^2
=
\|u\|_0^2+\|B_0u\|_0^2.
\]

The operator \(C_0:H_1\to H_0\) is unitary, because

\[
\|u\|_1=\|C_0u\|_0.
\]

Since \(C_0\ge I\), it has a bounded inverse on \(H_0\). Hence \(C_0\) identifies \(H_1\) unitarily with \(H_0\).

Now suppose that \(H_n\) and a positive self-adjoint operator

\[
B_n:D(B_n)\subset H_n\to H_n
\]

have been constructed. Define

\[
C_n=(I+B_n^2)^{1/2}.
\]

Set

\[
H_{n+1}=D(B_n)
\]

with inner product

\[
\langle u,v\rangle_{n+1}
=
\langle C_nu,C_nv\rangle_n.
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\boxed{
\|u\|_{n+1}^2
=
\|u\|_n^2+\|B_nu\|_n^2.
}
\]

Again,

\[
C_n:H_{n+1}\to H_n
\]

is unitary.

---

### 3.1. Recursive operators

Let \(f_n:[0,\infty)\to \mathbb R\) be continuous. Define

\[
B_{n+1}
=
C_n^{-1} f_n(B_n) C_n,
\]

with domain

\[
D(B_{n+1})
=
C_n^{-1}D(f_n(B_n)).
\]

Because \(C_n:H_{n+1}\to H_n\) is unitary and \(f_n(B_n)\) is self-adjoint on \(H_n\), the operator \(B_{n+1}\) is self-adjoint on \(H_{n+1}\).

Thus the pair \((H_n,B_n)\) evolves according to

\[
\boxed{
H_{n+1}=D(B_n),
}
\]

\[
\boxed{
\|u\|_{n+1}^2
=
\|u\|_n^2+\|B_nu\|_n^2,
}
\]

\[
\boxed{
B_{n+1}=C_n^{-1} f_n(B_n) C_n.
}
\]

This is the fundamental recursive Hilbert system.

---

### 3.2. Spectral recursion

Let

\[
\sigma_n=\sigma(B_n)
\]

denote the spectrum of \(B_n\) as an operator on \(H_n\).

---

#### Theorem 3.1 — Spectral mapping for recursive Hilbert systems

Let \(B_n\) be self-adjoint and let \(f_n:\sigma(B_n)\to \mathbb R\) be continuous. Then \(B_{n+1}=C_n^{-1}f_n(B_n)C_n\) is self-adjoint on \(H_{n+1}\), and

\[
\boxed{
\sigma(B_{n+1})
=
\overline{f_n(\sigma(B_n))}.
}
\]

Moreover, point spectrum and essential spectrum transform according to the corresponding functional-calculus mapping.

**Proof.** Since \(C_n:H_{n+1}\to H_n\) is unitary,

\[
B_{n+1}
=
C_n^{-1} f_n(B_n) C_n
\]

is unitarily equivalent to \(f_n(B_n)\). Therefore their spectra coincide. By the continuous functional calculus for self-adjoint operators,

\[
\sigma(f_n(B_n))
=
\overline{f_n(\sigma(B_n))}.
\]

Hence the result. \(\square\)

---

### 3.3. Eigenvalue recursion

Assume that \(B_n\) has discrete spectrum and that

\[
B_n e_{n,k}=\lambda_{n,k} e_{n,k},
\]

where \(\{e_{n,k}\}\) is an orthonormal basis of \(H_n\).

Then

\[
\lambda_{n+1,k}=f_n(\lambda_{n,k}).
\]

Thus the eigenvalues satisfy the scalar recursion

\[
\boxed{
\lambda_{n+1,k}=f_n(\lambda_{n,k}).
}
\]

A particularly important case is

\[
f_n(t)=\sqrt{1+t^2}.
\]

Then

\[
\lambda_{n+1,k}^2=\lambda_{n,k}^2+1,
\]

and therefore

\[
\boxed{
\lambda_{n,k}^2=\lambda_{0,k}^2+n.
}
\]

This simple recursion already produces a nontrivial hierarchy of spaces and norms.

---

### 3.4. Recursive norm weights

Suppose \(B_0\) has an orthonormal eigenbasis \(\{e_k\}\), and assume that the recursive operators remain diagonal in this basis. Write

\[
u=\sum_k u_k e_k.
\]

Then

\[
\|u\|_n^2
=
\sum_k w_{n,k}|u_k|^2,
\]

where the weights satisfy

\[
w_{0,k}=1,
\]

and

\[
w_{n+1,k}
=
w_{n,k}\left(1+\lambda_{n,k}^2\right).
\]

For the recursion

\[
\lambda_{n+1,k}^2=\lambda_{n,k}^2+1,
\]

one obtains

\[
w_{n,k}
=
\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\left(1+\lambda_{j,k}^2\right)
=
\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left(\lambda_{0,k}^2+j\right).
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\boxed{
w_{n,k}
=
\frac{\Gamma(\lambda_{0,k}^2+n+1)}
{\Gamma(\lambda_{0,k}^2+1)}.
}
\]

Thus the recursive norm penalizes high-frequency modes increasingly strongly.

---

### 3.5. Tensorial formulation

In a finite-dimensional approximation or in a chosen basis, write the inner product on \(H_n\) as

\[
\langle u,v\rangle_n
=
\overline{u^i}\,g^{(n)}_{ij}\,v^j,
\]

using Einstein summation convention. The operator \(B_n\) has components

\[
(B_n)^i{}_j.
\]

The recursive norm satisfies

\[
\|u\|_{n+1}^2
=
\|u\|_n^2+\|B_nu\|_n^2.
\]

In components,

\[
\boxed{
g^{(n+1)}_{ij}
=
g^{(n)}_{ij}
+
\overline{(B_n)^k{}_i}
\,g^{(n)}_{k\ell}\,
(B_n)^\ell{}_j.
}
\]

This is the **recursive metric equation**.

If \(B_n\) is self-adjoint with respect to \(g^{(n)}\), then the recursion preserves self-adjointness in the appropriate sense. The generalized eigenvalue problem at level \(n\) is

\[
(B_n)^i{}_j v^j
=
\lambda_{n,k} v^i,
\]

with norm

\[
\|v\|_n^2
=
\overline{v^i}g^{(n)}_{ij}v^j.
\]

The spectral recursion then becomes a recursion for the generalized eigenvalues.

---

### 3.6. Tensor products and many-body recursion

Let \(H_n^{\otimes m}\) be the \(m\)-fold Hilbert tensor product. Define the additive lifted operator

\[
B_n^{[m]}
=
\sum_{r=1}^m
I^{\otimes(r-1)}
\otimes B_n
\otimes I^{\otimes(m-r)}.
\]

If \(B_n\) has eigenvalues \(\lambda_{n,k}\), then \(B_n^{[m]}\) has eigenvalues

\[
\Lambda_{n,k_1,\dots,k_m}
=
\sum_{r=1}^m \lambda_{n,k_r}.
\]

The recursive construction extends naturally:

\[
C_n^{[m]}
=
\left(I+(B_n^{[m]})^2\right)^{1/2},
\]

\[
H_{n+1}^{\otimes m}
=
D(B_n^{[m]}),
\]

and

\[
B_{n+1}^{[m]}
=
(C_n^{[m]})^{-1}
f_n(B_n^{[m]})
C_n^{[m]}.
\]

The spectral mapping theorem gives

\[
\sigma(B_{n+1}^{[m]})
=
\overline{f_n(\sigma(B_n^{[m]}))}.
\]

This construction is particularly relevant in quantum many-body theory and second quantization.

## 4. Banach-Space Recursion Beyond Hilbert Spaces

Although Hilbert spaces provide the cleanest spectral theory, the Banach setting is essential for nonlinear PDEs, \(L^p\) theory, and interpolation.

### 4.1. \(p\)-graph recursion

Let \(X_n\) be a Banach space and \(A_n:D(A_n)\subset X_n\to X_n\) closed. For \(1\le p<\infty\), define

\[
X_{n+1}=D(A_n),
\]

with

\[
\|x\|_{n+1}
=
\left(
\|x\|_n^p+\|A_nx\|_n^p
\right)^{1/p}.
\]

Theorem 2.1 ensures that \(X_{n+1}\) is Banach.

If \(A_n\) is sectorial, then the graph recursion interacts naturally with interpolation spaces. One may define intermediate recursive spaces by

\[
X_{n+1}^{\theta}
=
(X_n,D(A_n))_{\theta,p},
\qquad 0<\theta<1.
\]

This yields fractional recursive scales, useful for parabolic regularity and maximal regularity theory.

---

### 4.2. Dual recursion

Given the graph space

\[
X_{n+1}=D(A_n),
\]

its dual may be represented as

\[
X_{n+1}^*
\cong
X_n^* + A_n^*X_n^*,
\]

with norm

\[
\|\ell\|_{n+1,*}
=
\inf
\left\{
\|\varphi\|_{n,*}+\|\psi\|_{n,*}
:
\ell=\varphi+A_n^*\psi
\right\}.
\]

In the Hilbert case, the Riesz map identifies \(H_{n+1}^*\) with \(H_{n+1}\), and the dual norm becomes

\[
\|\ell\|_{n+1,*}
=
\|C_n^{-1}\ell\|_n.
\]

Thus the dual spaces also evolve recursively.

---

### 4.3. Nonlinear recursive functional analysis

Let \(F:U\subset X_0\to Y_0\) be a \(C^1\) nonlinear map. Given an approximate solution \(u_n\), define the linearization

\[
A_n=DF(u_n).
\]

A nonlinear recursive scheme may be defined by

\[
X_{n+1}=D(A_n),
\]

with graph norm

\[
\|v\|_{n+1}^2
=
\|v\|_n^2+\|A_nv\|_n^2.
\]

A Newton correction \(s_n\) is then sought in \(X_{n+1}\) by solving

\[
A_n s_n=-F(u_n),
\]

and setting

\[
u_{n+1}=u_n+s_n.
\]

The recursive norm measures both the size and the linearized regularity of the correction.

Under standard Kantorovich-type assumptions, one obtains local convergence. Specifically, suppose that:

1. \(A_n^{-1}:Y_0\to X_{n+1}\) exists;
2. \(\|A_n^{-1}\|\le \beta\);
3. \(DF\) is Lipschitz in the recursive norm with constant \(\gamma\);
4. \(\beta\gamma\|F(u_n)\| \le \eta < 1/2\).

Then the Newton recursion is locally well-defined and converges quadratically in the appropriate recursive topology.

This gives a functional-analytic framework for nonlinear PDE solvers in which regularity and linearized operators evolve together.

## 5. Applications to Partial Differential Equations

### 5.1. Elliptic regularity towers

Let \(\Omega\subset\mathbb R^d\) be a bounded domain, and let \(L\) be a uniformly elliptic operator, for example

\[
Lu
=
-\partial_i(a^{ij}\partial_j u)+cu,
\]

with suitable boundary conditions. Assume \(L\) is positive self-adjoint on

\[
H_0=L^2(\Omega).
\]

Set

\[
B_0=L.
\]

Define recursively

\[
H_{n+1}=D(B_n),
\]

with

\[
\|u\|_{n+1}^2
=
\|u\|_n^2+\|B_nu\|_n^2.
\]

If \(L\) has elliptic regularity, then the first few spaces correspond to classical Sobolev spaces. For instance, under standard assumptions,

\[
H_1\sim H^2(\Omega)\cap H_0^1(\Omega).
\]

Further recursive steps generate higher regularity spaces.

Consider the elliptic problem

\[
B_n u=f.
\]

If \(f\in H_n\) and \(B_n^{-1}\) is bounded from \(H_n\) to \(H_n\), then

\[
u=B_n^{-1}f\in H_{n+1}.
\]

Moreover,

\[
\|u\|_{n+1}^2
=
\|u\|_n^2+\|B_nu\|_n^2
=
\|u\|_n^2+\|f\|_n^2.
\]

If a Poincaré-type estimate gives

\[
\|u\|_n\le C_P\|f\|_n,
\]

then

\[
\boxed{
\|u\|_{n+1}
\le
\sqrt{C_P^2+1}\,\|f\|_n.
}
\]

Thus the recursive framework gives a clean operator-theoretic expression of elliptic regularity.

---

### 5.2. Parabolic equations and recursive smoothing

Let \(B_n\) be positive self-adjoint. Consider the recursive gradient flow

\[
\partial_t u + C_n^2 u = f,
\]

where

\[
C_n^2=I+B_n^2.
\]

The natural energy is

\[
E_n(u)=\frac12\|u\|_{n+1}^2
=
\frac12\langle C_nu,C_nu\rangle_n.
\]

For the homogeneous equation,

\[
\partial_t u + C_n^2 u=0,
\]

we obtain

\[
\frac{d}{dt}\frac12\|u\|_n^2
+
\|u\|_{n+1}^2
=
0.
\]

Thus the recursive norm appears naturally as the dissipation norm.

More generally, if \(-B_n\) generates an analytic semigroup \(e^{-tB_n}\), then recursive regularity is expressed by estimates of the form

\[
\|e^{-tB_n}u_0\|_{n+1}
\le
C t^{-\alpha}\|u_0\|_n,
\qquad t>0,
\]

for appropriate \(\alpha>0\). Recursive spaces thereby encode parabolic smoothing.

---

### 5.3. Nonlinear PDEs and recursive Newton methods

Let \(F(u)=0\) be a nonlinear PDE. At step \(n\), let

\[
A_n=DF(u_n).
\]

Define the recursive space

\[
X_{n+1}=D(A_n)
\]

with graph norm. Solve

\[
A_ns_n=-F(u_n)
\]

in \(X_{n+1}\), then set

\[
u_{n+1}=u_n+s_n.
\]

The advantage is that the space in which the correction is measured adapts to the linearized operator. This is particularly useful for degenerate, singular, or multiscale PDEs where a fixed function space is not optimal.

## 6. Applications to Quantum Mechanics

### 6.1. Recursive state spaces

Let \(H_0\) be the physical Hilbert space of a quantum system, and let \(H_{\text{phys}}\) be a Hamiltonian. Set

\[
B_0=H_{\text{phys}}.
\]

The recursive Hilbert construction produces spaces

\[
H_{n+1}=D(B_n),
\]

with inner products

\[
\langle u,v\rangle_{n+1}
=
\langle u,v\rangle_n
+
\langle B_nu,B_nv\rangle_n.
\]

These spaces represent increasing energy regularity. A state \(\psi\in H_n\) has finite \(n\)-th recursive energy norm.

---

### 6.2. Recursive Hamiltonians

Given a continuous function \(f_n\), define

\[
B_{n+1}
=
C_n^{-1}f_n(B_n)C_n,
\qquad
C_n=(I+B_n^2)^{1/2}.
\]

The spectrum evolves by

\[
\sigma(B_{n+1})
=
\overline{f_n(\sigma(B_n))}.
\]

For example, if

\[
f_n(t)=\sqrt{1+t^2},
\]

then

\[
E_{n+1,k}^2=E_{n,k}^2+1.
\]

This gives a simple spectral renormalization in which high-energy levels are shifted systematically.

More generally, one may choose \(f_n\) to implement scale-dependent effective Hamiltonians.

---

### 6.3. Density matrices and expectations

Let \(\rho_n\) be a density matrix on \(H_n\), i.e.

\[
\rho_n\ge 0,
\qquad
\operatorname{Tr}_n\rho_n=1.
\]

The expectation of an observable \(O_n\) is

\[
\langle O_n\rangle_n
=
\operatorname{Tr}_n(\rho_n O_n).
\]

Under the unitary map \(C_n:H_{n+1}\to H_n\), states and observables transform as

\[
\rho_{n+1}
=
C_n^{-1}\rho_n C_n,
\]

and

\[
O_{n+1}
=
C_n^{-1}O_nC_n.
\]

Expectations are preserved:

\[
\operatorname{Tr}_{n+1}(\rho_{n+1}O_{n+1})
=
\operatorname{Tr}_n(\rho_nO_n).
\]

Thus recursive quantum mechanics may be formulated as a unitarily equivalent tower of state spaces with evolving observables.

---

### 6.4. Tensorial many-body formulation

For an \(N\)-particle system, define the recursive many-body space

\[
\mathcal H_n^{(N)}
=
H_n^{\otimes_s N},
\]

where \(\otimes_s\) denotes symmetrized or antisymmetrized tensor product according to statistics.

A many-body Hamiltonian may be written as

\[
\mathcal B_n^{(N)}
=
\sum_{a=1}^N
I^{\otimes(a-1)}
\otimes B_n
\otimes I^{\otimes(N-a)}
+
\sum_{a<b} V_{ab}.
\]

The recursive metric satisfies

\[
g^{(n+1)}_{ab}
=
g^{(n)}_{ab}
+
\overline{(B_n)^c{}_a}
g^{(n)}_{cd}
(B_n)^d{}_b.
\]

This provides a tensorial formulation of recursive quantum states and observables.

---

## 7. Applications to Numerical Analysis

### 7.1. Recursive Galerkin methods

Let \(H_{n+1}\) be a recursive Hilbert space. Suppose one seeks \(u\in H_{n+1}\) satisfying

\[
a_{n+1}(u,v)=\ell_{n+1}(v),
\qquad v\in H_{n+1}.
\]

Choose a finite-dimensional subspace

\[
V_{h}^{(n+1)}\subset H_{n+1}.
\]

The Galerkin approximation \(u_h\in V_h^{(n+1)}\) satisfies

\[
a_{n+1}(u_h,v_h)=\ell_{n+1}(v_h),
\qquad v_h\in V_h^{(n+1)}.
\]

If \(a_{n+1}\) is continuous and coercive,

\[
|a_{n+1}(u,v)|\le M\|u\|_{n+1}\|v\|_{n+1},
\]

\[
a_{n+1}(v,v)\ge \alpha\|v\|_{n+1}^2,
\]

then Céa’s lemma gives

\[
\boxed{
\|u-u_h\|_{n+1}
\le
\left(1+\frac{M}{\alpha}\right)
\inf_{v_h\in V_h^{(n+1)}}
\|u-v_h\|_{n+1}.
}
\]

The recursive norm therefore controls the approximation error.

---

### 7.2. Spectral approximation and recursive weights

Suppose \(u=\sum_k u_ke_k\). The recursive error after truncating to modes \(k\le K\) is

\[
\|u-u_K\|_n^2
=
\sum_{k>K} w_{n,k}|u_k|^2.
\]

Because the weights \(w_{n,k}\) grow with frequency, high modes are strongly penalized at higher recursive levels. This is useful for regularizing ill-posed inverse problems and for designing multilevel preconditioners.

---

### 7.3. Recursive preconditioning

The operator

\[
C_n=(I+B_n^2)^{1/2}
\]

is the natural preconditioner for problems posed in \(H_{n+1}\). Indeed,

\[
\|u\|_{n+1}=\|C_nu\|_n.
\]

Thus solving equations involving \(B_{n+1}\) can often be reduced, by conjugation with \(C_n\), to equations involving \(f_n(B_n)\) on \(H_n\).

The resolvent recursion is

\[
\boxed{
(B_{n+1}-z)^{-1}
=
C_n^{-1}
\left(f_n(B_n)-z\right)^{-1}
C_n,
}
\]

for

\[
z\notin \sigma(B_{n+1}).
\]

This identity is useful for iterative solvers and spectral transformations.

---

### 7.4. Error recursion

Let \(e_n=u-u_n\). A recursive numerical method often has the form

\[
e_{n+1}=T_ne_n+r_n,
\]

where \(T_n\) is a propagation operator and \(r_n\) is a residual or discretization error. If

\[
\|T_n\|_{\mathcal L(H_{n+1},H_{n+1})}\le \rho<1,
\]

then

\[
\|e_{n+1}\|_{n+1}
\le
\rho\|e_n\|_{n+1}
+
\|r_n\|_{n+1}.
\]

This gives a recursive stability condition for multilevel algorithms.

---

## 8. Applications to Signal Processing

### 8.1. Recursive frames

Let \(H_0\) be a signal space, for example \(L^2(\mathbb R^d)\). Suppose \(\{\psi_{0,k}\}_{k}\) is a frame for \(H_0\), with frame operator

\[
S_0x
=
\sum_k \langle x,\psi_{0,k}\rangle_0\psi_{0,k}.
\]

Assume

\[
A_0 I\le S_0\le B_0 I.
\]

Define a recursive norm by

\[
\|x\|_1^2
=
\|x\|_0^2+\langle S_0x,x\rangle_0.
\]

Then

\[
(1+A_0)\|x\|_0^2
\le
\|x\|_1^2
\le
(1+B_0)\|x\|_0^2.
\]

Thus the frame bounds induce norm equivalence between recursive levels.

More generally, if \(S_n\) is the frame operator at level \(n\), define

\[
\boxed{
\|x\|_{n+1}^2
=
\|x\|_n^2+\langle S_nx,x\rangle_n.
}
\]

Then the recursive metric evolves as

\[
g^{(n+1)}
=
g^{(n)}+S_n.
\]

---

### 8.2. Filter banks and tensor notation

Let a discrete signal at level \(n\) be represented by coefficients \(x^{(n)\beta}\). A linear recursive transform may be written as

\[
x^{(n+1)\alpha}
=
W^{(n)\alpha}{}_{\beta}
x^{(n)\beta},
\]

where \(W^{(n)}\) is a filter bank or learned transform.

If the inner product at level \(n\) is

\[
\langle x,y\rangle_n
=
\overline{x^i}g^{(n)}_{ij}y^j,
\]

then the recursive norm induced by the transform is

\[
\boxed{
g^{(n+1)}_{ij}
=
g^{(n)}_{ij}
+
\overline{W^{(n)k}{}_i}
g^{(n)}_{k\ell}
W^{(n)\ell}{}_j.
}
\]

This is the signal-processing analogue of the recursive metric equation.

---

### 8.3. Multiresolution and scale spaces

In wavelet theory, one has nested spaces

\[
V_0\subset V_1\subset V_2\subset\cdots.
\]

Recursive Functional Analysis generalizes this by allowing not only the approximation spaces but also the norms and operators to evolve. The recursive transform may represent:

1. wavelet decomposition;
2. scattering transforms;
3. learned filter banks;
4. graph signal processing operators;
5. neural multiscale transforms.

The advantage of RFA is that it provides a spectral and operator-theoretic account of such recursive representations.

---

## 9. Algorithmic Construction

A basic RFA algorithm may be written as follows.

---

### Algorithm: Recursive Functional System

**Input:**

- Seed space \(X_0\);
- Initial operator \(A_0\);
- Recursive maps \(\Phi_n,\Psi_n\);
- Maximum depth \(N\).

**Output:**

- Spaces \(X_0,\dots,X_N\);
- Norms \(\|\cdot\|_n\);
- Operators \(A_0,\dots,A_N\);
- Spectral data \(\sigma_0,\dots,\sigma_N\).

**Procedure:**

For \(n=0,\dots,N-1\):

1. Define

   \[
   X_{n+1}=\Phi_n(X_n,A_n).
   \]

2. Define the recursive norm, for example

   \[
   \|x\|_{n+1}^2
   =
   \|x\|_n^2+\|A_nx\|_n^2.
   \]

3. Define

   \[
   A_{n+1}=\Psi_n(X_n,A_n).
   \]

4. Compute or approximate

   \[
   \sigma_{n+1}=\sigma(A_{n+1}).
   \]

**Return** the recursive tower.

---

In the Hilbert case with spectral recursion, the algorithm becomes:

1. Set

   \[
   C_n=(I+B_n^2)^{1/2}.
   \]

2. Set

   \[
   H_{n+1}=D(B_n).
   \]

3. Define

   \[
   \langle u,v\rangle_{n+1}
   =
   \langle C_nu,C_nv\rangle_n.
   \]

4. Choose \(f_n\).

5. Set

   \[
   B_{n+1}=C_n^{-1}f_n(B_n)C_n.
   \]

6. Update spectrum:

   \[
   \sigma_{n+1}
   =
   \overline{f_n(\sigma_n)}.
   \]

---

## 10. Open Problems and Future Directions

Recursive Functional Analysis suggests several theoretical and applied problems.

### 10.1. Optimal recursive functors

Given a PDE or inverse problem, one may ask for a choice of \(\Phi\) that minimizes a complexity functional, condition number, or spectral radius.

### 10.2. Convergence in space metrics

One may study convergence of \(X_n\) in Banach-Mazur distance, gap metrics, or metric structures induced by embeddings.

### 10.3. Nonlinear spectral recursion

For nonlinear operators, the notion of spectrum is more subtle. Developing a recursive spectral theory for monotone, accretive, or Fréchet-differentiable operators is an important direction.

### 10.4. Learned recursive spaces

In data-driven settings, the map \(\Phi\) may be learned from data. RFA provides a functional-analytic framework for analyzing stability and generalization of learned multiscale transforms.

### 10.5. Recursive renormalization

In quantum field theory and statistical mechanics, recursive changes of Hilbert space and Hamiltonian are closely related to renormalization. A rigorous RFA formulation may clarify the analytic structure of such flows.

---

## 11. Conclusion

Recursive Functional Analysis replaces the classical static pair \((X,A)\) with a co-evolving system

\[
(X_n,\|\cdot\|_n,A_n,\sigma_n).
\]

The fundamental Hilbert construction

\[
H_{n+1}=D(B_n),
\qquad
\|u\|_{n+1}^2
=
\|u\|_n^2+\|B_nu\|_n^2,
\qquad
B_{n+1}=C_n^{-1}f_n(B_n)C_n
\]

produces a unitary tower of spaces and a spectral recursion

\[
\sigma_{n+1}
=
\overline{f_n(\sigma_n)}.
\]

This framework unifies several phenomena that are usually treated separately: regularity towers in PDEs, energy spaces in quantum mechanics, multilevel methods in numerical analysis, and multiresolution structures in signal processing.

The central thesis is therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Function spaces, norms, operators, and spectra should be allowed to evolve together recursively.}
}
\]

This principle provides a foundation for a broader, dynamic version of functional analysis suited to multiscale, nonlinear, and data-driven problems.
