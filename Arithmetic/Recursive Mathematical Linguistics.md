# Recursive Mathematical Linguistics  
## A Generative Theory of Mathematical Objects, Proofs, and Theories

**White Paper / Academic Preprint**

---

## Abstract

We develop **Recursive Mathematical Linguistics** (RML), a formal framework in which mathematical objects are treated as linguistic structures generated by recursive grammatical systems. In RML, definitions, proofs, theorems, and theories are not merely represented by language; they are produced by rule-governed grammatical evolution. The basic units of mathematics become typed derivations in recursive production systems, while semantic content is obtained through interpretation maps into algebraic, categorical, or tensorial structures. We introduce typed recursive grammars, fixed-point semantics for mathematical generation, tensorial composition laws for derivations, and a proof-theoretic calculus in which proofs are parsed as grammatical objects. We prove existence of least generated mathematical universes, compositionality of tensorial semantics, and soundness of proof grammars. We further analyze computational properties, including decidable fragments and general undecidability. Applications are given to formal language theory, logic, programming languages, and knowledge representation.

**Keywords:** recursive grammars, mathematical linguistics, proof theory, formal semantics, fixed-point theory, tensor semantics, type theory, knowledge representation.

## 1. Introduction

Mathematics has traditionally been viewed through three complementary lenses:

1. **Ontological:** mathematical objects exist as abstract entities.
2. **Formal:** mathematical objects are defined inside axiomatic systems.
3. **Linguistic:** mathematical practice is mediated by symbolic notation, syntax, and discourse.

Recursive Mathematical Linguistics proposes a fourth, generative perspective:

> **Mathematical objects are produced by recursive linguistic grammars.**

In this view, a group, a topology, a proof, or a theorem is not merely described by language. It is generated by a system of recursive production rules. Definitions are grammar extensions. Proofs are derivations. Theories are recursively closed languages. Mathematical knowledge evolves by grammatical operations on symbolic structures.

RML is therefore not simply a philosophy of mathematical language. It is a formal apparatus for modeling mathematical generation. It combines ideas from:

- formal grammar theory,
- algebraic semantics,
- proof theory,
- type theory,
- fixed-point theory,
- categorical logic,
- tensorial composition.

The central claim is that mathematical reasoning can be treated as a **recursive linguistic process** whose syntactic derivations are systematically mapped to semantic mathematical structures.

### 1.1 Core Theses

RML is organized around three theses.

#### Thesis 1: Generative Thesis

Mathematical objects are generated by formal grammars.

A mathematical object is not primitive. It is the output of a derivation in a typed production system. For example, the natural numbers are generated by the recursive grammar

\[
\mathsf{Nat} ::= 0 \mid S(\mathsf{Nat}).
\]

Similarly, groups, topological spaces, proofs, and theories can be generated by recursive grammatical schemes.

#### Thesis 2: Proof-Linguistic Thesis

Proofs are linguistic derivations.

A proof is a grammatical object generated by inference productions. To prove a proposition is to parse it in a proof grammar. Thus proof search becomes a form of linguistic parsing, and proof checking becomes grammatical validation.

#### Thesis 3: Theory-Evolution Thesis

Theories are recursively evolving grammars.

A theory is not a static set of sentences. It is a grammar that generates definitions, axioms, theorems, and further rules of inference. Mathematical progress may be modeled as grammatical evolution: the introduction of new nonterminals, new productions, and new semantic interpretations.

### 1.2 Contributions

This paper contributes the following:

1. A formal definition of **typed recursive mathematical grammars**.
2. A fixed-point semantics for recursively generated mathematical universes.
3. A tensorial semantics for derivations and proofs.
4. A proof-theoretic interpretation of mathematics as grammatical parsing.
5. A theory of grammatical evolution for definitions and theories.
6. Applications to formal languages, logic, programming languages, and knowledge representation.
7. An analysis of computational properties, including decidable fragments and undecidability.

## 2. Typed Recursive Grammars

We begin by formalizing the syntactic substrate of RML.

### 2.1 Sorted Signatures

Let \(S\) be a finite set of **sorts**. Examples include

\[
S = \{\mathsf{Term}, \mathsf{Formula}, \mathsf{Proof}, \mathsf{Definition}, \mathsf{Theory}\}.
\]

A **sorted signature** is a pair

\[
\Sigma = (S, \mathcal{O}),
\]

where \(\mathcal{O}\) is a set of operation symbols. Each operation symbol \(c \in \mathcal{O}\) has an arity

\[
c : s_1 \times \cdots \times s_n \to s.
\]

For example, in a grammar of monoids, one may have

\[
e : \mathsf{Term}, \qquad
\mathsf{mul} : \mathsf{Term} \times \mathsf{Term} \to \mathsf{Term}.
\]

### 2.2 Recursive Mathematical Grammars

An **RML grammar** is a tuple

\[
G = (S, N, T, R, A_0),
\]

where:

1. \(S\) is a finite set of sorts.
2. \(N\) is a finite set of typed nonterminals. Each \(A \in N\) has a sort \(s(A) \in S\).
3. \(T\) is a finite set of terminal constructors drawn from \(\Sigma\).
4. \(R\) is a finite or recursively specified set of productions.
5. \(A_0 \in N\) is the start nonterminal.

A production has the form

\[
A \to c(B_1, \dots, B_k),
\]

where:

- \(A \in N\),
- \(c \in \mathcal{O}\),
- each \(B_i \in N\),
- the typing is consistent.

A production may also be terminal:

\[
A \to c.
\]

The grammar is **recursive** if some nonterminal appears in the body of a production that ultimately depends on itself.

### 2.3 Recursive Language Equations

For each nonterminal \(A\), let \(L_A\) denote the set of syntactic objects generated from \(A\). The grammar induces a system of recursive equations:

\[
L_A
=
\bigcup_{p \in R_A}
\left\{
c_p(x_1,\dots,x_k)
\;\middle|\;
x_i \in L_{B_i}
\right\},
\]

where \(R_A\) is the set of productions with head \(A\).

This system may be written as

\[
\vec{L} = F_G(\vec{L}),
\]

where

\[
F_G : \prod_{A \in N} \mathcal{P}(U_A)
\to
\prod_{A \in N} \mathcal{P}(U_A)
\]

is the **generation operator** associated with \(G\).

### 2.4 Least Generated Languages

Because \(F_G\) is monotone with respect to inclusion, the Knaster–Tarski fixed-point theorem implies the existence of a least fixed point.

Define

\[
L_G = \operatorname{lfp}(F_G).
\]

Equivalently,

\[
L_G = \bigcup_{\alpha < \kappa} F_G^\alpha(\varnothing),
\]

for a sufficiently large ordinal \(\kappa\).

Thus the objects generated by \(G\) are precisely those obtained by finite or well-founded recursive derivations.

### 2.5 Example: Natural Numbers

Let

\[
S = \{\mathsf{Nat}\},
\]

with constructors

\[
0 : \mathsf{Nat}, \qquad S : \mathsf{Nat} \to \mathsf{Nat}.
\]

Let \(N = \{\mathsf{Nat}\}\), and let \(R\) contain

\[
\mathsf{Nat} \to 0,
\]

\[
\mathsf{Nat} \to S(\mathsf{Nat}).
\]

Then the least generated language is

\[
L_{\mathsf{Nat}}
=
\{0, S(0), S(S(0)), \dots\}.
\]

This is the minimal recursive generation of the natural numbers as linguistic objects.

## 3. Grammatical Evolution

Classical grammars are static. Mathematical practice, however, evolves by introducing definitions, notations, and new inference principles. RML therefore includes a theory of **grammatical evolution**.

### 3.1 Grammar States

A **grammar state** is an RML grammar

\[
\Gamma = (S, N, T, R, A_0).
\]

A grammar state may be transformed by meta-level operations.

### 3.2 Meta-Productions

A **meta-production** is a rule of the form

\[
\Gamma \Rightarrow \Gamma',
\]

where \(\Gamma'\) is obtained from \(\Gamma\) by operations such as:

1. adding a new nonterminal,
2. adding a new terminal constructor,
3. adding a new production,
4. quotienting by an equivalence relation,
5. introducing a definition,
6. adding an inference rule,
7. refining a sort.

An RML **evolution system** is a pair

\[
\mathcal{E} = (\Gamma_0, \mathcal{M}),
\]

where \(\Gamma_0\) is an initial grammar and \(\mathcal{M}\) is a set of meta-productions.

### 3.3 Evolution Sequences

An evolution sequence is a chain

\[
\Gamma_0
\Rightarrow
\Gamma_1
\Rightarrow
\Gamma_2
\Rightarrow
\cdots.
\]

If the sequence is increasing with respect to grammar inclusion, we may form the limit grammar

\[
\Gamma_\infty
=
\bigcup_{n < \omega} \Gamma_n.
\]

The evolved mathematical language is then

\[
L(\Gamma_\infty)
=
\bigcup_{n < \omega} L(\Gamma_n).
\]

This captures the growth of mathematical theories.

### 3.4 Definitions as Grammar Extensionsions

A definition is a special meta-production.

Suppose \(t(x_1,\dots,x_n)\) is an already generated term. Introducing a new symbol \(d\) by

\[
d(x_1,\dots,x_n) := t(x_1,\dots,x_n)
\]

corresponds to extending the grammar with a production

\[
D(x_1,\dots,x_n)
\to
t(x_1,\dots,x_n).
\]

Thus definitions are not semantic primitives. They are grammatical compressions.

### 3.5 Theories as Evolving Grammars

A theory is an RML system

\[
\mathcal{T}
=
(\Gamma, \mathsf{Ax}, \mathsf{Inf}),
\]

where:

- \(\Gamma\) is a grammar,
- \(\mathsf{Ax}\) is a recursively generated set of axioms,
- \(\mathsf{Inf}\) is a set of inference productions.

The theory evolves as new definitions, lemmas, and derived inference rules are added.

## 4. Semantic Interpretation

Syntax alone does not determine mathematical content. RML therefore associates grammars with semantic interpretations.

### 4.1 Algebraic Semantics

Let \(G\) be an RML grammar over signature \(\Sigma\). An **interpretation** of \(G\) is a \(\Sigma\)-algebra

\[
\mathcal{A}
=
\left(
(A_s)_{s \in S},
(c^\mathcal{A})_{c \in \mathcal{O}}
\right),
\]

where each sort \(s\) is interpreted as a set \(A_s\), and each constructor \(c : s_1 \times \cdots \times s_n \to s\) is interpreted as a function

\[
c^\mathcal{A}
:
A_{s_1}
\times \cdots \times
A_{s_n}
\to
A_s.
\]

For each derivation tree \(d\), the semantic value

\[
\llbracket d \rrbracket_\mathcal{A}
\]

is defined recursively.

If \(d\) is a terminal derivation

\[
A \to c,
\]

then

\[
\llbracket d \rrbracket_\mathcal{A}
=
c^\mathcal{A}.
\]

If

\[
d = p(d_1,\dots,d_k)
\]

with production

\[
p : A \to c(B_1,\dots,B_k),
\]

then

\[
\llbracket d \rrbracket_\mathcal{A}
=
c^\mathcal{A}
\left(
\llbracket d_1 \rrbracket_\mathcal{A},
\dots,
\llbracket d_k \rrbracket_\mathcal{A}
\right).
\]

This is the standard compositional interpretation of generated syntax.

### 4.2 Initial Algebra Semantics

For many recursive grammars, the most natural semantic object is the **initial algebra** of the signature.

Let \(T_\Sigma\) be the term algebra generated by \(\Sigma\). The language \(L_G\) embeds into \(T_\Sigma\). If the grammar is freely generated, then

\[
L_G \cong T_\Sigma.
\]

Initiality gives the induction principle:

For any \(\Sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{A}\), there exists a unique homomorphism

\[
h : L_G \to \mathcal{A}
\]

preserving constructors.

This is the semantic basis of recursive definition in mathematics.

## 5. Tensorial Semantics of Derivations

RML admits a stronger semantic layer in which derivations are interpreted as tensors. This is especially useful for proofs, programs, and knowledge representation.

Let \(\mathbb{K}\) be a commutative semiring or field. For each nonterminal \(A\), assign a \(\mathbb{K}\)-module \(V_A\).

A production

\[
p : A \to c(B_1,\dots,B_k)
\]

is assigned a tensor

\[
T_p
\in
V_A
\otimes
V_{B_1}^{*}
\otimes
\cdots
\otimes
V_{B_k}^{*}.
\]

Here \(V_{B_i}^{*}\) denotes the dual module.

Given derivations \(d_i\) of \(B_i\), their semantic tensors may be contracted with \(T_p\).

Define recursively:

\[
\llbracket p(d_1,\dots,d_k) \rrbracket
=
\operatorname{Contr}
\left(
T_p
\otimes
\llbracket d_1 \rrbracket
\otimes
\cdots
\otimes
\llbracket d_k \rrbracket
\right),
\]

where \(\operatorname{Contr}\) denotes contraction of each \(V_{B_i}^{*}\) against \(V_{B_i}\).

### 5.1 Compositionality Theorem

**Theorem 5.1.**  
The tensorial semantics of RML derivations is compositional: the semantic tensor of a derivation tree is determined solely by the tensors of its constituent productions and the tree structure.

**Proof.**  
By induction on derivation height.

For height \(0\), the derivation is terminal and the claim holds by definition.

Assume the claim holds for derivations of height at most \(n\). Let \(d\) be a derivation of height \(n+1\):

\[
d = p(d_1,\dots,d_k).
\]

By the induction hypothesis, each \(d_i\) has a well-defined tensor \(\llbracket d_i \rrbracket\). The production \(p\) has tensor \(T_p\). Contraction yields

\[
\llbracket d \rrbracket
=
\operatorname{Contr}
\left(
T_p
\otimes
\llbracket d_1 \rrbracket
\otimes
\cdots
\otimes
\llbracket d_k \rrbracket
\right).
\]

Thus the value is determined by the immediate production and the semantic values of subderivations. Hence the semantics is compositional. \(\square\)

### 5.2 String-Diagrammatic View

A derivation tree may be viewed as a string diagram in a monoidal category. Productions are nodes, nonterminals are wires, and derivations are composites.

If

\[
p : A \to B \otimes C,
\]

then \(T_p\) is a morphism

\[
T_p : B \otimes C \to A
\]

in the dual presentation. Recursive derivations correspond to feedback or fixed-point structure in the diagram.

This tensorial viewpoint is particularly powerful for proof theory, where inference rules become linear or multilinear operations on proof spaces.

## 6. Proofs as Linguistic Structures

In RML, proofs are generated by grammars. The central idea is:

> To prove a formula is to derive it in a proof grammar.

We develop this using a natural-deduction-style proof grammar.

### 6.1 Formula Grammar

Let \(\mathsf{Form}\) be the sort of formulas. A minimal propositional grammar may include

\[
\mathsf{Form}
::=
P
\mid
\top
\mid
\bot
\mid
(\mathsf{Form} \wedge \mathsf{Form})
\mid
(\mathsf{Form} \vee \mathsf{Form})
\mid
(\mathsf{Form} \to \mathsf{Form}),
\]

where \(P\) ranges over atomic propositions.

### 6.2 Sequent Nonterminals

A proof nonterminal is indexed by a context and a formula:

\[
\mathsf{Proof}(\Gamma; \varphi),
\]

where \(\Gamma\) is a finite multiset or set of assumptions.

A derivation of

\[
\mathsf{Proof}(\Gamma; \varphi)
\]

is a proof of \(\varphi\) from assumptions \(\Gamma\).

### 6.3 Proof Productions

The following are typical proof productions.

#### Axiom Rule

\[
\mathsf{Proof}(\Gamma, \varphi; \varphi).
\]

#### Conjunction Introduction

\[
\frac{
\mathsf{Proof}(\Gamma; \varphi)
\quad
\mathsf{Proof}(\Gamma; \psi)
}{
\mathsf{Proof}(\Gamma; \varphi \wedge \psi)
}.
\]

As a production:

\[
\mathsf{Proof}(\Gamma; \varphi \wedge \psi)
\to
\wedge I
\left(
\mathsf{Proof}(\Gamma; \varphi),
\mathsf{Proof}(\Gamma; \psi)
\right).
\]

#### Conjunction Elimination

\[
\mathsf{Proof}(\Gamma; \varphi)
\to
\wedge E_1
\left(
\mathsf{Proof}(\Gamma; \varphi \wedge \psi)
\right),
\]

\[
\mathsf{Proof}(\Gamma; \psi)
\to
\wedge E_2
\left(
\mathsf{Proof}(\Gamma; \varphi \wedge \psi)
\right).
\]

#### Implication Introduction

\[
\frac{
\mathsf{Proof}(\Gamma, \varphi; \psi)
}{
\mathsf{Proof}(\Gamma; \varphi \to \psi)
}.
\]

As a production:

\[
\mathsf{Proof}(\Gamma; \varphi \to \psi)
\to
\to I
\left(
\mathsf{Proof}(\Gamma, \varphi; \psi)
\right).
\]

#### Implication Elimination

\[
\frac{
\mathsf{Proof}(\Gamma; \varphi \to \psi)
\quad
\mathsf{Proof}(\Gamma; \varphi)
}{
\mathsf{Proof}(\Gamma; \psi)
}.
\]

As a production:

\[
\mathsf{Proof}(\Gamma; \psi)
\to
\to E
\left(
\mathsf{Proof}(\Gamma; \varphi \to \psi),
\mathsf{Proof}(\Gamma; \varphi)
\right).
\]

### 6.4 Example Derivation

We derive

\[
\vdash
(\alpha \wedge \beta) \to (\beta \wedge \alpha).
\]

Work under the assumption

\[
x : \alpha \wedge \beta.
\]

By conjunction elimination:

\[
\pi_1 : \beta
\]

from

\[
\wedge E_2(x),
\]

and

\[
\pi_2 : \alpha
\]

from

\[
\wedge E_1(x).
\]

By conjunction introduction:

\[
\pi_3 : \beta \wedge \alpha
\]

from

\[
\wedge I(\pi_1, \pi_2).
\]

By implication introduction, discharging \(x\):

\[
\pi
=
\to I(\pi_3)
:
(\alpha \wedge \beta) \to (\beta \wedge \alpha).
\]

Thus the proof is a grammatical derivation.

### 6.5 Tensorial Proof Semantics

Assign to each formula \(\varphi\) a proof space \(V_\varphi\).

The rule tensors include:

\[
T_{\wedge I}
\in
V_{\varphi \wedge \psi}
\otimes
V_\varphi^{*}
\otimes
V_\psi^{*},
\]

\[
T_{\wedge E_1}
\in
V_\varphi
\otimes
V_{\varphi \wedge \psi}^{*},
\]

\[
T_{\wedge E_2}
\in
V_\psi
\otimes
V_{\varphi \wedge \psi}^{*},
\]

\[
T_{\to I}
\in
V_{\varphi \to \psi}
\otimes
V_\psi^{*}
\otimes
V_\varphi,
\]

where the last expression abstracts over an assumption in \(V_\varphi\).

The proof of

\[
(\alpha \wedge \beta) \to (\beta \wedge \alpha)
\]

has semantic tensor

\[
\llbracket \pi \rrbracket
=
T_{\to I}
\left(
T_{\wedge I}
\left(
T_{\wedge E_2}(x),
T_{\wedge E_1}(x)
\right)
\right),
\]

with contraction over the assumption \(x : \alpha \wedge \beta\).

Thus proofs are tensor networks generated by inference grammars.

## 7. Soundness of Proof Grammars

Let \(\mathcal{M}\) be a semantic model for the formula grammar. Suppose each formula \(\varphi\) is assigned a truth value

\[
\llbracket \varphi \rrbracket_\mathcal{M}
\in
\{0,1\}
\]

or, more generally, a proof object in a suitable semantic category.

### 7.1 Soundness Theorem

**Theorem 7.1.**  
If the proof productions are interpreted as valid inference operations in \(\mathcal{M}\), then every derivable sequent is semantically valid.

Formally, if

\[
\mathsf{Proof}(\Gamma; \varphi)
\]

is derivable and every \(\gamma \in \Gamma\) is true in \(\mathcal{M}\), then \(\varphi\) is true in \(\mathcal{M}\).

**Proof.**  
By induction on the derivation of \(\mathsf{Proof}(\Gamma; \varphi)\).

Base case: axiom rule. If \(\varphi \in \Gamma\), then \(\varphi\) is true whenever all assumptions in \(\Gamma\) are true.

Inductive cases:

- For \(\wedge I\), if \(\varphi\) and \(\psi\) are true, then \(\varphi \wedge \psi\) is true.
- For \(\wedge E_1\), if \(\varphi \wedge \psi\) is true, then \(\varphi\) is true.
- For \(\wedge E_2\), if \(\varphi \wedge \psi\) is true, then \(\psi\) is true.
- For \(\to I\), if assuming \(\varphi\) yields \(\psi\), then \(\varphi \to \psi\) is valid.
- For \(\to E\), if \(\varphi \to \psi\) and \(\varphi\) are true, then \(\psi\) is true.

Thus each production preserves semantic validity. Therefore the conclusion is valid. \(\square\)

### 7.2 Curry–Howard Interpretation

Under the propositions-as-types principle, formulas are types and proofs are programs. RML refines this by treating propositions as grammatical sorts and proofs as derivations.

Thus:

\[
\text{Proposition}
=
\text{Grammatical Type},
\]

\[
\text{Proof}
=
\text{Derivation Term},
\]

\[
\text{Proof Normalization}
=
\text{Grammatical Simplification}.
\]

## 8. Theories as Recursive Closures

A theory in RML is not merely a set of axioms. It is a recursively closed grammatical system.

### 8.1 Theory Closure Operator

Let \(\mathcal{T} = (G, \mathsf{Ax}, \mathsf{Inf})\) be a theory. Define the theory closure operator

\[
\Phi_\mathcal{T} :
\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{Form})
\to
\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{Form})
\]

by

\[
\Phi_\mathcal{T}(X)
=
\mathsf{Ax}
\cup
\left\{
\psi
\;\middle|\;
\frac{\varphi_1 \quad \cdots \quad \varphi_n}{\psi}
\in \mathsf{Inf},
\;
\varphi_i \in X
\right\}.
\]

The theorems of \(\mathcal{T}\) are

\[
\operatorname{Th}(\mathcal{T})
=
\operatorname{lfp}(\Phi_\mathcal{T}).
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\operatorname{Th}(\mathcal{T})
=
\bigcup_{\alpha < \kappa}
\Phi_\mathcal{T}^\alpha(\varnothing).
\]

### 8.2 Derived Rules as Grammar Compression

A derived inference rule

\[
\frac{\varphi_1 \quad \cdots \quad \varphi_n}{\psi}
\]

is admissible if whenever the premises are in \(\operatorname{Th}(\mathcal{T})\), the conclusion is also in \(\operatorname{Th}(\mathcal{T})\).

Once derived, such a rule may be added to the grammar as a new production. This is grammatical compression: a repeated derivation becomes a primitive rule.

### 8.3 Mathematical Knowledge as Recursive Closure

Mathematical knowledge, in RML, is not a static corpus. It is the recursive closure of axioms under inference productions, together with the meta-level evolution of definitions and notations.

## 9. Applications

RML has applications across several domains.

---

## 9.1 Formal Language Theory

Classical formal language theory studies strings generated by grammars. RML generalizes this by allowing:

1. typed nonterminals,
2. semantic constructors,
3. recursive equations,
4. grammar evolution,
5. proof-like derivations.

### 9.1.1 Embedding Context-Free Grammars

Every context-free grammar can be represented as an RML grammar with trivial semantic interpretation.

Let a CFG production be

\[
A \to B_1 \cdots B_k.
\]

Introduce a constructor

\[
c_A : B_1 \times \cdots \times B_k \to A.
\]

Then the corresponding RML production is

\[
A \to c_A(B_1,\dots,B_k).
\]

Thus CFGs are a special case of RML.

### 9.1.2 Beyond Context-Free Grammars

RML can express dependencies that are difficult for ordinary CFGs, including:

- binding,
- variable scope,
- type dependency,
- proof contexts,
- recursive definitions,
- semantic constraints.

This places RML closer to indexed grammars, attribute grammars, and dependent type systems.

---

## 9.2 Logic

In logic, RML provides a unified linguistic account of:

- formula formation,
- proof construction,
- theory extension,
- definitional equivalence,
- normalization.

Proof search becomes parsing in a proof grammar. Proof checking becomes derivation validation.

### 9.2.1 Proof Search as Parsing

Given a goal formula \(\varphi\), proof search asks whether

\[
\mathsf{Proof}(\varnothing; \varphi)
\]

is derivable. This is analogous to asking whether \(\varphi\) belongs to the language generated by the proof grammar.

### 9.2.2 Normalization as Grammar Reduction

Cut elimination and normalization correspond to reducing derivation trees to canonical grammatical forms.

---

## 9.3 Programming Languages

Programming languages are mathematical languages. RML models programs as derivations and types as grammatical sorts.

### 9.3.1 Types as Nonterminals

A type system may be represented as an RML grammar.

For example, a simple typed lambda calculus has productions:

\[
\mathsf{Term}(\Gamma; A \to B)
\to
\lambda x^A.
\mathsf{Term}(\Gamma, x:A; B),
\]

\[
\mathsf{Term}(\Gamma; B)
\to
\mathsf{App}
\left(
\mathsf{Term}(\Gamma; A \to B),
\mathsf{Term}(\Gamma; A)
\right).
\]

Typing derivations are RML derivations.

### 9.3.2 Program Synthesis

Program synthesis may be formulated as:

Given a type or specification \(\varphi\), generate a derivation of

\[
\mathsf{Term}(\varnothing; \varphi).
\]

Thus program synthesis is proof search, and proof search is grammatical generation.

### 9.3.3 Dependent Types

Dependent type theory fits naturally into RML because types may depend on terms. RML allows nonterminals indexed by terms:

\[
\mathsf{Term}(\Gamma; A(t)).
\]

Recursive definitions, universes, and inductive families can all be modeled as recursive grammatical schemes.

---

## 9.4 Knowledge Representation

RML can serve as a foundation for structured knowledge systems.

### 9.4.1 Ontologies as Grammars

An ontology may be represented by sorts and productions:

\[
\mathsf{Animal} \to \mathsf{Mammal}(\mathsf{Animal}),
\]

\[
\mathsf{Mammal}(x) \to \mathsf{Animal}(x),
\]

\[
\mathsf{Human}(x) \to \mathsf{Mammal}(x).
\]

Classification becomes grammatical derivation.

### 9.4.2 Rules as Productions

Inference rules in knowledge bases are productions:

\[
\frac{
\mathsf{Parent}(x,y)
\quad
\mathsf{Parent}(y,z)
}{
\mathsf{Grandparent}(x,z)
}.
\]

Recursive closure generates inferred facts.

### 9.4.3 Tensor Knowledge Graphs

Entities and relations may be represented as tensors. A relation \(R\) of arity \(n\) is a tensor

\[
T_R \in V_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes V_n.
\]

Grammar rules become tensor contractions. This gives a bridge between symbolic knowledge representation and tensor-based machine learning models.

## 10. Case Study: Recursive Generation of Monoid Powers

We now present a concrete RML case study.

Let \(M\) be a monoid with identity \(e\) and multiplication \(\cdot\). We recursively define powers \(x^n\).

### 10.1 Grammar

Introduce sorts:

\[
\mathsf{Term}, \mathsf{Nat}.
\]

Constructors:

\[
e : \mathsf{Term},
\]

\[
\mathsf{mul} : \mathsf{Term} \times \mathsf{Term} \to \mathsf{Term},
\]

\[
0 : \mathsf{Nat},
\]

\[
S : \mathsf{Nat} \to \mathsf{Nat}.
\]

Introduce a derived nonterminal:

\[
\mathsf{Pow}(\mathsf{Term}, \mathsf{Nat}).
\]

Recursive productions:

\[
\mathsf{Pow}(x,0)
\to
e,
\]

\[
\mathsf{Pow}(x,S(n))
\to
\mathsf{mul}(x, \mathsf{Pow}(x,n)).
\]

### 10.2 Derivation

Let \(a\) be a term. We derive \(\mathsf{Pow}(a,S(S(0)))\).

First step:

\[
\mathsf{Pow}(a,S(S(0)))
\Rightarrow
\mathsf{mul}
\left(
a,
\mathsf{Pow}(a,S(0))
\right).
\]

Second step:

\[
\mathsf{Pow}(a,S(0))
\Rightarrow
\mathsf{mul}
\left(
a,
\mathsf{Pow}(a,0)
\right).
\]

Third step:

\[
\mathsf{Pow}(a,0)
\Rightarrow
e.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\mathsf{Pow}(a,S(S(0)))
\Rightarrow
\mathsf{mul}
\left(
a,
\mathsf{mul}(a,e)
\right).
\]

This is a grammatical derivation of the term \(a \cdot (a \cdot e)\).

### 10.3 Semantic Interpretation

In a monoid \(M\), interpret:

\[
\llbracket e \rrbracket = 1_M,
\]

\[
\llbracket \mathsf{mul} \rrbracket (u,v) = u v.
\]

Then:

\[
\llbracket \mathsf{Pow}(x,0) \rrbracket
=
1_M,
\]

\[
\llbracket \mathsf{Pow}(x,S(n)) \rrbracket
=
\llbracket x \rrbracket
\cdot
\llbracket \mathsf{Pow}(x,n) \rrbracket.
\]

Thus the recursive linguistic definition induces the usual algebraic definition of powers.

### 10.4 Tensor Interpretation

Let \(V_{\mathsf{Term}}\) be the module associated with monoid terms. Multiplication is represented by a tensor

\[
T_{\mathsf{mul}}
\in
V_{\mathsf{Term}}
\otimes
V_{\mathsf{Term}}^{*}
\otimes
V_{\mathsf{Term}}^{*}.
\]

The power rule

\[
\mathsf{Pow}(x,S(n))
\to
\mathsf{mul}(x,\mathsf{Pow}(x,n))
\]

has tensor

\[
T_{S}
=
T_{\mathsf{mul}}.
\]

The base rule

\[
\mathsf{Pow}(x,0)
\to
e
\]

has tensor

\[
T_0
=
\llbracket e \rrbracket.
\]

The derivation of \(\mathsf{Pow}(a,S(S(0)))\) becomes the tensor contraction

\[
T_{\mathsf{mul}}
\left(
a,
T_{\mathsf{mul}}
\left(
a,
T_0
\right)
\right).
\]

This demonstrates how recursive mathematical definitions become tensorial grammatical derivations.

## 11. Computational Properties

RML is expressive. Consequently, unrestricted RML is computationally undecidable. However, useful fragments are decidable.

### 11.1 Decidable Fragment: Acyclic Grammars

An RML grammar is **acyclic** if the dependency graph on nonterminals has no cycles.

For finite acyclic grammars, generation terminates after finitely many stages. Membership and generation are decidable.

### 11.2 Decidable Fragment: Typed Context-Free RML

If productions are context-free, typed, and finitely branching, then standard parsing algorithms generalize.

For a grammar of bounded production arity \(k\), parsing a term of size \(n\) may be performed in time polynomial in \(n\), typically

\[
O(n^{k+1}),
\]

depending on the parsing strategy.

### 11.3 Undecidability of General RML

**Theorem 11.1.**  
The general membership problem for unrestricted RML grammars is undecidable.

**Proof Sketch.**  
Unrestricted grammars can be encoded as RML grammars by treating each nonterminal and terminal of the unrestricted grammar as typed constructors in RML. Each unrestricted production

\[
\alpha \to \beta
\]

is represented as an RML production generating a derivation object corresponding to the rewrite step. The generated language of the unrestricted grammar is therefore simulated by the generated language of the RML system.

Since membership for unrestricted grammars is undecidable, membership for unrestricted RML grammars is also undecidable. \(\square\)

### 11.4 Proof Search and Complexity

Proof search in RML proof grammars inherits the complexity of the underlying logic.

For propositional intuitionistic logic, proof search is PSPACE-complete. For first-order logic, derivability is semi-decidable. For richer dependent type systems, proof search may be highly undecidable.

Thus RML does not eliminate computational difficulty. Rather, it reframes it as grammatical generation.

## 12. RML and Categorical Logic

RML has a natural categorical interpretation.

Let \(\mathcal{C}\) be a category with suitable structure, such as a cartesian closed category or a locally cartesian closed category.

Each sort is interpreted as an object of \(\mathcal{C}\). Each constructor is interpreted as a morphism. Each production is interpreted as a morphism or a natural transformation.

An RML grammar then determines a free categorical structure.

### 12.1 Grammars as Algebraic Theories

A many-sorted algebraic theory may be presented by objects and morphisms. RML grammars generalize algebraic theories by allowing recursive productions and meta-level evolution.

### 12.2 Proof Grammars as Fibrations

Context-dependent proof grammars may be modeled using fibrations:

\[
p : \mathcal{E} \to \mathcal{B},
\]

where:

- objects of \(\mathcal{B}\) are contexts,
- fibers contain propositions or types,
- morphisms in the total category represent substitutions and proof transformations.

This aligns RML with categorical semantics of dependent type theory.

## 13. Definitional Equivalence and Grammatical Isomorphism

Two mathematical theories are often considered equivalent if they are definitionally intertranslatable. In RML, this becomes a grammatical notion.

### 13.1 Grammar Morphisms

A grammar morphism

\[
F : G \to H
\]

maps sorts, nonterminals, terminals, and productions of \(G\) into those of \(H\) while preserving typing and derivation structure.

### 13.2 Definitional Extension

A grammar \(H\) is a definitional extension of \(G\) if \(H\) is obtained from \(G\) by adding productions that define new symbols in terms of old ones.

Such extensions are conservative at the level of generated semantic structures.

### 13.3 Grammatical Equivalence

Two grammars \(G\) and \(H\) are **RML-equivalent** if there exist grammar morphisms

\[
F : G \to H,
\qquad
G : H \to G
\]

such that their composites are naturally isomorphic to the identity grammars, up to definitional reduction.

This provides a linguistic account of theory equivalence.

## 14. Recursive Mathematical Linguistics and Induction

Induction is central to recursive mathematical generation.

### 14.1 Structural Induction Principle

Let \(L_G\) be the least language generated by an RML grammar. To prove that every object \(x \in L_G\) satisfies a property \(P\), it suffices to prove:

1. \(P\) holds for all terminal productions.
2. For every production

\[
A \to c(B_1,\dots,B_k),
\]

if \(P\) holds for the subobjects generated by \(B_1,\dots,B_k\), then \(P\) holds for the object generated by \(A\).

This is the grammatical form of structural induction.

### 14.2 Recursive Definition Principle

Given an interpretation \(\mathcal{A}\), a function on generated objects may be defined by specifying its value on each production.

If

\[
d = p(d_1,\dots,d_k),
\]

then define

\[
f(d)
=
F_p(f(d_1),\dots,f(d_k)).
\]

Because \(L_G\) is the least fixed point, such recursive definitions are well-founded.

### 14.3 Inductive Families

RML naturally supports inductive families. For example, the even numbers may be generated by:

\[
\mathsf{Even}(0),
\]

\[
\mathsf{Even}(n) \to \mathsf{Even}(S(S(n))).
\]

Here the nonterminal is indexed by a term. This generalizes to vectors, typed syntax, and dependent proofs.

## 15. Philosophical Implications

RML suggests a shift in the philosophy of mathematics.

### 15.1 From Objects to Productions

Mathematical objects are not primary. Productions are primary. An object is what is generated by a rule system.

### 15.2 From Truth to Derivability

Semantic truth is analyzed relative to interpretations. Derivability is internal to the grammar. Mathematical validity becomes the alignment of grammatical derivation and semantic interpretation.

### 15.3 From Static Theories to Evolving Grammars

Mathematical theories are not fixed axiom systems. They are evolving grammars that generate new concepts, definitions, and proofs.

### 15.4 Mathematical Language as Constitutive

RML rejects the view that mathematical language is merely descriptive. In RML, mathematical language is constitutive: it generates the objects it speaks about.

## 16. Future Directions

Several research directions arise.

### 16.1 Mechanized RML

RML could be implemented in proof assistants or grammar-based theorem provers. Mathematical theories could be stored as evolving grammars.

### 16.2 Learning Mathematical Grammars

Machine learning systems may infer RML grammars from mathematical corpora. This connects RML to automated conjecture and formal mathematics.

### 16.3 Homological RML

Derivation spaces may be studied using homological or topological methods. Proof deformations, rewriting paths, and grammar morphisms may form higher categorical structures.

### 16.4 Probabilistic RML

Assigning probabilities to productions yields probabilistic mathematical grammars. This may be useful for inductive program synthesis and heuristic proof search.

### 16.5 Tensor Knowledge Systems

RML may be combined with tensor networks to create hybrid symbolic-tensor knowledge representation systems.

## 17. Conclusion

Recursive Mathematical Linguistics provides a unified formal framework in which mathematical objects, proofs, definitions, and theories are generated by recursive grammatical systems. By treating mathematics as a generative linguistic phenomenon, RML connects formal language theory, proof theory, type theory, categorical semantics, and knowledge representation.

The central formal results are:

1. Recursive grammars induce monotone generation operators with least fixed points.
2. Derivations admit compositional algebraic and tensorial semantics.
3. Proofs are grammatical derivations whose validity is established by soundness of production rules.
4. Theories are recursive closures under inference productions.
5. Definitions and theory growth are grammatical evolution operations.
6. General RML is computationally undecidable, but important fragments are decidable and practically useful.

RML therefore offers a rigorous foundation for understanding mathematics as a recursively generated linguistic structure.
