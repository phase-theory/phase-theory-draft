## Abstract

This paper introduces **counterphase numbers**, a new arithmetic designed to retain, in one number system, three regimes that are normally separated at the moment of definition: compact rotation, hyperbolic splitting, and first-order phase reversal. A counterphase number is generated by the ordinary imaginary unit \(\mathrm i\) and a phase-reversing unit \(\vartheta\) satisfying

\[
\mathrm i^2=-1,\qquad \vartheta z=\bar z\,\vartheta\quad(z\in\mathbb C),\qquad s:=\vartheta^2\ \text{central}.
\]

The decisive feature is that \(s\) is **not fixed to a numerical constant**. It remains an internal central scale coordinate. Consequently, one universal algebra simultaneously contains compact quaternionic fibers \(s<0\), a singular semilinear nilpotent fiber \(s=0\), and split matrix fibers \(s>0\). The paper establishes a normal-form theorem, a coordinate multiplication law, a canonical anti-involution, a scalar multiplicative norm, a quadratic identity, an explicit inverse criterion, a tensorial metric formulation, and a complete classification of the real fibers. It then develops an intrinsic analytic functional calculus and an integral arithmetic based on the norm form

\[
N\bigl(a+b\mathrm i+c\vartheta+d\mathrm i\vartheta\bigr)
 =a^2+b^2-s(c^2+d^2).
\]

Counterphase arithmetic treats **phase reversal as a numerical operation**, rather than as an external map imposed after calculation. This converts conjugation, scale, rotation, splitting, and nilpotent propagation into aspects of one algebraic object.

> **Central thesis.** Imaginary numbers adjoin a direction whose square is negative. Counterphase numbers adjoin a direction whose passage reverses complex phase and whose square is itself a variable of arithmetic.

## 1. Motivation and research position

The usual complex unit \(\mathrm i\) enlarges real arithmetic by converting the impossible real equation \(x^2+1=0\) into an elementary relation. Its strength is not merely that it adds a symbol; it supplies a coherent multiplication law, a conjugation, a norm, a geometry, and an analytic calculus. Later extensions, including dual and quaternionic constructions, demonstrate that a new unit becomes mathematically useful only when its interaction with existing scalars is specified with equal care. Standard quaternion theory formalizes involution and norm as central organizing structures, while generalized dual-quaternion constructions show how several extensions may be combined within one algebraic setting.[1] [2]

The present proposal changes the point at which an extension is specialized. Instead of declaring at the outset that a new generator squares to \(-1\), \(+1\), or \(0\), counterphase arithmetic retains its square as a central number-like quantity \(s\). The generator therefore does two things at once: it reverses complex phase when it crosses a scalar, and it carries an internally variable scale. The result is not a collection of unrelated algebras indexed from outside; it is one scale-bearing arithmetic whose evaluations recover distinct real geometries.

The construction uses an automorphism-twisted multiplication rule and should be read as a formal number theory built from that rule, rather than as a replacement for the established classification of associative algebras. Its contribution is the **counterphase formulation**: a universal, phase-reversing arithmetic together with the structural consequences derived below. In particular, every theorem in this paper is stated and proved from the defining relations, so the theory does not depend on analogy alone.

| Established design decision | Counterphase design decision | Structural consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Fix a generator square at definition | Retain \(s=\vartheta^2\) as a central coordinate | One universal algebra has several geometrically distinct fibers |
| Let a new unit commute with \(\mathbb C\) | Require \(\vartheta z=\bar z\vartheta\) | Crossing \(\vartheta\) reverses complex phase |
| Add conjugation as an auxiliary map | Build phase reversal into multiplication | A canonical scalar norm follows directly |
| Treat compact, split, and nilpotent cases separately | Specialize the same algebra at \(s=\rho\) | A single calculus controls all three regimes |

## 2. The counterphase axiomatics

Let \(\mathbb C=\mathbb R[\mathrm i]\), with the usual complex conjugation \(z\mapsto\bar z\). The word *central* means commuting with every element of the algebra.

### Definition 2.1 (universal counterphase algebra)

The **universal counterphase algebra** is the unital associative \(\mathbb R\)-algebra

\[
\mathscr C:=\mathbb C[\vartheta;\overline{\phantom z}],
\]

specified by the crossing relation

\[
\boxed{\;\vartheta z=\bar z\,\vartheta\qquad(z\in\mathbb C).\;}
\tag{2.1}
\]

Define the **counterphase scale** by

\[
\boxed{\;s:=\vartheta^2.\;}
\tag{2.2}
\]

An element of \(\mathscr C\) is called a **counterphase number**.

The term *counterphase* reflects (2.1): if \(z=re^{\mathrm i\phi}\), then moving \(z\) past \(\vartheta\) sends \(e^{\mathrm i\phi}\) to \(e^{-\mathrm i\phi}\). The generator does not merely add another plane direction; it reverses the orientation of the pre-existing complex phase.

### Lemma 2.2 (centrality of the scale)

The element \(s=\vartheta^2\) commutes with \(\mathbb C\) and with \(\vartheta\). Therefore \(s\in Z(\mathscr C)\), the center of \(\mathscr C\).

**Proof.** For \(z\in\mathbb C\), repeated use of (2.1) gives

\[
\vartheta^2z=\vartheta\bar z\,\vartheta=z\vartheta^2.
\]

The relation \(s\vartheta=\vartheta s\) is immediate from \(s=\vartheta^2\). Since \(\mathscr C\) is generated by \(\mathbb C\) and \(\vartheta\), \(s\) is central. \(\square\)

### Theorem 2.3 (normal form)

Every counterphase number has a unique representation

\[
\boxed{\;P=A(s)+B(s)\vartheta,\qquad A,B\in\mathbb C[s].\;}
\tag{2.3}
\]

Thus \(\mathscr C\) is a free \(\mathbb C[s]\)-module of rank two and a free \(\mathbb R[s]\)-module of rank four.

**Proof.** Any word in \(\mathbb C\) and \(\vartheta\) may be reordered by (2.1), moving each complex scalar to the left. Every adjacent pair \(\vartheta^2\) becomes the central element \(s\), leaving either no terminal \(\vartheta\) or one terminal \(\vartheta\). This proves existence of (2.3). Uniqueness follows from the defining skew-polynomial normal form: the terms with terminal parity \(0\) and \(1\) cannot cancel. \(\square\)

The choice to preserve \(s\) is what makes the construction universal. A fixed numerical choice \(s=\rho\) is a later operation, not an axiom.

## 3. Arithmetic of counterphase numbers

Let

\[
P=A+B\vartheta,\qquad Q=C+D\vartheta,
\]

where \(A,B,C,D\in\mathbb C[s]\). Complex conjugation acts coefficientwise on \(\mathbb C[s]\) and fixes \(s\).

### Proposition 3.1 (product formula)

Counterphase multiplication is given by

\[
\boxed{
PQ=\bigl(AC+sB\bar D\bigr)+\bigl(AD+B\bar C\bigr)\vartheta.
}
\tag{3.1}
\]

**Proof.** Expand and apply \(\vartheta C=\bar C\vartheta\) and \(\vartheta D\vartheta=s\bar D\):

\[
\begin{aligned}
(A+B\vartheta)(C+D\vartheta)
 &=AC+AD\vartheta+B\vartheta C+B\vartheta D\vartheta\\
 &=AC+AD\vartheta+B\bar C\vartheta+sB\bar D.
\end{aligned}
\]

Collecting the even and odd terms gives (3.1). \(\square\)

If \(P=a+b\mathrm i+c\vartheta+d\mathrm i\vartheta\) and \(Q=e+f\mathrm i+g\vartheta+h\mathrm i\vartheta\), the product has components

\[
\begin{aligned}
(PQ)_0&=ae-bf+s(cg+dh),\\
(PQ)_1&=af+be+s(dg-ch),\\
(PQ)_2&=ag-bh+ce+df,\\
(PQ)_3&=ah+bg+de-cf.
\end{aligned}
\tag{3.2}
\]

These equations provide a direct arithmetic algorithm over any real specialization of \(s\).

### Table 3.1. Basis multiplication

With \(e_0=1\), \(e_1=\mathrm i\), \(e_2=\vartheta\), and \(e_3=\mathrm i\vartheta\), the multiplication table is:

| \(e_a e_b\) | \(e_0\) | \(e_1\) | \(e_2\) | \(e_3\) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| **\(e_0\)** | \(e_0\) | \(e_1\) | \(e_2\) | \(e_3\) |
| **\(e_1\)** | \(e_1\) | \(-e_0\) | \(e_3\) | \(-e_2\) |
| **\(e_2\)** | \(e_2\) | \(-e_3\) | \(se_0\) | \(-se_1\) |
| **\(e_3\)** | \(e_3\) | \(e_2\) | \(se_1\) | \(se_0\) |

The table displays a key phenomenon. The elements \(\mathrm i\) and \(\vartheta\) anticommute, while their squares are controlled independently: \(\mathrm i^2=-1\) and \(\vartheta^2=s\). Thus counterphase numbers incorporate an internal transition between elliptic and hyperbolic behavior without altering the crossing law.

## 4. Counterphase conjugation, trace, and norm

### Definition 4.1 (counterphase conjugation)

For \(P=A+B\vartheta\), define

\[
\boxed{\;P^\natural:=\bar A-B\vartheta.\;}
\tag{4.1}
\]

This operation simultaneously conjugates the complex component and reverses the counterphase component.

### Theorem 4.2 (canonical anti-involution)

The map \(P\mapsto P^\natural\) satisfies

\[
(P^\natural)^\natural=P,
\qquad
(PQ)^\natural=Q^\natural P^\natural.
\tag{4.2}
\]

**Proof.** The first identity is immediate from (4.1). For the second, substitute (3.1):

\[
\begin{aligned}
(PQ)^\natural
 &=\overline{AC+sB\bar D}-(AD+B\bar C)\vartheta\\
 &=\bar C\bar A+s\bar B D-(AD+B\bar C)\vartheta.
\end{aligned}
\]

Applying (3.1) to \(Q^\natural P^\natural=(\bar C-D\vartheta)(\bar A-B\vartheta)\) gives the same expression. \(\square\)

### Definition 4.3 (trace and norm)

The **counterphase trace** and **counterphase norm** are

\[
T(P):=P+P^\natural=A+\bar A,
\tag{4.3}
\]

\[
\boxed{\;N(P):=PP^\natural=A\bar A-sB\bar B.\;}
\tag{4.4}
\]

Both lie in the central ring \(\mathbb R[s]\).

### Theorem 4.4 (scalarity and multiplicativity of the norm)

For all \(P,Q\in\mathscr C\),

\[
N(P)\in\mathbb R[s],\qquad N(PQ)=N(P)N(Q).
\tag{4.5}
\]

**Proof.** From (3.1),

\[
\begin{aligned}
PP^\natural
&=(A+B\vartheta)(\bar A-B\vartheta)\\
&=A\bar A-sB\bar B+(-AB+B\bar{\bar A})\vartheta\\
&=A\bar A-sB\bar B.
\end{aligned}
\]

The result is fixed by complex conjugation, hence lies in \(\mathbb R[s]\). By Theorem 4.2 and centrality,

\[
N(PQ)=PQQ^\natural P^\natural=P\,N(Q)\,P^\natural=N(Q)N(P).
\]

Since the central factors commute, this is (4.5). \(\square\)

The norm is neither appended by definition nor extracted from a matrix after the fact. It is forced by phase reversal. This is the central structural reason counterphase arithmetic behaves as a number theory rather than as a formal symbolic game.

### Corollary 4.5 (quadratic identity and inverse)

Every \(P\in\mathscr C\) obeys

\[
\boxed{\;P^2-T(P)P+N(P)=0.\;}
\tag{4.6}
\]

If \(N(P)\) is invertible in the central coefficient ring, then

\[
\boxed{\;P^{-1}=\frac{P^\natural}{N(P)}.\;}
\tag{4.7}
\]

**Proof.** Since \(P^\natural=T(P)-P\), one has \(N(P)=P(T(P)-P)\), which rearranges to (4.6). Multiplying \(PP^\natural=N(P)\) by \(N(P)^{-1}\) proves (4.7). \(\square\)

## 5. Tensorial formulation and counterphase metric

The algebra can be expressed in structural-tensor form. Let \(p^a=(a,b,c,d)\) and write \(P=p^ae_a\), with summation over repeated indices. Define structure functions \(C_{ab}{}^c(s)\in\mathbb R[s]\) by

\[
e_ae_b=C_{ab}{}^c(s)e_c.
\tag{5.1}
\]

Associativity is the tensor identity

\[
\boxed{
C_{ab}{}^d(s)C_{dc}{}^e(s)
=C_{bc}{}^d(s)C_{ad}{}^e(s).
}
\tag{5.2}
\]

Equation (3.2) is precisely \((PQ)^c=C_{ab}{}^c p^a q^b\). The norm is the quadratic form

\[
\boxed{
N(P)=g_{ab}(s)p^ap^b,
\qquad
[g_{ab}(s)]=\operatorname{diag}(1,1,-s,-s).
}
\tag{5.3}
\]

The metric changes signature only when the scale passes through the singular fiber \(s=0\). This supplies an algebraic explanation for the sharp distinction between compact and split fibers: they are not different multiplication laws but different signatures of the same universal norm form.

| Scale regime | Norm signature | Interpretation of the \(\vartheta\)-plane |
|---|---|---|
| \(s<0\) | \((4,0)\) | compact: all nonzero elements have positive norm |
| \(s=0\) | rank \(2\) with two null directions | singular: first-order phase-reversing displacements |
| \(s>0\) | \((2,2)\) | split: null directions and determinant-zero elements appear |

The real and counterphase coordinates are therefore not merely placed side by side. They are coupled by the scale-dependent tensor \(g_{ab}(s)\), whose sign change controls the qualitative geometry of multiplication.

## 6. Real fibers: compact, singular, and split arithmetic

For each \(\rho\in\mathbb R\), define the **real fiber**

\[
\mathscr C_\rho:=\mathscr C/(s-\rho).
\tag{6.1}
\]

A fiber is obtained by the specialization \(s\mapsto\rho\). The universal algebra is a book in which every fiber is a page: the crossing rule remains constant, while the square of \(\vartheta\) receives a real value.

### Theorem 6.1 (fiber trichotomy)

Let \(\rho\in\mathbb R\).

1. If \(\rho=-\kappa^2<0\), then \(\mathscr C_\rho\cong\mathbb H\), the real quaternion algebra.
2. If \(\rho=0\), then \(\mathscr C_0=\mathbb C\oplus\mathbb C\vartheta\), where \(\vartheta^2=0\) and \(\vartheta z=\bar z\vartheta\). The ideal \(\mathbb C\vartheta\) is square-zero.
3. If \(\rho=\kappa^2>0\), then \(\mathscr C_\rho\cong M_2(\mathbb R)\).

**Proof.** For \(\rho=-\kappa^2\), set \(\mathbf j=\vartheta/\kappa\). Then \(\mathbf j^2=-1\) and \(\mathrm i\mathbf j=-\mathbf j\mathrm i\), which are the standard quaternion relations.

For \(\rho=0\), the defining relation gives \((B\vartheta)(D\vartheta)=0\), hence \(\mathbb C\vartheta\) is a square-zero ideal.

For \(\rho=\kappa^2\), define

\[
\mathrm i\longmapsto
\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\vartheta\longmapsto
\kappa\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{6.2}
\]

The images square to \(-I\) and \(\kappa^2I\), respectively, and satisfy \(\vartheta\mathrm i=-\mathrm i\vartheta\). They generate \(M_2(\mathbb R)\), giving the asserted isomorphism. \(\square\)

The three fibers are familiar mathematical territories, but the universal counterphase theory exhibits them as phases of one arithmetic. The novelty is not the isolated observation that one may choose a negative, zero, or positive square; it is the decision to compute before that choice is made and to treat the scale itself as a central coordinate.

### Proposition 6.2 (matrix realization)

The map

\[
\Phi:\mathscr C\longrightarrow M_2(\mathbb C[s]),
\qquad
\Phi(A+B\vartheta)=
\begin{pmatrix}
A&sB\\
\bar B&\bar A
\end{pmatrix},
\tag{6.3}
\]

is an injective algebra homomorphism and satisfies

\[
\det\Phi(P)=N(P).
\tag{6.4}
\]

**Proof.** Direct matrix multiplication reproduces (3.1), while the determinant equals \(A\bar A-sB\bar B\). The matrix entries recover \(A\) and \(B\), so \(\Phi\) is injective. \(\square\)

This representation makes the norm’s determinant-like character explicit without reducing the construction to a chosen matrix model.

## 7. Counterphase analysis

The quadratic identity permits a functional calculus that never requires diagonalization. Let

\[
X:=P-\frac{T(P)}{2}.
\tag{7.1}
\]

Then \(X^\natural=-X\) and, by (4.6),

\[
X^2=-N(X).
\tag{7.2}
\]

Write \(\nu:=N(X)\in\mathbb R[s]\). Define the universal even and odd functions

\[
\mathsf C_\nu(t):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\nu)^nt^{2n}}{(2n)!},
\qquad
\mathsf S_\nu(t):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\nu)^nt^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!}.
\tag{7.3}
\]

### Theorem 7.1 (counterphase exponential)

For every traceless counterphase number \(X\),

\[
\boxed{
\exp(tX)=\mathsf C_\nu(t)+\mathsf S_\nu(t)X,
\qquad \nu=N(X).
}
\tag{7.4}
\]

Moreover, \(N(\exp(tX))=1\).

**Proof.** Separate the exponential series into even and odd powers and use \(X^{2n}=(-\nu)^n\) and \(X^{2n+1}=(-\nu)^nX\). Since \(X^\natural=-X\), \((\exp(tX))^\natural=\exp(-tX)\). Therefore

\[
N(\exp(tX))=\exp(tX)\exp(-tX)=1.
\]

\(\square\)

The notation is deliberately universal. At a numerical evaluation where \(\nu>0\), \(\mathsf C_\nu(t)=\cos(\sqrt\nu\,t)\) and \(\mathsf S_\nu(t)=\sin(\sqrt\nu\,t)/\sqrt\nu\). Where \(\nu<0\), the same series become hyperbolic functions. The nilpotent boundary \(\nu=0\) gives \(\exp(tX)=1+tX\). A single formal exponential thereby passes continuously between circular, hyperbolic, and first-order propagation.

### Theorem 7.2 (polynomial reduction)

For every polynomial \(F\in\mathbb R[s][\lambda]\) and every \(P\in\mathscr C\), there exist central polynomials \(\alpha_F,\beta_F\in\mathbb R[s][T(P),N(P)]\) such that

\[
\boxed{\;F(P)=\alpha_F+\beta_FP.\;}
\tag{7.5}
\]

**Proof.** Use the quadratic relation \(P^2=T(P)P-N(P)\) to reduce powers recursively. \(\square\)

This theorem is the counterphase analogue of the fact that analytic functions of a two-eigenvalue object reduce to a scalar part plus one directional part. It supplies a practical symbolic calculus for powers, exponentials, resolvents, and rational functions.

## 8. Integral counterphase arithmetic

A number theory requires a discrete domain in which addition, multiplication, and norm remain integral. Fix \(m\in\mathbb Z\) and specialize \(s=m\). Define the **integral counterphase order**

\[
\mathcal O_m:=\mathbb Z[\mathrm i]\oplus\mathbb Z[\mathrm i]\vartheta,
\qquad
\vartheta z=\bar z\vartheta,
\qquad
\vartheta^2=m.
\tag{8.1}
\]

For

\[
P=a+b\mathrm i+c\vartheta+d\mathrm i\vartheta,
\qquad a,b,c,d\in\mathbb Z,
\]

the integral norm is

\[
\boxed{
N_m(P)=a^2+b^2-m(c^2+d^2)\in\mathbb Z.
}
\tag{8.2}
\]

### Theorem 8.1 (integral closure under multiplication and norm)

The set \(\mathcal O_m\) is a unital ring. Its norm is integer-valued and multiplicative:

\[
N_m(PQ)=N_m(P)N_m(Q).
\tag{8.3}
\]

**Proof.** Formula (3.2), after setting \(s=m\), has integer coefficients in the coordinates of \(P\) and \(Q\). Hence \(PQ\in\mathcal O_m\). Equation (8.3) is Theorem 4.4 under the same specialization. \(\square\)

### Corollary 8.2 (unit criterion)

An element \(P\in\mathcal O_m\) is a unit if and only if

\[
N_m(P)=\pm1.
\tag{8.4}
\]

**Proof.** If \(N_m(P)=\pm1\), equation (4.7) gives \(P^{-1}=\pm P^\natural\in\mathcal O_m\). Conversely, if \(PQ=1\), multiplicativity gives \(N_m(P)N_m(Q)=1\) in \(\mathbb Z\), hence \(N_m(P)=\pm1\). \(\square\)

The norm equations

\[
a^2+b^2-m(c^2+d^2)=n
\tag{8.5}
\]

are therefore the basic Diophantine surfaces of counterphase arithmetic. Their qualitative behavior changes with the sign of \(m\): for negative \(m\), the form is positive definite; for positive \(m\), it is indefinite; and for \(m=0\), it degenerates. Unlike a collection of separate quadratic forms, they arise from one multiplication law, so every composition identity is automatic:

\[
N_m(PQ)=N_m(P)N_m(Q).
\]

| Fiber parameter \(m\) | Integral norm form | Arithmetic character |
|---|---|---|
| \(m<0\) | \(a^2+b^2+|m|(c^2+d^2)\) | positive-definite norm arithmetic |
| \(m=0\) | \(a^2+b^2\) | degenerate arithmetic with a square-zero ideal |
| \(m>0\) | \(a^2+b^2-m(c^2+d^2)\) | indefinite arithmetic with nontrivial null solutions in suitable cases |

No unique-factorization assertion is needed for this initial theory. The multiplicative norm is already sufficient to organize units, factor obstructions, representation problems, and composition of solutions to (8.5).

## 9. Worked calculations

### Example 9.1 (a compact-fiber rotation)

Take \(s=-1\) and \(X=\vartheta\). Then \(N(X)=1\), \(X^2=-1\), and Theorem 7.1 gives

\[
\exp(t\vartheta)=\cos t+\vartheta\sin t.
\]

Although \(\vartheta\) is not the ordinary imaginary unit, it becomes a second compact rotation direction in the negative fiber. Because \(\vartheta\mathrm i=-\mathrm i\vartheta\), it interacts with complex phase noncommutatively.

### Example 9.2 (a split-fiber boost)

Take \(s=1\) and \(X=\vartheta\). Then \(N(X)=-1\), \(X^2=1\), and

\[
\exp(t\vartheta)=\cosh t+\vartheta\sinh t.
\]

The same generator that created a rotation in the compact fiber creates a hyperbolic one-parameter subgroup in the split fiber. The difference is controlled solely by the scale evaluation.

### Example 9.3 (the singular phase impulse)

Take \(s=0\) and \(X=(1+\mathrm i)\vartheta\). Then \(X^2=0\), so

\[
\exp(tX)=1+t(1+\mathrm i)\vartheta.
\]

The nilpotent fiber contains finite first-order transformations. Its phase reversal is nontrivial even though its square vanishes: for \(z\in\mathbb C\),

\[
Xz=(1+\mathrm i)\bar z\,\vartheta,
\qquad
zX=z(1+\mathrm i)\vartheta.
\]

Thus the singular fiber is not merely the commutative dual-number limit; it retains the orientation-reversing crossing operation.

### Example 9.4 (integral norm composition)

In \(\mathcal O_2\), choose \(P=1+\mathrm i+\vartheta\) and \(Q=2+\mathrm i+(1-\mathrm i)\vartheta\). Then

\[
N_2(P)=1^2+1^2-2(1^2)=0,
\qquad
N_2(Q)=2^2+1^2-2(1^2+(-1)^2)=1.
\]

Consequently, \(N_2(PQ)=0\), showing that a norm-one counterphase unit preserves the null cone under multiplication. Such calculations make the split norm geometry arithmetically explicit.

## 10. Interpretation: what is new about a counterphase number?

A complex number has the form \(a+b\mathrm i\): it combines magnitude with one quarter-turn direction. A counterphase number has the form

\[
P=A(s)+B(s)\vartheta,
\]

where \(A\) is an ordinary complex scale-polynomial and \(B\vartheta\) is a phase-reversing component. The new datum is not simply an additional coordinate. It is the rule that the coordinate acts **semilinearly** on complex phase:

\[
\vartheta(re^{\mathrm i\phi})=re^{-\mathrm i\phi}\vartheta.
\]

The operation contains a precise algebraic distinction between a transformation and its counterphase. In ordinary complex arithmetic, conjugation is usually performed outside the multiplication law. Here conjugation is enacted when a scalar crosses \(\vartheta\). This changes the architecture of the arithmetic: a central norm emerges as \(A\bar A-sB\bar B\), and the sign of \(s\) becomes a geometrically meaningful variable.

The theory is consequently suitable for problems whose algebra must keep track of a phase together with a reversible phase-reflecting channel. The present paper establishes the formal core. Natural next developments include counterphase modules, spectral theory for the matrix realization (6.3), integral norm-representation theorems for (8.5), and a geometry of counterphase projective lines over the split and singular fibers.

## 11. Research programme

The following questions are mathematically concrete consequences of the new framework.

| Programme | Precise objective | Expected method |
|---|---|---|
| **Integral representation theory** | Classify the integers represented by \(N_m\) and the orbits of primitive solutions to (8.5) | local congruence analysis, composition by multiplication in \(\mathcal O_m\) |
| **Counterphase spectral theory** | Develop eigenvalue and resolvent theory for \(\Phi(P)\) while preserving the intrinsic \(T,N\) calculus | quadratic identity, matrix realization, central discriminant |
| **Singular-fiber geometry** | Characterize modules and projective objects over \(\mathscr C_0\) | square-zero extension methods with semilinear action |
| **Scale-dynamic analysis** | Treat \(s\) as a variable and study degenerations across \(s=0\) | algebraic families and signature transition of \(g_{ab}(s)\) |
| **Counterphase differential systems** | Study \(Y'(t)=XY(t)\) for variable or matrix-valued \(X\in\mathscr C\) | universal exponential, ordered products, matrix representation |

The analytical advantage of the universal approach is that a proof may be performed over \(\mathbb R[s]\) first and only then evaluated at the compact, singular, or split regime. This prevents the common loss of structure that occurs when a sign choice is made too early.

## 12. Conclusion

Counterphase arithmetic introduces a number system in which a new unit \(\vartheta\) reverses complex phase and has an unfixed central square \(s\). From the two axioms

\[
\vartheta z=\bar z\vartheta,
\qquad
s=\vartheta^2\in Z(\mathscr C),
\]

there follows a complete algebraic package: normal form, noncommutative product, anti-involution, multiplicative scalar norm, inverse formula, tensor metric, matrix realization, analytic functional calculus, and integral norm arithmetic. The compact, singular, and split number systems are not competing definitions but fibers of a single universal object.

The conceptual step is simple but consequential. Instead of asking only what new number should square to, counterphase theory asks how a new number should **act when it meets phase**. The answer is a reversible phase crossing. Once that law is admitted, scale becomes a variable, the norm becomes canonical, and three distinct geometries become one continuous algebraic family.

## Appendix A. Full coordinate verification of the norm identity

Let \(P=(a,b,c,d)\) and \(Q=(e,f,g,h)\) denote coordinates in the basis \((1,\mathrm i,\vartheta,\mathrm i\vartheta)\). From (3.2),

\[
\begin{aligned}
r_0&=ae-bf+s(cg+dh),\\
r_1&=af+be+s(dg-ch),\\
r_2&=ag-bh+ce+df,\\
r_3&=ah+bg+de-cf.
\end{aligned}
\]

Substitution into \(N(PQ)=r_0^2+r_1^2-s(r_2^2+r_3^2)\), followed by expansion and collection of terms, yields

\[
N(PQ)=(a^2+b^2-s(c^2+d^2))(e^2+f^2-s(g^2+h^2)).
\]

This is the coordinate form of Theorem 4.4. An accompanying symbolic check verifies both this polynomial identity and the associativity identity (5.2); the reproducible source is supplied with this preprint.
