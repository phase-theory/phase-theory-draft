## Abstract

This paper introduces **Resonance Number Theory (RNT)**, a proposed new mathematical framework in which a number is studied not only through its numerical value, divisibility, magnitude, residue classes, or factorization, but through the **structured family of arithmetic transitions generated by that number**.

The central object of RNT is the **resonance number**

[
\mathfrak N(n)=
\left(
n,,
\mathcal T(n),,
\mathcal R(n),,
\mathcal C(n)
\right),
]

where (n) is the underlying integer, (\mathcal T(n)) is its transition spectrum under a prescribed family of arithmetic transformations, (\mathcal R(n)) is the resulting resonance profile, and (\mathcal C(n)) is a hierarchy of invariant correlations among its transition states.

The fundamental hypothesis is that ordinary arithmetic sees only the **static projection**

[
n,
]

whereas RNT studies the larger object

[
n
\longmapsto
{\text{arithmetic states accessible from }n}.
]

The theory therefore replaces the traditional question

> “What properties does the number (n) possess?”

with the more general question

> “What arithmetic structure does (n) generate under controlled transformations?”

This produces a new hierarchy of numerical objects: **primitive numbers, resonant numbers, antiresonant numbers, phase-locked numbers, transition-complete numbers, arithmetic attractors, and resonance classes**.

The framework is designed to interact with classical number theory while remaining formally independent of it. Contemporary number theory already contains deep interactions among algebraic, analytic, (p)-adic, geometric, combinatorial, probabilistic, and dynamical methods. RNT proposes a different organizing principle: **transition structure as a primary arithmetic invariant**.

The principal conjecture of the theory is the **Resonance Compression Conjecture**:

[
\boxed{
\text{For every sufficiently large arithmetic transformation family, }
\mathcal R(n)
\text{ contains asymptotically more structural information than }
n\text{ alone.}
}
]

A second central conjecture, the **Prime Resonance Conjecture**, predicts that prime numbers are characterized not merely by absence of nontrivial divisors but by a distinctive minimum-complexity transition spectrum.

The paper develops the axioms, algebraic construction, resonance operators, spectral invariants, arithmetic geometry, prime theory, analytic theory, algorithms, examples, conjectures, and possible extensions of this proposed framework.
