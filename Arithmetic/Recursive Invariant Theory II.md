# Recursive Invariant Theory II  
## Generative Invariants, Stability Operators, and Spectral Recursion

**Preprint**

---

## Abstract

Classical invariant theory studies quantities preserved by group actions: given a group \(G\) acting on a space or algebraic object \(X\), one seeks the invariant ring, module, or algebra \(I(G)=\mathcal{A}^G\). Recursive Invariant Theory II (RIT-II) reframes invariance as a dynamical and generative phenomenon. Instead of treating invariants as static elements of a fixed-point subalgebra, RIT-II studies recursive systems of the form
\[
I_{n+1}=\mathcal{R}(I_n),
\]
where \(\mathcal{R}\) is an invariant generator acting on an algebra, sheaf, tensor field, cohomology theory, or feature space. The central primitive objects are recursive invariants, invariant generators, stability operators, and invariant spectra. The present paper develops a systematic foundation for RIT-II. We introduce equivariant recursive operators, prove invariance propagation theorems, develop spectral and stability theory for linear and nonlinear recursions, formulate tensorial recursive invariants on Riemannian manifolds, and demonstrate applications to representation theory, differential geometry, algebraic topology, and computer vision. The central thesis is that many invariant-theoretic structures are best understood not merely as fixed points of group actions but as orbits of equivariant evolutionary operators.

---

## 1. Introduction

Classical invariant theory is organized around the fixed-point principle. If a group \(G\) acts on a vector space, algebra, manifold, or category \(\mathcal{C}\), one studies the subobject
\[
\mathcal{A}^G=\{a\in \mathcal{A}\mid g\cdot a=a\ \text{for all }g\in G\}.
\]
In polynomial invariant theory, for example, if \(G\) acts linearly on a vector space \(V\), one studies the invariant ring
\[
\mathbb{K}[V]^G.
\]
In differential geometry, one studies scalar curvature invariants invariant under diffeomorphisms or isometries. In topology, one studies characteristic classes invariant under pullback. In vision, one studies descriptors invariant under rotations, translations, scalings, or more general transformations.

The classical perspective is powerful but fundamentally static. It asks:

> What remains unchanged under a transformation group?

Recursive Invariant Theory II asks a different question:

> What invariant structures generate further invariant structures through recursion?

The central object is not merely an invariant \(I\), but a recursive invariant system
\[
I_0,\quad I_1=\mathcal{R}(I_0),\quad I_2=\mathcal{R}(I_1),\quad \ldots,
\]
or compactly,
\[
I_{n+1}=\mathcal{R}(I_n).
\]
Here \(\mathcal{R}\) is an equivariant or invariant-preserving operator. The invariant is no longer a terminal object; it becomes a seed.

This paper develops RIT-II as a formal theory of generative invariance. The four primitive objects are:

1. **Recursive invariants**: sequences \(\{I_n\}_{n\ge 0}\) generated by recursion.
2. **Invariant generators**: operators \(\mathcal{R}\) that map invariants to new invariants.
3. **Stability operators**: linearizations \(D\mathcal{R}\) governing perturbations and asymptotic behavior.
4. **Invariant spectra**: spectral data of \(\mathcal{R}\) or \(D\mathcal{R}\), encoding growth, decay, periodicity, and structural stability.

The central philosophical shift is as follows:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Classical invariant theory: } \mathcal{A}^G
}
\]

\[
\boxed{
\text{Recursive invariant theory: } (\mathcal{A}^G,\mathcal{R})
}
\]

Thus invariance becomes a dynamical system internal to the invariant subalgebra.

## 2. Foundational Framework

Let \(\mathbb{K}\) denote \(\mathbb{R}\) or \(\mathbb{C}\). Let \(G\) be a group acting on an algebraic or geometric object, and let \(\mathcal{A}\) be an algebra of observables associated with that object. The action of \(G\) on \(\mathcal{A}\) is denoted
\[
g:\mathcal{A}\to \mathcal{A},\qquad a\mapsto g\cdot a.
\]
We assume that the action is by automorphisms of the relevant algebraic structure.

### 2.1 Invariant subalgebra

The classical invariant subalgebra is
\[
\mathcal{A}^G=\{a\in\mathcal{A}\mid g\cdot a=a,\ \forall g\in G\}.
\]

### 2.2 Recursive invariant operators

We now introduce the central operator-theoretic object.

**Definition 2.1.** A map \(\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}\) is called a **recursive invariant operator** if it preserves invariants:
\[
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{A}^G)\subseteq \mathcal{A}^G.
\]
If, more strongly,
\[
\mathcal{R}(g\cdot a)=g\cdot \mathcal{R}(a)
\]
for all \(g\in G\) and \(a\in\mathcal{A}\), then \(\mathcal{R}\) is called **equivariant**.

Equivariance implies invariant preservation:
\[
a\in\mathcal{A}^G
\implies
g\cdot \mathcal{R}(a)
=
\mathcal{R}(g\cdot a)
=
\mathcal{R}(a),
\]
so \(\mathcal{R}(a)\in\mathcal{A}^G\).

### 2.3 Recursive invariants

**Definition 2.2.** Given \(I_0\in\mathcal{A}^G\) and a recursive invariant operator \(\mathcal{R}\), the sequence
\[
I_{n+1}=\mathcal{R}(I_n),\qquad n\ge 0,
\]
is called a **recursive invariant** generated by \((I_0,\mathcal{R})\).

The sequence may be algebraic, analytic, geometric, or categorical depending on the nature of \(\mathcal{A}\).

### 2.4 Invariant generators

An operator \(\mathcal{R}\) is an **invariant generator** if it produces genuinely new invariants, not merely scalar multiples or trivial repetitions.

A quantitative measure is the **generative rank**.

**Definition 2.3.** The generative rank of \((I_0,\mathcal{R})\) is
\[
\operatorname{grank}(I_0,\mathcal{R})
=
\dim_{\mathbb{K}}
\operatorname{span}\{I_0,I_1,I_2,\ldots\}.
\]
If this dimension is finite, the recursion satisfies a linear or nonlinear recurrence relation. If it is infinite, the generator produces an infinite-dimensional invariant orbit.

More generally, a family \(\{\mathcal{R}_\alpha\}_{\alpha\in A}\) may generate a semigroup
\[
\langle \mathcal{R}_\alpha\rangle
\]
whose orbits define multi-parameter recursive invariants.

### 2.5 Fixed points and stability operators

A particularly important case occurs when \(I_*\) is a fixed point:
\[
\mathcal{R}(I_*)=I_*.
\]
If \(\mathcal{R}\) is differentiable near \(I_*\), define the **stability operator**
\[
\mathcal{L}_{I_*}=D\mathcal{R}_{I_*}.
\]
For a perturbation
\[
I_n=I_*+\varepsilon_n,
\]
one obtains
\[
\varepsilon_{n+1}
=
\mathcal{L}_{I_*}\varepsilon_n
+
O(\|\varepsilon_n\|^2).
\]
The spectral data of \(\mathcal{L}_{I_*}\) determine local stability, instability, and marginal behavior.

### 2.6 Invariant spectra

If \(\mathcal{R}\) is linear, or if we study the linearized recursion near a fixed point, the relevant spectrum is
\[
\sigma(\mathcal{R})=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{K}\mid \mathcal{R}-\lambda I\ \text{is not invertible}\}.
\]
For a given seed \(I_0\), the **spectral support** is the subset of eigenvalues that actually appear in the orbit of \(I_0\).

**Definition 2.4.** The **invariant spectrum** of the recursive invariant \((I_0,\mathcal{R})\) is
\[
\sigma(I_0,\mathcal{R})
=
\{\lambda\in\sigma(\mathcal{R})\mid P_\lambda I_0\neq 0\},
\]
where \(P_\lambda\) is the spectral projection associated with \(\lambda\), when such projections exist.

The invariant spectrum is a recursive refinement of classical invariant data: it records not only what is invariant, but how invariants evolve under generation.

## 3. Invariance Propagation

The most basic theorem of RIT-II is that equivariant recursion preserves invariance.

### Theorem 3.1: Propagation of invariance

Let \(\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}\) be equivariant with respect to a group action of \(G\). If \(I_0\in\mathcal{A}^G\) and
\[
I_{n+1}=\mathcal{R}(I_n),
\]
then
\[
I_n\in\mathcal{A}^G
\]
for all \(n\ge 0\).

**Proof.** The claim is proved by induction. For \(n=0\), \(I_0\in\mathcal{A}^G\) by assumption. Suppose \(I_n\in\mathcal{A}^G\). Then for every \(g\in G\),
\[
g\cdot I_{n+1}
=
g\cdot \mathcal{R}(I_n)
=
\mathcal{R}(g\cdot I_n)
=
\mathcal{R}(I_n)
=
I_{n+1}.
\]
Thus \(I_{n+1}\in\mathcal{A}^G\). Induction completes the proof. \(\square\)

This theorem is the recursive analogue of the classical statement that invariant operations preserve invariants.

## 4. Linear Recursive Invariant Theory

Assume now that \(\mathcal{A}^G\) is a finite-dimensional vector space or, more generally, a Banach space, and that \(\mathcal{R}\) is linear:
\[
\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{A}^G\to\mathcal{A}^G.
\]
Then the recursion is
\[
I_n=\mathcal{R}^n I_0.
\]

### 4.1 Spectral decomposition

Suppose \(\mathcal{A}^G\) is finite-dimensional over \(\mathbb{C}\). Let
\[
\sigma(\mathcal{R})=\{\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_m\}
\]
be the spectrum of \(\mathcal{R}\). The Jordan decomposition gives
\[
\mathcal{A}^G
=
\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\sigma(\mathcal{R})}
E_\lambda,
\]
where \(E_\lambda\) is the generalized eigenspace. Write
\[
I_0=\sum_{\lambda} I_{0,\lambda},
\qquad I_{0,\lambda}\in E_\lambda.
\]
Then
\[
I_n
=
\sum_{\lambda}
\mathcal{R}^n I_{0,\lambda}.
\]

If \(\mathcal{R}\) is diagonalizable, then
\[
I_n
=
\sum_{\lambda}
\lambda^n I_{0,\lambda}.
\]
If \(\mathcal{R}\) has nontrivial Jordan blocks, then
\[
\mathcal{R}^n
=
\sum_{\lambda}
\lambda^n
\sum_{k=0}^{m_\lambda-1}
\binom{n}{k}
\lambda^{-k}
N_\lambda^k,
\]
where \(N_\lambda=\mathcal{R}-\lambda I\) on \(E_\lambda\). Hence
\[
I_n
=
\sum_{\lambda}
\lambda^n
\sum_{k=0}^{m_\lambda-1}
\binom{n}{k}
\lambda^{-k}
N_\lambda^k I_{0,\lambda}.
\]

### 4.2 Growth theorem

Define the asymptotic growth rate
\[
\gamma(I_0,\mathcal{R})
=
\limsup_{n\to\infty}
\frac{1}{n}
\log \|I_n\|.
\]

**Theorem 4.1.** If \(\mathcal{R}\) is linear on a finite-dimensional normed space, then
\[
\gamma(I_0,\mathcal{R})
=
\log
\max_{\lambda\in\sigma(I_0,\mathcal{R})}
|\lambda|.
\]

**Proof.** From the Jordan decomposition, the dominant term in \(I_n\) is a finite sum of terms of the form
\[
n^k \lambda^n v.
\]
Polynomial factors \(n^k\) do not contribute to the exponential growth rate:
\[
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log n^k=0.
\]
Therefore the exponential growth rate is determined by the largest \(|\lambda|\) appearing with nonzero coefficient. \(\square\)

Thus the invariant spectrum controls asymptotic behavior.

### 4.3 Recursive zeta function

For finite-dimensional linear recursions, define the recursive zeta function
\[
\zeta_{\mathcal{R}}(z)
=
\det(I-z\mathcal{R})^{-1}.
\]
For a seed \(I_0\), one may define a restricted zeta function using the minimal polynomial of the cyclic subspace generated by \(I_0\):
\[
\zeta_{I_0,\mathcal{R}}(z)
=
\prod_{\lambda\in\sigma(I_0,\mathcal{R})}
(1-\lambda z)^{-m_\lambda},
\]
where \(m_\lambda\) is the size of the largest Jordan block of \(\mathcal{R}\) contributing to the orbit of \(I_0\).

This object is a dynamical invariant of the recursive invariant system.

## 5. Nonlinear Recursive Invariants and Stability

Let \(\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{A}^G\) be a Banach space and let
\[
\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{B}
\]
be a nonlinear \(C^1\) map.

### 5.1 Fixed points and linearization

Let \(I_*\in\mathcal{B}\) satisfy
\[
\mathcal{R}(I_*)=I_*.
\]
The stability operator is
\[
\mathcal{L}_{I_*}=D\mathcal{R}_{I_*}.
\]
Writing
\[
I_n=I_*+\varepsilon_n,
\]
we obtain
\[
\varepsilon_{n+1}
=
\mathcal{L}_{I_*}\varepsilon_n
+
\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon_n),
\]
where
\[
\|\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon)\|=o(\|\varepsilon\|).
\]

### 5.2 Local stability theorem

**Theorem 5.1.** Suppose \(\mathcal{L}_{I_*}\) has spectral radius
\[
\rho(\mathcal{L}_{I_*})<1.
\]
Then \(I_*\) is locally asymptotically stable under the recursion
\[
I_{n+1}=\mathcal{R}(I_n).
\]
That is, there exists a neighborhood \(U\) of \(I_*\) such that if \(I_0\in U\), then
\[
\mathcal{R}^n(I_0)\to I_*.
\]

**Proof.** Since \(\rho(\mathcal{L}_{I_*})<1\), there exists an equivalent norm and a constant \(q<1\) such that
\[
\|\mathcal{L}_{I_*}\|\le q.
\]
For sufficiently small \(\varepsilon\),
\[
\|\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon)\|
\le
c\|\varepsilon\|,
\]
with \(c>0\) arbitrarily small. Hence
\[
\|\varepsilon_{n+1}\|
\le
(q+c)\|\varepsilon_n\|.
\]
Choose \(c<1-q\). Then
\[
\|\varepsilon_{n+1}\|
\le
\theta\|\varepsilon_n\|,
\qquad
\theta<1.
\]
Therefore \(\varepsilon_n\to 0\), and \(I_n\to I_*\). \(\square\)

### 5.3 Instability theorem

**Theorem 5.2.** Suppose \(\mathcal{L}_{I_*}\) has an eigenvalue \(\lambda\) with
\[
|\lambda|>1.
\]
Then \(I_*\) is spectrally unstable: there exist arbitrarily small perturbations whose orbits leave a sufficiently small neighborhood of \(I_*\).

**Proof sketch.** Let \(E_u\) be the unstable spectral subspace associated with eigenvalues of modulus greater than one. On \(E_u\), the linearized dynamics expands. For sufficiently small perturbations in the unstable direction, the linear term dominates the nonlinear remainder for a finite number of iterates, forcing the orbit away from \(I_*\). \(\square\)

### 5.4 Spectral classification

The spectrum of \(\mathcal{L}_{I_*}\) decomposes into
\[
\sigma_s=\{\lambda:|\lambda|<1\},
\]
\[
\sigma_c=\{\lambda:|\lambda|=1\},
\]
\[
\sigma_u=\{\lambda:|\lambda|>1\}.
\]
We call these the stable, center, and unstable spectra.

A fixed recursive invariant \(I_*\) is:

- **strictly stable** if \(\sigma(\mathcal{L}_{I_*})\subset\{|\lambda|<1\}\);
- **marginally stable** if \(\sigma(\mathcal{L}_{I_*})\subset\{|\lambda|\le 1\}\) and unit-modulus eigenvalues are semisimple;
- **unstable** if \(\sigma_u\neq\varnothing\).

This classification is central to geometric and computational applications.

## 6. Tensorial Recursive Invariants

A natural setting for RIT-II is differential geometry, where invariants are tensor fields or scalar contractions constructed from a metric, connection, curvature, and covariant derivatives.

Let \((M,g)\) be a Riemannian manifold, and let \(G\subseteq \operatorname{Isom}(M,g)\). Tensor fields are written in abstract index notation. For example,
\[
T^{a_1\cdots a_r}{}_{b_1\cdots b_s}
\]
denotes a tensor of type \((r,s)\).

### 6.1 Natural recursive operators

A recursive tensor operator \(\mathcal{R}\) is natural if it is constructed from the metric \(g_{ab}\), curvature tensor \(R_{abcd}\), covariant derivative \(\nabla_a\), contractions, tensor products, and fixed background invariant tensors.

A general tensorial recursion may be written as
\[
I_{n+1}^{A}
=
C^{A}{}_{B C_1\cdots C_k}
\nabla^{C_1}\cdots \nabla^{C_k}
I_n^{B}
+
Q^{A}(I_n),
\]
where \(A,B,C_i\) denote multi-indices, \(C^{A}{}_{B C_1\cdots C_k}\) is an invariant tensor built from \(g\), \(R_{abcd}\), and \(\nabla R_{abcd}\), and \(Q^{A}\) is an algebraic expression polynomial in \(I_n\).

### 6.2 Invariance propagation for tensor recursions

**Theorem 6.1.** Let \(\mathcal{R}\) be a natural tensor operator built from \(g\), \(\nabla\), curvature, and invariant contractions. If \(I_0\) is invariant under \(G\), then the recursively defined tensors
\[
I_{n+1}=\mathcal{R}(I_n)
\]
are invariant under \(G\).

**Proof.** Let \(\phi\in G\). Since \(\phi\) is an isometry,
\[
\phi^*g=g,
\]
\[
\phi^*\nabla=\nabla,
\]
\[
\phi^*R_{abcd}=R_{abcd}.
\]
Naturality of \(\mathcal{R}\) implies
\[
\phi^*\mathcal{R}(T)
=
\mathcal{R}(\phi^*T)
\]
for any tensor field \(T\). If \(I_n\) is invariant, then \(\phi^*I_n=I_n\), hence
\[
\phi^*I_{n+1}
=
\phi^*\mathcal{R}(I_n)
=
\mathcal{R}(\phi^*I_n)
=
\mathcal{R}(I_n)
=
I_{n+1}.
\]
The result follows by induction. \(\square\)

### 6.3 Scalar recursive invariants

Given tensor invariants \(I_n^A\), scalar invariants may be obtained by complete contraction:
\[
S_n
=
C_A I_n^A,
\]
where \(C_A\) is an invariant covariant tensor. Then
\[
S_n\in C^\infty(M)^G.
\]
Global invariants may be formed by integration:
\[
\mathcal{S}_n
=
\int_M S_n\,d\operatorname{vol}_g,
\]
assuming compactness or suitable decay.

### 6.4 Example: Lichnerowicz-type recursion

Let \(h_{ab}\) be a symmetric 2-tensor. Define
\[
h^{(n+1)}_{ab}
=
\Delta_L h^{(n)}_{ab},
\]
where \(\Delta_L\) is the Lichnerowicz Laplacian:
\[
(\Delta_L h)_{ab}
=
\nabla^c\nabla_c h_{ab}
+
2R_{acbd}h^{cd}
-
R_a{}^c h_{cb}
-
R_b{}^c h_{ac}.
\]
If \(h^{(0)}_{ab}\) is invariant under \(G\subseteq \operatorname{Isom}(M,g)\), then all \(h^{(n)}_{ab}\) are invariant.

The associated scalar recursion is
\[
I_n
=
g^{ab}h^{(n)}_{ab}.
\]
The spectral properties of \(\Delta_L\) give the invariant spectrum of the recursion.

## 7. Representation-Theoretic Recursive Invariants

Let \(G\) be a reductive algebraic group acting linearly on a finite-dimensional vector space \(V\). Let
\[
\mathcal{A}=\operatorname{Sym}(V^*)
\]
be the algebra of polynomial functions on \(V\). The classical invariant ring is
\[
\mathcal{A}^G=\mathbb{K}[V]^G.
\]

### 7.1 Equivariant operators on polynomial invariants

Let
\[
\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}
\]
be a \(G\)-equivariant algebraic operator. Then
\[
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{A}^G)\subseteq \mathcal{A}^G.
\]
Thus every seed invariant \(I_0\in\mathcal{A}^G\) generates a recursive invariant
\[
I_{n+1}=\mathcal{R}(I_n).
\]

If \(\mathcal{R}\) is homogeneous of degree \(d\), then
\[
\deg(I_{n+1})=\deg(I_n)+d,
\]
or, in the multiplicative case,
\[
\deg(I_{n+1})=d\deg(I_n).
\]
Thus recursive invariants can generate hierarchies of higher-degree invariants.

### 7.2 Casimir recursion

Let \(\mathfrak{g}\) be a semisimple Lie algebra and let \(U(\mathfrak{g})\) be its universal enveloping algebra. The center
\[
Z(U(\mathfrak{g}))
\]
acts on \(G\)-modules by scalars on irreducible components. Let \(z\in Z(U(\mathfrak{g}))\), and let
\[
D_z:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}
\]
be the corresponding invariant differential operator.

If
\[
\mathcal{A}=\bigoplus_\pi \mathcal{A}_\pi
\]
is the decomposition into irreducible \(G\)-modules, then
\[
D_z|_{\mathcal{A}_\pi}=\chi_\pi(z)\operatorname{Id},
\]
where \(\chi_\pi(z)\) is the central character.

Define the recursion
\[
I_{n+1}=D_z I_n.
\]
Then
\[
I_n
=
\sum_\pi
\chi_\pi(z)^n
P_\pi I_0,
\]
where \(P_\pi\) is the projection onto \(\mathcal{A}_\pi\).

Thus the invariant spectrum is the set of central characters appearing in the seed:
\[
\sigma(I_0,D_z)
=
\{\chi_\pi(z)\mid P_\pi I_0\neq 0\}.
\]

This gives a direct bridge between recursive invariant theory and representation theory.

### 7.3 Transvectants and classical invariant theory

For \(G=SL_2(\mathbb{K})\), let \(V_d=\operatorname{Sym}^d(\mathbb{K}^2)\) be the space of binary forms of degree \(d\). Given two binary forms \(f\) and \(h\), their \(r\)-th transvectant is
\[
(f,h)_r
=
\frac{(d-r)!(e-r)!}{d!e!}
\sum_{k=0}^r
(-1)^k
\binom{r}{k}
\frac{\partial^r f}{\partial x^{r-k}\partial y^k}
\frac{\partial^r h}{\partial x^k\partial y^{r-k}},
\]
where \(f\) has degree \(d\) and \(h\) has degree \(e\).

The transvectant is \(SL_2\)-equivariant. If \(Q\) is an invariant binary form, one may define a recursive invariant by
\[
I_{n+1}=(I_n,Q)_r.
\]
If the degrees and weights are compatible, this produces a new invariant or covariant.

Thus classical invariant-theoretic operations such as transvectants, Cayley’s \(\Omega\)-process, and polarization operators become invariant generators in RIT-II.

## 8. Geometric Recursive Invariants

Differential geometry supplies a rich class of recursive invariants through curvature tensors, characteristic classes, geometric flows, and natural differential operators.

### 8.1 Scalar curvature invariants

Let \((M,g)\) be a Riemannian manifold. The algebra of scalar polynomial curvature invariants is generated by complete contractions of tensors of the form
\[
\nabla_{a_1}\cdots \nabla_{a_k}R_{bcde}.
\]
A natural scalar differential operator
\[
\mathcal{D}:C^\infty(M)\to C^\infty(M)
\]
constructed from \(g,\nabla,R_{abcd}\) maps isometry-invariant functions to isometry-invariant functions.

Given an initial scalar invariant \(I_0\), define
\[
I_{n+1}=\mathcal{D}I_n.
\]
For example,
\[
I_{n+1}=\Delta_g I_n
\]
or more generally
\[
I_{n+1}
=
\Delta_g I_n
+
P(R_{abcd},\nabla R_{abcd},\ldots)I_n,
\]
where \(P\) is a scalar invariant polynomial.

### 8.2 Recursive invariants from geometric flows

Let \(g(t)\) evolve by a geometric flow
\[
\frac{\partial g_{ij}}{\partial t}
=
F_{ij}(g),
\]
where \(F_{ij}\) is a natural symmetric 2-tensor, such as
\[
F_{ij}=-2R_{ij}
\]
for Ricci flow.

A discrete recursion may be written as
\[
g_{n+1}
=
g_n+\varepsilon F(g_n).
\]
Given any natural scalar invariant functional
\[
\mathcal{I}[g],
\]
one obtains a recursive invariant sequence
\[
I_n=\mathcal{I}[g_n].
\]

For Ricci flow,
\[
\frac{\partial g_{ij}}{\partial t}
=
-2R_{ij}.
\]
The scalar curvature evolves by
\[
\frac{\partial R}{\partial t}
=
\Delta R+2|R_{ij}|^2.
\]
A first-order discrete recursion gives
\[
R_{n+1}
=
R_n
+
\varepsilon
\left(
\Delta R_n+2|R_{ij}^{(n)}|^2
\right)
+
O(\varepsilon^2).
\]
Thus curvature invariants generate higher curvature invariants under flow.

### 8.3 Stability of Einstein metrics

Let \(g_*\) be an Einstein metric:
\[
R_{ij}[g_*]=\lambda g_{ij}.
\]
Consider a metric recursion
\[
g_{n+1}
=
g_n-\varepsilon \operatorname{Ric}(g_n).
\]
Let
\[
g_n=g_*+h_n.
\]
The linearized recursion has the form
\[
h_{n+1}
=
h_n
-
\varepsilon D\operatorname{Ric}_{g_*}(h_n).
\]
In transverse-traceless gauge, the relevant stability operator is essentially the Lichnerowicz Laplacian:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{g_*}h_{ij}
=
\nabla^k\nabla_k h_{ij}
+
2R_{ikjl}h^{kl}.
\]
The spectrum of \(\mathcal{L}_{g_*}\) determines whether recursive curvature invariants stabilize, oscillate, or diverge near \(g_*\).

Thus RIT-II provides a dynamical interpretation of geometric stability.

## 9. Topological Recursive Invariants

In algebraic topology, invariants are often natural transformations from geometric objects to cohomology theories. RIT-II applies naturally because stable cohomology operations are invariant-preserving recursive operators.

### 9.1 Characteristic classes as invariants

Let \(E\to X\) be a vector bundle. Characteristic classes such as
\[
c_i(E)\in H^{2i}(X;\mathbb{Z}),
\]
\[
p_i(E)\in H^{4i}(X;\mathbb{Z}),
\]
\[
w_i(E)\in H^i(X;\mathbb{Z}_2),
\]
are natural under pullback. If \(f:Y\to X\), then
\[
c_i(f^*E)=f^*c_i(E).
\]
Thus characteristic classes are topological invariants.

### 9.2 Cohomology operations as invariant generators

Let
\[
\mathcal{O}:H^*(X;R)\to H^{*+d}(X;R)
\]
be a stable cohomology operation. Stability means that \(\mathcal{O}\) commutes with pullback:
\[
\mathcal{O}(f^*a)=f^*(\mathcal{O}a).
\]
Therefore, if \(I_0\) is a natural topological invariant, then
\[
I_{n+1}=\mathcal{O}(I_n)
\]
is again a natural topological invariant.

### 9.3 Steenrod recursion

For mod-2 cohomology, the Steenrod squares
\[
Sq^i:H^*(-;\mathbb{Z}_2)\to H^{*+i}(-;\mathbb{Z}_2)
\]
are natural operations. Given a Stiefel-Whitney class \(w(E)\), define
\[
I_{n+1}=Sq(I_n),
\]
where \(Sq=\sum_i Sq^i\) is the total Steenrod square.

By naturality,
\[
Sq(f^*I_n)=f^*Sq(I_n),
\]
so the recursive sequence remains topological invariant.

The Cartan formula
\[
Sq(a\smile b)
=
Sq(a)\smile Sq(b)
\]
ensures compatibility with multiplicative invariant structures.

### 9.4 Adams operations and K-theoretic spectra

In complex topological \(K\)-theory, the Adams operations
\[
\Psi^k:K^0(X)\to K^0(X)
\]
are natural ring endomorphisms. For a line bundle \(L\),
\[
\Psi^k(L)=L^{\otimes k}.
\]
The Chern character satisfies
\[
\operatorname{ch}(\Psi^k x)
=
\sum_{m\ge 0}
k^m \operatorname{ch}_m(x).
\]
Define the recursion
\[
I_{n+1}=\Psi^k(I_n).
\]
Then
\[
\operatorname{ch}_m(I_n)
=
k^{mn}
\operatorname{ch}_m(I_0).
\]
Thus the degree-\(m\) component evolves with eigenvalue \(k^m\). The invariant spectrum is
\[
\sigma(I_0,\Psi^k)
=
\{k^m\mid \operatorname{ch}_m(I_0)\neq 0\}.
\]

This is a clean example of RIT-II in algebraic topology: recursive invariants, invariant generators, stability operators, and spectra arise from natural cohomology operations.

## 10. Recursive Invariants in Computer Vision

Computer vision provides a natural applied setting for RIT-II. Images and geometric features are often represented as tensor fields, and invariance under transformations such as translations, rotations, scalings, and gauge transformations is central.

### 10.1 Feature fields and group actions

Let \(F:\mathbb{R}^d\to V\) be a feature field, where \(V\) is a representation space of a group \(G\). The action of \(G\) on fields is
\[
(g\cdot F)(x)
=
\rho(g)F(g^{-1}x),
\]
where \(\rho:G\to GL(V)\) is a representation.

A descriptor \(D(F)\) is invariant if
\[
D(g\cdot F)=D(F).
\]

### 10.2 Equivariant recursive feature operators

Let
\[
\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{F}\to\mathcal{F}
\]
be an operator on feature fields. We require equivariance:
\[
\mathcal{R}(g\cdot F)=g\cdot \mathcal{R}(F).
\]
Then the recursive feature sequence
\[
F_{n+1}=\mathcal{R}(F_n)
\]
satisfies
\[
F_n=\mathcal{R}^n(F_0).
\]
If \(D\) is an invariant functional, then
\[
I_n=D(F_n)
\]
is a recursive scalar invariant.

### 10.3 Tensorial feature recursion

Let \(F^A(x)\) be a feature tensor field, where \(A\) is a multi-index. A local-linear recursive operator may be written as
\[
F_{n+1}^{A}(x)
=
\int_{\mathbb{R}^d}
K^{A}{}_{B}(x,y;F_n)
F_n^{B}(y)\,dy
+
N^{A}(F_n)(x),
\]
where \(K^{A}{}_{B}\) is a steerable kernel and \(N^{A}\) is a pointwise nonlinearity.

Equivariance requires
\[
K^{A}{}_{B}(gx,gy)
=
\rho(g)^{A}{}_{A'}
K^{A'}{}_{B'}(x,y)
\rho(g^{-1})^{B'}{}_{B}.
\]
Then the induced recursion commutes with the group action.

### 10.4 Stability and robustness

Let \(F_0^\varepsilon=F_0+\varepsilon \eta\) be a noisy input. The recursive features satisfy
\[
F_n^\varepsilon
=
F_n
+
\varepsilon \eta_n
+
O(\varepsilon^2).
\]
Linearizing,
\[
\eta_{n+1}
=
\mathcal{L}_{F_n}\eta_n,
\]
where
\[
\mathcal{L}_{F_n}=D\mathcal{R}_{F_n}.
\]
If the spectral radius satisfies
\[
\rho(\mathcal{L}_{F_*})<1
\]
near an attractor \(F_*\), then noise decays:
\[
\|\eta_n\|\le C\theta^n\|\eta_0\|,
\qquad
\theta<1.
\]
Thus the stability operator provides a rigorous notion of robust invariant feature extraction.

### 10.5 Invariant spectra for visual dynamics

Given a sequence of feature vectors \(I_n\), one may estimate the invariant spectrum using dynamic mode decomposition or Koopman approximation. The resulting eigenvalues encode:

- stable visual modes,
- oscillatory texture modes,
- scale cascades,
- deformation modes,
- symmetry-breaking instabilities.

Thus RIT-II supplies a mathematical foundation for recursive, stable, equivariant feature learning.

## 12. Structural Theorems and General Principles

We now collect several general principles that organize RIT-II.

### Principle 1: Equivariance implies invariance propagation

If \(\mathcal{R}\) is \(G\)-equivariant, then every orbit starting in \(\mathcal{A}^G\) remains in \(\mathcal{A}^G\).

### Principle 2: Recursive invariants form modules

If \(\mathcal{R}\) is linear, the orbit
\[
\operatorname{span}\{\mathcal{R}^n I_0:n\ge 0\}
\]
is a cyclic module over the polynomial ring \(\mathbb{K}[t]\), with \(t\) acting as \(\mathcal{R}\).

### Principle 3: Spectra refine invariants

The invariant spectrum records not only the presence of an invariant component, but its recursive growth rate and phase.

### Principle 4: Stability is spectral

For differentiable recursions, local stability of fixed recursive invariants is governed by the spectrum of the stability operator \(D\mathcal{R}\).

### Principle 5: Natural operations generate recursive invariants

In geometry and topology, any natural operation commuting with pullback or diffeomorphism action generates recursive invariants.

### Principle 6: Generative invariant theory is dynamical invariant theory

RIT-II may be viewed as the study of dynamical systems internal to invariant subalgebras.

## 13. Examples in Compact Form

### Example 13.1: Linear algebra

Let \(A\in\operatorname{End}(V)\) commute with a representation of \(G\). If \(v_0\in V^G\), then
\[
v_{n+1}=Av_n
\]
is a recursive invariant. The invariant spectrum is the spectrum of \(A\) restricted to the invariant subspace generated by \(v_0\).

### Example 13.2: Polynomial differential operators

Let
\[
\mathcal{R}=x\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+y\frac{\partial}{\partial y}
\]
act on homogeneous polynomials. If \(I_0\) is invariant and homogeneous of degree \(d\), then
\[
I_n=d^n I_0.
\]
The invariant spectrum is \(\{d\}\).

### Example 13.3: Laplace-Beltrami recursion

Let \(f_0\) be an isometry-invariant function on \((M,g)\). Define
\[
f_{n+1}=\Delta_g f_n.
\]
Then each \(f_n\) is isometry-invariant. If \(f_0\) is an eigenfunction,
\[
\Delta_g f_0=\lambda f_0,
\]
then
\[
f_n=\lambda^n f_0.
\]

### Example 13.4: Chern character recursion

Let \(E\in K^0(X)\). Define
\[
I_{n+1}=\Psi^k(I_n).
\]
Then
\[
\operatorname{ch}_m(I_n)=k^{mn}\operatorname{ch}_m(E).
\]
The invariant spectrum is \(\{k^m\}\).

### Example 13.5: Equivariant neural recursion

Let \(F_n\) be a feature field and let \(\mathcal{R}\) be an equivariant convolutional operator. Then
\[
F_{n+1}=\mathcal{R}(F_n)
\]
produces recursive features. If \(D\) is invariant pooling, then
\[
I_n=D(F_n)
\]
is a recursive invariant descriptor.

## 14. Open Problems and Research Directions

RIT-II suggests several foundational problems.

### Problem 14.1: Classification of invariant generators

Classify equivariant operators
\[
\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{A}^G\to\mathcal{A}^G
\]
for important invariant rings, including polynomial invariants of reductive groups, differential invariants of Lie group actions, and topological invariants of manifolds.

### Problem 14.2: Noetherianity of recursive invariant modules

If \(\mathcal{A}^G\) is Noetherian, under what conditions is the recursive module generated by \(\{I_n\}\) finitely generated?

### Problem 14.3: Spectral completeness

When does the invariant spectrum of \(\mathcal{R}\) determine the recursive invariant orbit up to conjugacy?

### Problem 14.4: Geometric recursive invariants

Develop a systematic theory of recursive curvature invariants under Ricci flow, mean curvature flow, Yamabe flow, and higher-order geometric flows.

### Problem 14.5: Topological recursion

Classify natural cohomology operations that generate nontrivial recursive characteristic classes.

### Problem 14.6: Learned invariant generators

In machine learning, construct trainable operators \(\mathcal{R}\) that are equivariant by construction and whose stability spectra are controlled to ensure robustness.

## 15. Conclusion

Recursive Invariant Theory II proposes a structural extension of classical invariant theory. The invariant is no longer merely a fixed element of a group-invariant algebra; it becomes a generator of a dynamical orbit inside the invariant algebra. The primitive objects are recursive invariants, invariant generators, stability operators, and invariant spectra.

The formalism unifies several domains:

- in representation theory, recursive operators arise from central elements, Casimir operators, and transvectants;
- in geometry, they arise from natural differential operators, curvature recursions, and geometric flows;
- in topology, they arise from stable cohomology operations and Adams operations;
- in computer vision, they arise from equivariant feature transforms and stable recursive descriptors.

The central thesis is that invariance is not only preservation but generation. The appropriate object of study is therefore not merely
\[
\mathcal{A}^G,
\]
but the pair
\[
(\mathcal{A}^G,\mathcal{R}),
\]
together with its spectral and stability data.

RIT-II thus reframes invariant theory as a dynamical discipline: the study of invariant systems that evolve, generate, stabilize, and reveal their structure through recursion.
