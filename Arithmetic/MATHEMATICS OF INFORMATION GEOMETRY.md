MATHEMATICS OF INFORMATION GEOMETRY

A Foundational Theory of Information, Geometric Structure, Compression, and Emergent Dynamics

Abstract

Information theory traditionally quantifies uncertainty, distinguishability, coding cost, and statistical dependence. Its central quantities include entropy, mutual information, relative entropy, channel capacity, and algorithmic complexity. Information geometry subsequently places families of probability distributions on geometric spaces, introducing metrics, connections, curvature, geodesics, and dual affine structures.

A broader mathematical question remains:

[
\boxed{
\text{Can information itself serve as the substrate from which geometry emerges?}
}
]

Rather than beginning with a pre-existing space and assigning information to it, consider the reverse construction:

[
\boxed{
\mathcal I
\longrightarrow
\mathcal G_{\mathcal I}
\longrightarrow
\mathcal T_{\mathcal I}
\longrightarrow
\mathcal D_{\mathcal I}
\longrightarrow
\mathcal O_{\mathcal I}.
}
]

Here (\mathcal I) denotes informational structure, (\mathcal G_{\mathcal I}) an information-induced geometry, (\mathcal T_{\mathcal I}) its topology, (\mathcal D_{\mathcal I}) its induced dynamics, and (\mathcal O_{\mathcal I}) its observable structure.

This paper develops a foundational framework for such a program. Information is generalized from probability distributions to distinguishability relations, conditional dependencies, coding structures, compression maps, inference channels, causal information flows, and relational state spaces. A general information geometry is constructed from an information divergence or distinguishability functional. Under regularity assumptions, local distinguishability generates a metric tensor; higher-order informational asymmetry generates connections; variation of local informational geometry generates curvature; coarse-graining induces geometric renormalization; and informational equivalence classes generate quotient spaces and emergent topology.

A central distinction is established between information assigned to geometry and geometry generated by information. Standard information geometry generally begins with a statistical manifold and derives geometry from a divergence. The present framework asks the inverse and more general question: given informational relations without an assumed physical geometry, under what conditions does a metric space, manifold, topology, dimensionality, or dynamical structure emerge?

Several conjectural structures are introduced: the information substrate (\mathfrak I), the informational distinguishability functional (\mathcal D), the information metric (g^{(\mathcal I)}), informational curvature (R^{(\mathcal I)}), compression geometry, information dimension, informational topology, and information-induced dynamics. The paper also develops an epistemic hierarchy distinguishing what is mathematically established from what is merely permitted, selected, or still open.

The central thesis is:

[
\boxed{
\text{Geometry may be understood as a stable invariant of informational distinguishability and compression.}
}
]

The strongest form of the program asks whether familiar geometric structure—including dimensionality, topology, metric, curvature, and perhaps causal order—can arise as necessary consequences of informational organization rather than being imposed as primitive structure.
